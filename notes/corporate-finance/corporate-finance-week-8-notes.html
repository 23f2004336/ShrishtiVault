How to read these notes

Week 7 gave you the toolbox of portfolio theory: mean-variance utility, covariance, diversification, the feasible set and the Markowitz problem. Week 8 puts the toolbox to work. It asks three practical questions:

  1. If I can mix a safe asset with a risky portfolio, how much should I put in each? (Parts 1 and 2)
  2. If there are two (or many) risky assets, which risky mix is best, and how does that depend on me? (Parts 3, 4 and 5)
  3. Does piling on more independent risky bets, as an insurance company does, really cut risk? (Part 6)
The whole week in one sentence

First pick the risky mix with the highest Sharpe ratio (the same for everybody). Then use your own risk aversion A to decide how much of that mix to hold and how much to keep in the safe asset. Pooling many uncorrelated bets improves the Sharpe ratio, but only sharing the pool lowers the risk.

Quick refresher of the Week 7 results used here
  • Portfolio return rc = w₁r₁ + w₂r₂. Mean = w₁E(r₁) + w₂E(r₂).
  • Variance = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁₂, with σ₁₂ = ρσ₁σ₂.
  • Mean-variance utility U = E(r) − ½Aσ². Indifference curves slope up and are convex.
  • The Sharpe ratio is (E(rp) − rf)/σp.

Part 1: Mixing a safe asset with a risky portfolio (the CAL)

1.1 Two assets, recap

Take two assets with random returns r₁ and r₂, means E(r₁), E(r₂), standard deviations σ₁, σ₂ and covariance σ₁₂. A portfolio C with weights w₁, w₂ (w₁ + w₂ = 1) has

E(rc) = w₁E(r₁) + w₂E(r₂)     σc² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁₂

1.2 The special case: one asset is risk-free

Let asset 1 be the safe asset: E(r₁) = rf, σ₁ = 0, so σ₁₂ = 0. Let asset 2 be a risky asset or portfolio P with return rp. Put weight w on P and 1 − w on the safe asset.

Derivation of the Capital Allocation Line
Variance. σc² = (1 − w)²·0 + w²σp² + 2(1 − w)w·0 = w²σp². A constant has no variance and no covariance with anything.
Standard deviation. σc = wσp (for w ≥ 0). So risk is proportional to the weight on P, and w = σc/σp.
Mean. E(rc) = wE(rp) + (1 − w)rf = rf + w[E(rp) − rf].
The bracket E(rp) − rf is the risk premium of P: the reward for taking risk.
Replace w by σc/σp: E(rc) = rf + [(E(rp) − rf)/σp]·σc = rf + Sσc.
Sharpe ratio (reward-to-volatility) and the CAL
S = (E(rp) − rf) / σp
CAL:   E(rc) = rf + S·σc

The relationship between the mean and the standard deviation of the mix is a straight line (the CAL). Its starting point is (0, rf) and its slope is the Sharpe ratio: the extra return you earn for each extra 1% of risk.

Example 1 (slides): rf = 7%, E(rp) = 15%, σp = 22%
S = (15% − 7%)/22% = 8/22 ≈ 0.36     CAL: E(rc) = 0.07 + (8/22)σc

Every extra 1 point of σ is paid with about 0.36 points of expected return.

Example 1B (slides): a leveraged investor
Budget 300,000. She borrows 120,000 and puts all 420,000 in P.
Weight on P = 420,000/300,000 = 1.4. Weight on the safe asset = 1 − 1.4 = −0.4 (a short position in the safe asset, i.e. borrowing).
E(rc) = 0.07 + 1.4 × 0.08 = 18.2%.
σc = 1.4 × 22% = 30.8%.
Sharpe = (18.2% − 7%)/30.8% = 11.2/30.8 ≈ 0.36. The Sharpe ratio does not change along the CAL; only the amount of risk does.

1.4 When borrowing costs more than lending (kinked CAL)

A lender may worry about default and charge a borrowing rate rB above rf. Suppose rB = 9% in Example 1.

Derivation of the borrowing segment
For w > 1 you hold P and a negative position in the borrowing asset that costs rB: E(rc) = wE(rp) + (1 − w)rB = rB + w[E(rp) − rB].
Risk is still σc = wσp, so E(rc) = rB + [(E(rp) − rB)/σp]σc.
New slope = (15% − 9%)/22% = 6/22 ≈ 0.27, flatter than 0.36 because each borrowed rupee costs more.
Check at P (w = 1): lending line gives 7% + 0.364 × 22% = 15%. Borrowing line gives 9% + 0.273 × 22% = 15%. The two lines meet at P, so the CAL is kinked at P.
Extra: the leveraged investor of Example 1B (w = 1.4) now gets 9% + 1.4 × 6% = 17.4% instead of 18.2% for the same risk 30.8%.

1.5 Which risky asset to pair with the safe asset?

Suppose you may combine the safe asset with either risky asset P or Q, but not both. Let

(E(rP) − rf)/σP  >  (E(rQ) − rf)/σQ,   i.e. SP > SQ

Then the CAL through P starts at the same point (0, rf) but is steeper, so at every level of risk it offers a higher expected return. Any mean-variance investor prefers P. The rule is: pick the asset with the higher Sharpe ratio, not the one with the higher return.

Extra: a numeric check

rf = 7%. P: mean 15%, σ = 22%, S = 0.364. Q: mean 12%, σ = 10%, S = (12 − 7)/10 = 0.50. Q has the lower mean but the steeper CAL. To get 15%, hold w = (15 − 7)/(12 − 7) = 1.6 of Q: σ = 16%, which is less than P’s 22%.

Common mistakes
  • Choosing the asset with the highest return. Compare Sharpe ratios.
  • Forgetting that σc = wσp (risk scales with w, not w²; it is the variance that scales with w²).
  • Thinking the Sharpe ratio changes as you move along the CAL. It does not.

Part 2: The optimal choice of w

The CAL lists everything you can get. You still need to pick one point. The choice trades risk against return, so it depends on risk aversion: a more risk-averse person holds less of the risky asset and more of the safe one, even though both face the same CAL.

2.1 Deriving w*

Maximise U = E(r) − ½Aσ² over w
Along the CAL: E(rC) = rf + w[E(rP) − rf] and σC² = w²σP².
U(w) = rf + w[E(rP) − rf] − ½A w²σP². It is a downward-opening parabola in w, so it has a single peak.
First-order condition: dU/dw = [E(rP) − rf] − A wσP² = 0.
w* = (E(rP) − rf) / (A σP²).
Second-order check: d²U/dw² = −AσP² < 0 for A > 0, so this is a maximum.
Extra: plugging w* back, U* = rf + (E(rP) − rf)²/(2AσP²) = rf + S²/(2A). A higher Sharpe ratio always means higher attainable utility.
Reading the formula
  • w* rises with the risk premium E(rP) − rf (more reward, more risk taken).
  • w* falls with risk aversion A.
  • w* falls with the variance σP² (a wilder asset gets a smaller weight).
  • w* > 1 (borrowing) exactly when the premium exceeds AσP².
Example 1C (slides): A = 4
w* = (0.15 − 0.07)/(4 × 0.22²) = 0.08/(4 × 0.0484) = 0.08/0.1936 = 0.4132. The slide rounds this to 0.41.
Safe share = 1 − 0.4132 = 58.68%.
E(r) = 0.07 + 0.4132 × 0.08 = 10.31% (the slide, using w = 0.41, gets 10.28%).
σ = 0.4132 × 22% = 9.09% (the slide gets 9.02%).
Sharpe = (10.31% − 7%)/9.09% = 0.3636, the same as P’s Sharpe ratio. Nothing changes along the CAL.
Extra: utility U* = 0.07 + 0.3636²/(2 × 4) = {Ustar(4)*100:.2f}%.

Picture: the optimum is where the CAL touches the highest indifference curve. The slope of the indifference curve (Aσ, from Week 7) equals the CAL slope S, so σ* = S/A = 0.3636/4 = 9.09%. ✓.

Part 3: Portfolios of two risky assets

Now there is no safe asset. We have a debt fund D and an equity fund E. These numbers are used in the rest of the week (Examples 2 and 3).

Example 2 (slides): the debt and equity funds

Let wD be the debt weight and wE = 1 − wD the equity weight.

E(rP) = wDE(rD) + wEE(rE)
σP² = wD²σD² + wE²σE² + 2wDwEσDσEρDE

3.1 How correlation shapes risk

The three cases
ρ = +1. σP² = wD²σD² + wE²σE² + 2wDwEσDσE = (wDσD + wEσE)². So σP is just the weighted average of the two σ’s. This is the highest risk the mix can have. No diversification benefit.
ρ < 1. The cross term is smaller, so σP is strictly less than the weighted average. Less-than-perfectly-correlated assets always give some diversification benefit.
ρ = −1 (the lowest possible). σP² = (wDσD − wEσE)², so σP = |wDσD − wEσE|. It is zero when wDσD = (1 − wD)σE, i.e. wD = σE/(σD + σE), wE = σD/(σD + σE).

For our funds the zero-risk mix at ρ = −1 would be wD = 0.20/0.32 = 0.625, wE = 0.375, with mean 0.625(8%) + 0.375(13%) = 9.875% (Extra). Our actual ρ is +0.30, so no such mix exists.

3.2 The minimum-variance portfolio (MVP)

The slide states that wD = 0.82 minimises the variance. Here is the derivation (same as Practice Problem 2 in Week 7).

Derivation
σP²(wD) = wD²σD² + (1 − wD)²σE² + 2wD(1 − wD)σDE.
dσP²/dwD = 2wDσD² − 2(1 − wD)σE² + 2(1 − 2wD)σDE = 0.
Divide by 2 and collect: wD(σD² + σE² − 2σDE) = σE² − σDE.
wDMVP = (σE² − σDE)/(σD² + σE² − 2σDE).
Numbers: numerator 0.04 − 0.0072 = 0.0328. Denominator 0.0144 + 0.04 − 0.0144 = 0.04. So wD = 0.0328/0.04 = 0.82, wE = 0.18.
Mean = 0.82(8%) + 0.18(13%) = 6.56% + 2.34% = 8.90%.
Variance = 0.82²(0.0144) + 0.18²(0.04) + 2(0.82)(0.18)(0.0072) = 0.009683 + 0.001296 + 0.002125 = 0.013104, so σ = 11.45%.

This is below the debt fund’s own 12%: mixing in a bit of equity lowers risk below that of the safest asset. (The slide gives 11.45%.)

3.3 Choosing along the opportunity set

Which point is best depends on risk aversion. Points to the north-east have higher mean and higher risk; a more risk-averse investor prefers points to the south-west. To find the exact best point, maximise U = E(r) − ½Aσ² over wD.

Derivation of the optimal debt weight
U(wD) = E(rE) + wD[E(rD) − E(rE)] − ½A[wD²σD² + (1 − wD)²σE² + 2wD(1 − wD)σDE].
dU/dwD = [E(rD) − E(rE)] − A[wDσD² − (1 − wD)σE² + (1 − 2wD)σDE] = 0.
The bracket equals wD(σD² + σE² − 2σDE) − (σE² − σDE).
Solve: wD* = [E(rD) − E(rE) + A(σE² − σDE)] / [A(σD² + σE² − 2σDE)].
Split it: wD* = wDMVP + [E(rD) − E(rE)]/[A(σD² + σE² − 2σDE)]. The second term is negative here (debt earns less), so the investor holds less debt than the MVP in exchange for more return, and it shrinks as A grows.
Extra: numbers for Example 2

For A = 4: numerator = −0.05 + 4(0.0328) = 0.0812, denominator = 4(0.04) = 0.16, so wD* = 0.5075. As A → ∞ the answer approaches the MVP weight 0.82 (a hugely risk-averse person just minimises risk).

Part 4: Two risky assets plus a safe asset: the tangency portfolio

4.1 A top-down process

  1. Capital allocation: how much to put in the risky portfolio vs the safe asset. It sets your exposure to risk.
  2. Asset allocation: which risky mix to hold (here, how to split between D and E).

Work from the top down: first identify the best risky portfolio, then decide how much of it to hold.

Example 3 (slides): same funds, plus a safe asset at 5%

The goal is the steepest possible CAL: the highest Sharpe ratio. Compare three candidate risky portfolios.

Portfolio B beats A (the MVP) even though A has the least risk: its extra return more than pays for the extra risk. The best risky mix is the one with the maximum Sharpe ratio.

4.2 Finding the tangency portfolio

Maximise S(wD) = [E(rP) − rf]/σP over wD. Write the excess returns RD = E(rD) − rf and RE = E(rE) − rf.

Derivation of the tangency weight
Let N(w) = wRD + (1 − w)RE (the numerator) and V(w) = σP² (the variance), with w = wD. Then S = N/√V.
dS/dw = (N′V − ½NV′)/V3/2. Setting it to zero gives N′V = N·(V′/2).
N′ = RD − RE. Write c = σD² + σE² − 2σDE and d = σDE − σE². Then V = σE² + 2dw + cw² and V′/2 = d + cw.
Condition: (RD − RE)(σE² + 2dw + cw²) = [RE + (RD − RE)w](d + cw).
The w² terms cancel (both are (RD − RE)c). What is left is linear in w: (RD − RE)σE² − REd = [REc − (RD − RE)d]w.
Substitute c and d and simplify: wD* = [RDσE² − REσDE] / [RDσE² + REσD² − (RD + RE)σDE].

(The slide writes this with the excess returns RD, RE as capital letters.)

Numbers (rf = 5%)
RD = 8% − 5% = 0.03, RE = 13% − 5% = 0.08. σD² = 0.0144, σE² = 0.04, σDE = 0.0072.
Numerator: 0.03(0.04) − 0.08(0.0072) = 0.0012 − 0.000576 = 0.000624.
Denominator: 0.03(0.04) + 0.08(0.0144) − 0.11(0.0072) = 0.0012 + 0.001152 − 0.000792 = 0.00156.
wD* = 0.000624/0.00156 = 0.40, so wE = 0.60. A numerical search over all weights from −50% to 150% confirms the maximum at 0.40.
E(rP) = 0.4(8%) + 0.6(13%) = 3.2% + 7.8% = 11%.
σP² = 0.16(0.0144) + 0.36(0.04) + 2(0.4)(0.6)(0.0072) = 0.002304 + 0.0144 + 0.003456 = 0.02016, σP = 14.2%.
Sharpe = (11% − 5%)/14.2% = 0.4226 ≈ 0.42.

Part 5: The Markowitz model and the separation property

Everything so far with two risky assets generalises to n risky assets and one safe asset. The slides split the problem into three parts.

5.1 Part 1: the risk-return set

The manager needs estimates of:

  • n expected returns E(rᵢ),
  • n variances σᵢ²,
  • n(n − 1)/2 different covariances (the covariance matrix has n² entries, symmetric, and the n diagonal ones are the variances).

E(rp) = Σᵢ wᵢE(rᵢ)     σp² = Σᵢ Σⱼ wᵢwⱼCov(rᵢ, rⱼ)

The efficient frontier of risky assets is found as in Week 7: for each risk level take the portfolio with the highest mean, or equivalently, for each target mean take the portfolio with the lowest variance (the Lagrangian problem of Week 7, Part 6). Extra constraints can be added here: no short selling, a minimum dividend yield, socially responsible investing, and so on.

5.2 Part 2: the optimal risky portfolio

Now add the safe asset. Search for the CAL with the highest Sharpe ratio. It touches the efficient frontier at the tangency portfolio P. P is the optimal risky portfolio for the manager’s clients. (For n assets, the weights solve the Sharpe-maximisation problem; with a covariance matrix Σ they are proportional to Σ⁻¹ times the vector of excess returns. This general formula is Extra; the two-asset version is in Part 4.)

5.3 Part 3: the complete portfolio, and why it separates

The same P is offered to every client. The client’s risk aversion enters only through the capital allocation, i.e. where they sit on the CAL. A more risk-averse client holds more of the safe asset and less of P, but everyone uses P as the risky vehicle.

Separation property (Tobin) and mutual funds

Investors with very different risk aversion are happy with a universe of just two funds: a money-market fund (the safe asset) and a fund that holds the optimal risky portfolio P. This is the theoretical basis of the mutual fund and index fund industry: one fund P, and each investor just changes how much cash to hold.

Why real investors differ (summary)
  • Different beliefs about returns and covariances.
  • Short-sale limits (wᵢ ≥ 0).
  • Social-responsibility screens.
  • Taxes and transaction costs.

Each of these changes the frontier or the inputs, so the tangency portfolio is no longer identical for everyone.

Part 6: Risk pooling versus risk sharing

6.1 The puzzle

Risk pooling means merging uncorrelated risky projects. For an insurer it means selling many uncorrelated policies, known as the insurance principle. Conventional wisdom says pooling reduces risk. Is that right?

Think of a gambler who says “a few more bets will diversify my wagers.” He now has more money at stake, so the swing in his total wealth is wider. His average result per bet becomes more predictable, but his total result becomes less predictable.

Extra: the coin example (summary)

A toss pays +$100 with probability 0.4 and −$100 with probability 0.6. Mean = 0.4(100) − 0.6(100) = −$20. E(x²) = 10,000, so variance = 10,000 − 20² = 9,600 and σ = $97.98.

For N independent tosses the mean is −20N, the standard deviation is 97.98√N, and the standard deviation per toss is 97.98/√N.

6.2 Example 4A: risk pooling

Siraj has 1 unit of wealth. Portfolio P: weight w in risky asset A and 1 − w in the safe asset. A has risk premium R and standard deviation σ.

RP = wR    σP = wσ    SP = wR/(wσ) = R/σ

He finds asset B with the same R and σ, uncorrelated with A (covariance 0), and adds a position of the same size w by moving money out of the safe asset. This is portfolio Z: w in A, w in B, 1 − 2w safe.

Derivation for Z
Risk premium: RZ = wR + wR = 2wR.
Variance: σZ² = w²σ² + w²σ² + 2w²·0 = 2w²σ².
Standard deviation: σZ = √2·wσ, which is larger than wσ.
Sharpe ratio: SZ = 2wR/(√2 wσ) = √2·R/σ, better than R/σ.
For N assets: RZ = NwR, σZ = √N·wσ, SZ = √N·R/σ.

Conclusion. Pooling raises the Sharpe ratio, but it does so by enlarging the risky budget from w to 2w (or Nw). Risk itself goes up, by a factor √N. The insurance principle says only that risk rises less than proportionally (√N versus N), so profitability per unit of risk improves. It does not reduce risk.

6.3 Example 4B: risk sharing

If Siraj finds √2·wσ too much risk, he can share risk: sell part of his pool to other investors, so the total money in risky assets stays fixed at w. With two assets he holds w/2 in each and keeps 1 − w in the safe asset. Call this V.

Derivation for V
Risk premium: RV = (w/2)R + (w/2)R = wR (unchanged).
Variance: σV² = (w/2)²σ² + (w/2)²σ² = w²σ²/2.
Standard deviation: σV = wσ/√2, lower than wσ.
Sharpe ratio: SV = wR/(wσ/√2) = √2·R/σ, the same as Z.
For N assets with w/N each: RV = wR, σV = wσ/√N → 0, SV = √N·R/σ.

V has the same Sharpe ratio as Z but much less risk. Risk sharing combined with risk pooling is the key to the insurance industry. Insurers often reinsure by selling parts of their policies to other insurers, which is exactly sharing.

Real diversification

True diversification means spreading a portfolio of fixed size across many assets, not adding more risky bets to an ever-growing risky portfolio. V is the same equal-weight diversification you saw in Week 7 (variance σ²/N).

Common mistakes
  • Saying “pooling reduces risk”. It improves the Sharpe ratio; the risk of the bigger position goes up.
  • Forgetting that V keeps the total risky weight fixed at w.
  • Mixing up the √N: pooling multiplies σ by √N, sharing divides it by √N.

Part 7: Practice problems

7.1 Question 1 (slides): bonds, corporate debt and equities

Setup

Government bonds: rf = 2%. Corporate debt D: mean 6%, σ = 5%. Equities E: mean 12%, σ = 20%. Correlation ρDE = 0.25, so σDE = 0.25 × 0.05 × 0.20 = 0.0025.

1A. Smallest possible variance using only debt and equity
Let wE be the equity weight. σP² = (1 − wE)²σD² + wE²σE² + 2(1 − wE)wEσDE.
Collect powers of wE: σP² = wE²(σE² + σD² − 2σDE) + wE(−2σD² + 2σDE) + σD².
Numbers: first bracket 0.04 + 0.0025 − 0.005 = 0.0375. Second bracket −0.005 + 0.005 = 0. So σP² = 0.0375wE² + 0.0025.
The smallest value is at wE = 0 (100% debt): minimum variance 0.0025 (σ = 5%).
Why? The linear term is zero because σDE = σD². In general you can lower risk by adding a little of E only when σDE < σD², i.e. ρ < σD/σE = 0.25. Here ρ is exactly 0.25.
1B. Equal weights (wE = 0.5)
E(r) = 0.5(6%) + 0.5(12%) = 9%.
σ² = 0.0375(0.25) + 0.0025 = 0.009375 + 0.0025 = 0.011875, σ ≈ 10.9%.
Sharpe = (0.09 − 0.02)/√0.011875 = 0.07/0.10897 ≈ 0.6424.
1C. The optimal risky portfolio
S(wE) = [0.06 + 0.06wE − 0.02]/√(0.0375wE² + 0.0025) = (0.04 + 0.06wE)/√(0.0375wE² + 0.0025).
Set dS/dwE = 0, i.e. N′V = N·V′/2: 0.06(0.0375wE² + 0.0025) = (0.04 + 0.06wE)(0.0375wE).
Left: 0.00225wE² + 0.00015. Right: 0.0015wE + 0.00225wE². The squares cancel: 0.00015 = 0.0015wE.
wE = 0.10, wD = 0.90. Cross-check with the Part 4 formula: RD = 0.04, RE = 0.10: wD = (0.04 × 0.04 − 0.10 × 0.0025)/(0.04 × 0.04 + 0.10 × 0.0025 − 0.14 × 0.0025) = 0.00135/0.0015 = 0.9 ✓.
E(r) = 0.9(6%) + 0.1(12%) = 6.6%.
σ² = 0.9²(0.0025) + 0.1²(0.04) + 2(0.9)(0.1)(0.0025) = 0.002025 + 0.0004 + 0.00045 = 0.002875, σ ≈ 5.36%.
Sharpe = (0.066 − 0.02)/0.05362 = 0.8579.
1D. A 10% return using only debt and equity
wE(0.12) + (1 − wE)(0.06) = 0.10 ⇒ 0.06wE = 0.04 ⇒ wE = 2/3, wD = 1/3. (The slide’s right-hand side is garbled but this is the intended equation.)
σ² = (1/3)²(0.0025) + (2/3)²(0.04) + 2(1/3)(2/3)(0.0025) = 0.000278 + 0.017778 + 0.001111 ≈ 0.0192.
σ ≈ 13.84%.
1E. A 10% return using bonds, debt and equity optimally
Use the optimal risky portfolio P* (mean 6.6%) and the bond. Put y in P*: 0.02 + y(0.066 − 0.02) = 0.10 ⇒ y = 0.08/0.046 = 40/23 ≈ 1.739.
Slide method: P* has wD/wE = 9, and the weights satisfy wD(0.06 − 0.02) + wE(0.12 − 0.02) = 0.10 − 0.02. So 9wE(0.04) + 0.10wE = 0.08 ⇒ 0.46wE = 0.08 ⇒ wE = 4/23.
wE = 4/23 ≈ 17.39%, wD = 36/23 ≈ 156.52%, wbonds = −17/23 ≈ −73.91% (borrowing).
σ = y × 5.362% = (40/23)(5.362%) ≈ 9.32%, much less than the 13.84% in 1D. Reason: the highest Sharpe ratio (0.858) beats the Sharpe ratio of any debt/equity mix.
1F. Preferences U = E(r) − 3σ²
Compare with U = E − ½Aσ²: ½A = 3, so A = 6.
CAL: E = 0.02 + 0.8579σ. Indifference curve: E = U + 3σ², slope 6σ.
Tangency: 6σ = 0.8579 ⇒ σ* = 0.143 (14.3%).
E* = 0.02 + 0.8579(0.143) ≈ 14.27%.
Weight in P*: y* = σ*/σP* = 0.143/0.0536 ≈ 2.667. The formula y* = (E(rP*) − rf)/(AσP*²) = 0.046/(6 × 0.002875) = 8/3 = 2.6667 confirms it. (The slide’s 2.6674 comes from rounding E to 14.27%.)
Holdings: debt 2.6667 × 0.9 = 2.40, equity 2.6667 × 0.1 = 0.267, bonds 1 − 2.6667 = −1.667. This investor borrows 167% of her wealth.

7.2 Question 2 (slides): Gandalf and Boromir

Setup

Gondor G: mean 8%, σ = 28%. Mordor M: mean 6%, σ = 28%. Covariance 0. Risk-free rate 0%.

2A. Premiums and Sharpe ratios. Premium: G 8%, M 6%. SG = 8/28 = 2/7 ≈ 0.286. SM = 6/28 = 3/14 ≈ 0.214.

2B. The portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio (weights 4/7, 3/7)
σP = √[(4/7)²(28%)² + (3/7)²(28%)²] = 28% × √(25/49) = 28% × 5/7 = 20%.
Risk premium = (4/7)(8%) + (3/7)(6%) = (32 + 18)/7 % = 50/7 % ≈ 7.14%.
Sharpe = (50/7 %)/20% = 50/140 = 5/14 ≈ 0.357.
Extra check on the 4:3 weights: with equal variances and zero covariance, the tangency weights are proportional to the excess returns, 8 : 6 = 4 : 3 ✓.
2C. Gandalf’s holdings (A = 25/14)
wP = (E(rP) − rf)/(AσP²) = (50/7 %)/[(25/14)(0.20)²] = 0.071429/0.071429 = 1.
He puts 0% in the safe asset, 4/7 ≈ 57.1% in Gondor and 3/7 ≈ 42.9% in Mordor.
2D. Boromir’s inefficiency
Boromir holds only Gondor: mean 8%, σ = 28%. He should hold G and M in the ratio 4 : 3.
Find the point on the CAL with the same σ = 28%: w²σP² = 0.28² ⇒ w = 28/20 = 1.4.
Mean there = 1.4 × 50/7 % = 10%, which beats his 8%.
Holdings: 1.4 × 4/7 = 0.8 in Gondor, 1.4 × 3/7 = 0.6 in Mordor, −0.4 in the safe asset.

7.3 Fourteen more practice problems (Extra)

Click a question to see its answer.

1. CAL. rf = 4%, P has mean 12% and σ = 16%. (a) Sharpe ratio and CAL? (b) What w gives σ = 24% and what is E?
(a) S = (12 − 4)/16 = 0.5. CAL: E = 0.04 + 0.5σ. (b) w = 24/16 = 1.5 (borrow 50% of wealth). E = 0.04 + 0.5(0.24) = 16%; check 0.04 + 1.5(0.08) = 16% ✓.
2. Kinked CAL. Same as above but you borrow at 6%. Find the borrowing slope and E at w = 1.5.
Slope = (12 − 6)/16 = 0.375. E = 0.06 + 1.5(0.12 − 0.06) = 15% (instead of 16%). At w = 1 both lines give 12%, so the kink is at P.
3. Which asset?. rf = 2%. P: mean 10%, σ = 10%. Q: mean 14%, σ = 20%. Which should you pair with the safe asset?
SP = 8/10 = 0.80, SQ = 12/20 = 0.60. Choose P, even though Q has the higher mean. P’s CAL is steeper.
4. Optimal w. rf = 3%, risky asset mean 11%, σ = 20%, A = 5. Find w*, E, σ and U.
w* = 0.08/(5 × 0.04) = 0.4. E = 0.03 + 0.4(0.08) = 6.2%. σ = 0.4(20%) = 8%. U = 0.062 − ½(5)(0.0064) = 0.046. Check: U* = rf + S²/(2A) = 0.03 + 0.4²/10 = 0.046 ✓.
5. When does she borrow?. In Example 1 (rf = 7%, P: 15%, 22%), for which A is w* > 1?
w* > 1 ⇔ 0.08/(A × 0.0484) > 1 ⇔ A < 0.08/0.0484 = 1.65. Only very risk-tolerant investors borrow.
6. Two-asset risk. Debt/equity funds of Example 2. Find σ of a 50/50 mix when ρ = 0.3 and when ρ = 1.
ρ = 0.3: variance = 0.25(0.0144) + 0.25(0.04) + 2(0.25)(0.0072) = 0.0036 + 0.01 + 0.0036 = 0.0172, σ = 13.11%. ρ = 1: σ = 0.5(12%) + 0.5(20%) = 16%. The difference (2.9 points) is the diversification benefit.
7. Zero-risk mix. ρ = −1, σ₁ = 10%, σ₂ = 30%, means 6% and 12%. Find the zero-risk weights and the sure return.
w₁ = σ₂/(σ₁ + σ₂) = 30/40 = 0.75, w₂ = 0.25. Check: 0.75(10%) − 0.25(30%) = 0. Return = 0.75(6%) + 0.25(12%) = 7.5%.
8. MVP. σ₁ = 10%, σ₂ = 25%, ρ = 0.2. Find the minimum-variance weights and σ.
σ₁₂ = 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.25 = 0.005. w₁ = (0.0625 − 0.005)/(0.01 + 0.0625 − 0.01) = 0.0575/0.0625 = 0.92, w₂ = 0.08. Variance = 0.92²(0.01) + 0.08²(0.0625) + 2(0.92)(0.08)(0.005) = 0.008464 + 0.0004 + 0.000736 = 0.0096, σ = 9.80%, below the safer asset’s 10%.
9. Tangency formula. rf = 2%. Debt: mean 6%, σ = 10%. Equity: mean 12%, σ = 20%. ρ = 0.2. Find the tangency weights, its Sharpe ratio, and y* for A = 3.
σ₁₂ = 0.004, RD = 0.04, RE = 0.10. Numerator = 0.04(0.04) − 0.10(0.004) = 0.0012. Denominator = 0.04(0.04) + 0.10(0.01) − 0.14(0.004) = 0.00204. wD = 0.5882, wE = 0.4118. E = 8.47%, σ = 11.04%, Sharpe = 0.586. y* = (E − 2%)/(3σ²) = 1.771.
10. Count the inputs. How many estimates are needed for 50 risky assets?
50 means + 50 variances + 50 × 49/2 = 1,225 covariances = 1,325.
11. Two clients. In Example 3 (P: 11%, σ = 14.2%, rf = 5%), find the holdings of a client with A = 4 and one with A = 10.
A = 4: y* = 0.744: 29.8% debt, 44.6% equity, 25.6% safe. A = 10: y* = 0.06/(10 × 0.02016) = 0.298: 11.9% debt, 17.9% equity, 70.2% safe. Both hold debt and equity in the ratio 40:60.
12. Pooling vs sharing. w = 0.5, R = 4%, σ = 25%, N = 9 uncorrelated assets. Find premium, σ and Sharpe for P, Z and V.
P: premium 2%, σ = 12.5%, S = 0.16. Z: premium 9 × 0.5 × 4% = 18%, σ = 3 × 12.5% = 37.5%, S = 0.48. V: premium 2%, σ = 12.5%/3 = 4.17%, S = 0.48. Z needs total risky weight 4.5, i.e. borrowing of 3.5.
13. Coin game. A toss pays +50 or −40 with probability 0.5 each. For N = 100 tosses find the mean, σ of the total, and σ per toss.
Mean per toss +5, so E(total) = +500. Per-toss σ = (50 − (−40))/2 = 45, so σ(total) = 45√100 = 450, σ per toss = 450/100 = 4.5. Per-toss risk is tiny but total dollar risk is large.
14. Why not just pool?. A fund adds 15 more uncorrelated risky assets of the same size to its single risky position. Has it reduced risk?
No. N = 16: premium and σ both rise (σ by √16 = 4 times). Sharpe ratio is 4 times higher but the total exposure is larger. To cut risk it must share: hold 1/16 of the original risky budget in each asset, so σ falls to a quarter.

7.4 Short conceptual questions

1. Why do investors with different A hold the same risky portfolio?
Because the best risky mix is the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, which depends only on returns and covariances. A only decides how much of it to hold (separation).
2. Why is the MVP not the best risky portfolio when a safe asset exists?
The MVP minimises risk, ignoring return. With a safe asset you want the steepest CAL (highest Sharpe ratio), which is the tangency portfolio (Portfolio B beats the MVP A in Example 3).
3. Does the Sharpe ratio change as you move along a CAL?
No. Moving along the CAL changes w, which scales both the risk premium and σ by the same factor.
4. What happens to the CAL when borrowing costs more than lending?
It kinks at P and becomes flatter beyond it: slope (E(rP) − rB)/σP.
5. Does risk pooling reduce risk?
No. It raises the Sharpe ratio (by √N) but also raises σ (by √N). Risk falls only if the pool is shared so the total risky budget is fixed.
6. What is the difference between capital allocation and asset allocation?
Capital allocation = split between safe asset and risky portfolio (depends on A). Asset allocation = split inside the risky portfolio to get the best Sharpe ratio (same for everyone).

Exam tips
  • Always check whether the question gives σ or variance before plugging into a formula.
  • Use excess returns (E − rf) in the tangency formula.
  • A negative weight is allowed: it means borrowing (safe asset) or short selling (risky asset).
  • For “same risk, higher return” questions (Boromir), move vertically to the CAL.
  • When asked about pooling, state clearly both effects: Sharpe ratio up, risk up (pooling) or down (sharing).
