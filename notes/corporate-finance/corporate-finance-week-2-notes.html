1. Setting the Stage (Lecture 1, first half)

1.1 Time value of money (TVM)

Principle₹100 today is worth more than ₹100 next year.

Why? You can deposit today's ₹100 in a bank at a positive interest rate. A year later you hold more than ₹100. So money now can be turned into more money later, but not the other way round. Waiting has a price: the interest you give up (the opportunity cost).

1.2 Who is on each side?

Lenders / investors give money today to get more later. Borrowers (such as firms) use it. A firm must decide which projects to run with borrowed money. This is capital budgeting. The main method is the NPV rule:

Convert every future inflow and outflow into today's money
↓
Add them up and subtract the cost today → NPV
↓
NPV > 0 → accept  |  NPV < 0 → reject

1.3 Perfect market (start with the simplest world)

PillarMeaning
P1 No taxesNo income, corporate or capital-gains tax
P2 No transaction costsBuying/selling is free (no brokerage, no bid–ask spread)
P3 No information/opinion differencesEveryone has the same information and reads it the same way
P4 Many buyers and sellersNobody can move the price alone: everyone is a price-taker

Three extra simplifications (not part of "perfect market"): E1 same interest rate every period; E2 no inflation; E3 no risk, everything is known for sure. Later weeks relax each one (inflation comes back in Section 6).

1.4 Loans and bonds

  • Loan: borrower receives cash today and promises fixed amounts at fixed future dates. The last repayment date is the maturity (T).
  • Bond: a particular type of loan. Buying a bond = lending. A firm "issues" or "sells" a bond when it borrows.
  • Loans and bonds are called fixed-income securities because the payments are fixed in advance.

1.5 Why does a rate of interest exist at all? (plain English)

Three reasons a lender wants a premium for waiting: (1) impatience: people prefer to spend now; (2) opportunity: the lender could have used the money for something productive; (3) in the real world, inflation and risk (we switch these off for now through E2 and E3). In our simplified world, the interest rate r is just the price of moving money across time, like an exchange rate between "rupees today" and "rupees next year".

₹1 today
⇄ exchange rate = (1 + r) ⇄
₹(1 + r) next year
Fixed-income securities (why the name?)Loans and bonds promise fixed payments in advance. Contrast stocks, whose payments are not promised. That is why bond valuation can be done exactly with the formulas of this week.

1.6 Why start with a "perfect market"?

Think of a physics problem that ignores friction first. Without taxes, costs, secret information, and powerful traders, the maths is clean, and one price rules every asset. Later weeks add the frictions back. The most important consequence: in a perfect market the interest rate is the same for borrowing and lending, so one rate r can be used everywhere.

2. Returns (Lecture 1)

2.1 Notation

SymbolMeaning

Tfinal period
C₀cash at time 0 (the price paid / cost)
C₁cash returned at time 1

2.2 Net return and rate of return

Net return = C₁ − C₀   (profitable if > 0)
Rate of return: r₁ = (C₁ − C₀) / C₀
ExampleCosts ₹100, returns ₹120. Net return = 20. Rate of return = 20/100 = 20%.

Why divide by C₀? ₹20 profit on ₹100 is great; ₹20 on ₹10,000 is poor. The rate is "profit per rupee invested", so investments of different sizes can be compared.

2.3 Interim payments (dividends, coupons, rent)

Stocks pay dividends, bonds pay coupons, real estate pays rent. These arrive before the final sale, so they count as part of the return:

r₁ = (C₁ + D₁ − C₀) / C₀

2.4 Decomposition (proof)

r₁ = (C₁ + D₁ − C₀)/C₀
Rearrange the numerator: = [(C₁ − C₀) + D₁]/C₀
Split the fraction: = (C₁ − C₀)/C₀  +  D₁/C₀
Total return = capital-gain yield + dividend (rental/coupon) yield ∎
Worked exampleBuy at 92, receive dividend 5, sell at 110.
Total: (110 + 5 − 92)/92 = 23/92 = 25%
Capital gain yield: (110 − 92)/92 = 18/92 ≈ 19.57%
Dividend yield: 5/92 ≈ 5.43%  → 19.57 + 5.43 = 25 ✓
Key point: capital gain can be negative while the total return is still positive, if the dividend yield is bigger than the price fall. Example: buy at 100, dividend 8, sell at 95 → (95 + 8 − 100)/100 = +3%.

2.5 Points vs basis points

"10% is 5% more than 5%" is ambiguous. Is it 5 more points, or 100% more relatively? Fix it with units:

UnitValue

1 point= 100 bp
10% vs 5%: difference = 5 points = 500 bp; relative increase = (10 − 5)/5 = 100%

2.7 Units: more basis-point practice

  • Interest rate moves from 6.50% to 6.75%: the change is 25 bp (0.25 points). Relative increase = 0.25/6.50 ≈ 3.85%.
  • "Central bank cuts by 50 bp" means the rate falls by 0.50 percentage points, e.g. from 6.50% to 6.00%.
  • If someone says "up 5%", ask: 5 points or 5 percent of the old value?

3. Future Value and Compounding (Lecture 2)

3.1 One period

From r = (C₁ − C₀)/C₀:  r·C₀ = C₁ − C₀  ⟹  C₁ = C₀(1 + r)

The ₹110 you get next year from ₹100 at 10% is the future value (FV) of ₹100: its value at a later date.

3.2 Many periods: compounding

If you reinvest everything each year at the same rate r, then:

C₂ = C₁(1 + r) = C₀(1 + r)(1 + r) = C₀(1 + r)²
C₃ = C₂(1 + r) = C₀(1 + r)³  ⟹  C_t = C₀(1 + r)^t  (by induction)
t-period return: 1 + r_t = (1 + r)^t
Compound vs simple interest (₹100 at 10%)Simple: 10 a year → 120 after 2 years (20%). Compound: 110 → 110 × 1.1 = 121 (21%). The extra 1 is the 10% earned in year 2 on year 1's interest of 10. That is "interest on interest".
Year1251012
₹100 at 10%110121161.05259.37313.84

Slide example: ₹100 for 12 years at 10% = 100 × (1.1)¹² ≈ 313.84.

3.3 Finding the equivalent yearly rate

QuestionWhich constant yearly rate r gives a 2-year return of 50%?
(1 + r)² = 1 + 0.50 = 1.5  ⟹  1 + r = √1.5 = 1.22474  ⟹  r ≈ 22.47%
General: r = (1 + r_t)^(1/t) − 1
TrapIt is NOT 50%/2 = 25%. Check: 1.25² − 1 = 56.25%, too big. Returns multiply, they do not add.

3.4 Doubling time (derivation)

Want x with (1 + r)^x = 2
Take logs: x·ln(1 + r) = ln 2  ⟹  x = ln 2 / ln(1 + r)
r = 3%: x = 0.693147 / 0.029559 ≈ 23.45 years (the lecture summary rounds to 23.44)

Sanity check (rule of 72): 72/3 = 24 years, close. Higher rate → faster doubling.

3.5 Fractional periods

Compounding also works for half a year. If half-year rate is r₀.₅ then two half-years must equal one year:

(1 + r₀.₅)² = 1 + r₁  ⟹  r₀.₅ = √(1 + r₁) − 1
r₁ = 5%: √1.05 − 1 = 1.024695 − 1 ≈ 2.47%  (not 2.5%!)
Remember: to change the period length, take a root (or power) of (1 + r), never divide r. Section 9's bond example depends on this.

3.6 Why compounding makes returns multiply (slow explanation)

After year 1 your 100 has become 110. In year 2 the bank does not treat the 10 as "old interest"; it simply sees 110 of principal and pays 10% on it. So each year multiplies the amount by the same factor 1.10. Doing it t times gives 1.10 × 1.10 × … = 1.10^t. That is the only idea behind the formula.

Why not just divide?1% a month compounded 12 times gives 1.01¹² = 12.68% a year, more than 12%. So 1% a month is not the same as 12% a year. Exact conversion needs roots.

4. Present Value and Discounting (Lecture 2)

Future value asks "what will my money be?". Present value (PV) reverses it: "what is a future sum worth today?" This is what a firm needs: if Project Y returns ₹1 million in 5 years, how much should we pay for it now?

4.1 Derivation

Start with C_t = C₀(1 + r)^t and solve for C₀:
PV = C₀ = C_t / (1 + r)^t   (one period: C₁/(1 + r))
Discount factor: DF_t = 1/(1 + r)^t, so PV = C_t × DF_t
  • "Discounting" = reverse of compounding.
  • The rate r is also called the discount rate or cost of capital (what your money could earn elsewhere).
  • If r > 0 then DF < 1: the future is worth less than today.
Today (t = 0)
⇄ × (1+r) per year ⇄ ÷ (1+r) per year
Future (t)

4.2 Examples from the slides

Save for 100 next year at 10%: PV = 100/1.1 ≈ 90.91 (DF ≈ 0.91)
100 in two periods at 10%: PV = 100/1.1² = 100/1.21 ≈ 82.64 (DF ≈ 0.83)
Is a project a good deal?It costs 90.91 and pays 100 next year.
Market rate 12%: PV = 100/1.12 = 89.29 < 90.91 cost → bad deal
Market rate 9%: PV = 100/1.09 = 91.74 > 90.91 cost → good deal
Reason: at 12% you can get 100 next year by putting only 89.29 in the bank, so paying 90.91 is wasteful.

4.3 Bond prices and rates move oppositely

Bond pays 1,000 in a year. r = 5%: P = 1000/1.05 = 952.38. r = 6%: P = 1000/1.06 = 943.40
Proof: P = C₁(1 + r)⁻¹ ⟹ dP/dr = −C₁(1 + r)⁻² = −C₁/(1 + r)² < 0 for all r > −1

Intuition: the payout is fixed, so a higher required return can only be achieved by paying a lower price. Price up ⟺ implied return down.

4.4 Discounting vs compounding on a timeline

Read the picture: 82.64 today, 90.91 a year from now and 100 two years from now are all the same wealth at 10%. They are just stated at different dates. That is the whole meaning of "equivalent".

4.5 PV intuition: the "bank test"

To value a future ₹100, ask: "How much would I need to put in the bank today so that it grows to exactly ₹100?" The answer is the PV. If a seller asks more than that, you are better off using the bank. If a seller asks less, buy.

5. Net Present Value, NPV (Lecture 3)

5.1 Why PV makes adding possible (value additivity)

You cannot add ₹10 next year and ₹8 in five years to get ₹18: different dates mean different "units" of money. But after converting each to today's rupees they are in the same unit and can be added, subtracted and compared.

Example at 5%: PV(10 in 1 yr) = 10/1.05 = 9.52; PV(8 in 5 yrs) = 8/1.05⁵ = 6.27 → total 15.79, not 18.

5.2 Formula and rule

NPV = C₀ + C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + … + C_T/(1+r)^T  (C₀ negative if it is a cost)

"Net" = include the upfront cost. Three steps: (1) discount every future cash flow, (2) add them, (3) subtract the cost.

NPVMeaningDecision
> 0earns more than the market rate rAccept
= 0earns exactly rIndifferent
< 0earns less than rReject

5.3 Two ways to understand NPV

Example: pay 12 now, get 10 in year 1 and 8 in year 5, r = 5%NPV = 10/1.05 + 8/1.05⁵ − 12 = 9.524 + 6.268 − 12 = 3.79.
Interpretation 1 – value added
The market value of the cash flows is 15.79 but the project costs 12. The difference, 3.79, is the extra value created.
Interpretation 2 – versus the capital market
Put the same 12 in the bank at 5%: it becomes 12.60 next year. Take out 10, leaving 2.60. In 4 more years: 2.60 × 1.05⁴ ≈ 3.16. The project gives 8. Gap = 4.84, whose PV is 4.84/1.05⁵ = 3.79 = NPV ✓

5.7 How the answer depends on r (mining project)

Higher r shrinks every future inflow, so NPV falls. The project is a good idea at 5% (NPV +68.15) and a bad idea at 10% (NPV -41.25). The NPV hits zero at roughly r ≈ 8.0% (extra detail beyond the lecture: this break-even rate is called the IRR; your lecture only needs the NPV sign at the given r). Because the cost is up front and the benefits come later, projects like this are the most sensitive to the discount rate.

5.8 Why NPV is the right rule (common doubts)

  • Why not "total profit"? It adds rupees from different dates. NPV puts them on the same date first.
  • Why not "highest return %"? A 50% return on ₹10 is worth less in rupees than a 12% return on ₹10 lakh. NPV measures rupees of value created.
  • Why does the sign alone decide? NPV > 0 means doing the project leaves you richer today than putting the same money in the market. Nobody should refuse a free gain.
  • Why is r the market rate? It is the opportunity cost. If the market gives 5% for a similar investment, a project earning less than 5% is a poor use of money.

5.9 Why the growth-vs-shrinking result is not a paradox

Think of two apartments. One is in a booming area (high rent growth) and the other in a stagnant area. The first is much more expensive. If both were priced "fairly", an investor in each earns the same 10% return, and the booming-area buyer just gets more of it as price growth and less as rent. G's return of 10% is cash 100 (24.8% of the 402.70) plus a price change of −59.73 (i.e. 342.98 − 402.70), which is negative, and the sum is still 10%. This shows the decomposition from Section 2 at work.

G: cash yield = 100/402.70 = 24.83%; capital gain = (342.98 − 402.70)/402.70 = −14.83%; total = 10.00%
S: cash yield = 100/225.39 = 44.37%; capital gain = (147.93 − 225.39)/225.39 = −34.37%; total = 10.00%

So S pays a bigger cash yield but its price shrinks faster. Everything is balanced by the market so that all investments earn the same 10%.

6. Intertemporal Choice: Borrowing and Saving (Lecture 4)

6.1 Warm-up: the ordinary budget constraint

Income m, goods with prices p₁, p₂. A bundle (x₁, x₂) is affordable if p₁x₁ + p₂x₂ ≤ m. The budget line p₁x₁ + p₂x₂ = m has slope −p₁/p₂: to get one more unit of good 1 you give up p₁/p₂ units of good 2.

6.2 Same idea across time

Two periods. Incomes m₁, m₂; consumption c₁, c₂; interest rate r. For now prices are 1. The "goods" are consumption today and consumption tomorrow. The bank lets you shift spending between them.

Case A – Endowment point
No saving or borrowing: c₁ = m₁, c₂ = m₂.
Case B – Save everything
c₁ = 0, save s₁ = m₁. Period 2: m₂ + (1+r)m₁ (this is the future value of income).
Case C – Borrow the max
c₂ = 0. Loan b₁ must satisfy b₁(1+r) = m₂, so b₁ = m₂/(1+r). Then c₁ = m₁ + m₂/(1+r) (the present value of income).

6.3 Deriving the budget line

Spend c₁ in period 1 → saving s₁ = m₁ − c₁ (negative means borrowing)
Period 2: c₂ = m₂ + (1 + r)(m₁ − c₁)
Expand: c₂ = m₂ + (1+r)m₁ − (1+r)c₁
Move c₁ to the left: (1+r)c₁ + c₂ = (1+r)m₁ + m₂
Divide by (1+r): c₁ + c₂/(1+r) = m₁ + m₂/(1+r)
Fundamental law: PV of consumption = PV of income. You can shift consumption through time, but you cannot spend more than the present value of everything you will earn. The slope is −(1+r): one extra rupee consumed today costs (1+r) rupees tomorrow.

Numbers used: m₁ = m₂ = 100, r = 10%

Example: borrow 50 todayc₁ = 150, so s₁ = −50. Period 2: c₂ = 100 + 1.1(100 − 150) = 100 − 55 = 45. Check PV: 150 + 45/1.1 = 190.91 = 100 + 100/1.1 ✓. Points above the line are unaffordable. Points below are affordable but wasteful.

6.4 Adding prices

p₁c₁ + p₂c₂/(1+r) = m₁ + m₂/(1+r)

6.5 Inflation and the real interest rate

Let inflation π be defined by p₂ = p₁(1 + π). (π = 0.05 means prices rise 5%.) Choose units so p₁ = 1, giving p₂ = 1 + π.

Budget: c₁ + (1+π)c₂/(1+r) = m₁ + m₂/(1+r)
Solve for c₂: c₂ = −[(1+r)/(1+π)]·c₁ + [1/(1+π)]·[m₁(1+r) + m₂]
Slope = −(1+r)/(1+π) ≡ −(1+ρ), so 1 + ρ = (1+r)/(1+π)

ρ is the real interest rate: the trade-off in terms of goods, not rupees. If you save ₹1 you get (1+r) rupees back, but each rupee buys 1/(1+π) as much.

Approximation: (1+r)/(1+π) ≈ (1+r)(1−π) = 1 + r − π − rπ. If rπ is tiny: ρ ≈ r − π
rπExact ρApprox r − π

NoteThe shortcut is good when rates are small. When r and π are large, use the exact ratio.

6.6 Plain-English picture of the intertemporal budget

You have two "pots" of income: m₁ now and m₂ later. The bank lets you slide spending along the line, like a see-saw. Spend more now (borrow) and you must spend less later by (1+r) for every rupee. Spend less now (save) and you get (1+r) more later for every rupee. You can never leave the line, because the PV of what you spend equals the PV of what you earn.

Saver: c₁ < m₁ → points to the upper-left of the endowment point
Borrower: c₁ > m₁ → points to the lower-right of the endowment point

6.8 Inflation example in full

r = 10%, π = 5%, m₁ = m₂ = 100, p₁ = 1
p₂ = 1.05. Real rate: 1+ρ = 1.10/1.05 = 1.0476 ⟹ ρ = 4.76% (shortcut r − π = 5%)

So giving up 1 unit of goods today gets only 1.0476 units of goods tomorrow, not 1.10. That is the slope −(1+ρ).

6.9 Why the real rate matters

  • Savers care about purchasing power, not rupee amounts. A 6% deposit with 8% inflation has a negative real rate (≈ −1.9%): you become poorer in real terms.
  • If π > r, ρ < 0. Borrowing then is "cheap" in real terms.
  • Rule: in an exam, state the exact formula first, and use r − π only if rates are small.
Also try (prices differ): p₁ = 1, p₂ = 2 (100% inflation), r = 10%1 + ρ = 1.1/2 = 0.55 ⟹ ρ = −45%. Saving 1 rupee gives 1.1 rupees later, but those buy only 0.55 units of goods. The shortcut would say 10% − 100% = −90%, which is far off. This is why the shortcut only works for small rates.

7. Asset Markets I: No-Arbitrage (Lecture 5)

7.1 Same certainty ⟹ same return

With no uncertainty, nobody would hold an asset that pays a lower return than an otherwise identical one. So all riskless assets that are held must pay the same return.

7.2 The condition

Asset A: price p₀ now, known price p₁ tomorrow, no dividends. Asset B: bank deposit at rate r. Put ₹1 in each:

A: buys 1/p₀ units, worth p₁/p₀ tomorrow  |  B: worth 1 + r tomorrow
Equilibrium: 1 + r = p₁/p₀  ⟹  p₀ = p₁/(1 + r)

7.3 What if it fails? The arbitrage machine

Suppose 1 + r > p₁/p₀ (A is overpriced)
↓
Sell A today for p₀ (or short-sell it if you don't own it)
↓
Put the p₀ in the bank → p₀(1 + r) tomorrow
↓
Buy A back for p₁. Profit = p₀(1 + r) − p₁ > 0
↓
Everyone sells A → supply up → p₀ falls until 1 + r = p₁/p₀
Numbers: p₁ = 110, r = 10%Fair price = 110/1.1 = 100. Suppose A trades at 105. Sell A at 105; the bank turns it into 115.50; buy A back for 110; free profit 5.50 with no risk and no money of your own. Price must fall to 100.
Reverse case (extra, same logic): at 95, borrow 95 (owe 104.50), buy A, sell for 110 → profit 5.50. Demand pushes the price up to 100.
Riskless arbitrageBuying and selling equivalent assets to lock in a sure profit with zero risk and zero net investment. Well-functioning markets remove it quickly, so we can assume it does not last.
Big result: if no arbitrage exists, price = present value of future cash flows: p₀ = Σ C_t/(1+r)^t. This is why PV is the right tool for valuing assets.

7.4 Real-world adjustments

  • Liquidity: how easily an asset turns into cash without losing value. Government bonds are liquid, real estate is not. Illiquid assets must offer a liquidity premium (higher return, so lower price).
  • Risk: riskier assets must offer a risk premium, i.e. a required return above r.
  • The no-arbitrage rule assumes the two assets are identical except for money terms.

7.5 Slowing down: what does "short selling" mean?

Short selling = sell something you do not own. You borrow the asset, sell it now for p₀, and promise to give back the same asset later. If you can buy it back later for less than you sold it for, you keep the difference. For arbitrage you do not need your own money: you borrow the asset, sell it, and put the cash in the bank.

StepCash todayCash tomorrow
Borrow & sell A+p₀
Deposit p₀ in bank−p₀+p₀(1+r)
Buy A back (return it)−p₁
Total0p₀(1+r) − p₁ > 0

Zero today and positive tomorrow in every scenario: that is a pure arbitrage.

7.6 Extending to many periods (derivation)

Apply the one-period rule at each date. With a known price path p₀, p₁, p₂:

p₁ = p₂/(1+r)  (at date 1)  and  p₀ = p₁/(1+r)  (at date 0)
Substitute: p₀ = p₂/(1+r)²
Repeat: p₀ = p_T/(1+r)^T

If the asset also pays dividends d₁, d₂ …, then each period the total payoff is price plus dividend:

p₀ = (p₁ + d₁)/(1+r) = d₁/(1+r) + [d₂ + p₂]/(1+r)² = … = Σ d_t/(1+r)^t + p_T/(1+r)^T

If T is far away, the last term vanishes and price becomes the PV of all dividends. This is the basis of stock valuation.

7.7 Examples

Dividend asset: pays 6 next year, then trades at 106; r = 6%. Fair price = (106 + 6)/1.06 = 105.66. If it trades at 108: sell short at 108, bank → 114.48, buy back at 106 and pay the dividend 6 (the short seller must also pass on the dividend), total outflow 112. Profit = 2.48, so price falls.
Gold-type asset, no payments: price now 1000, known to be 1100 next year. r = 10%. Fair price = 1100/1.1 = 1000. Equal returns, so no arbitrage. If next year's price were 1155 (a 15.5% growth), the price today would have to jump to 1050 first.

7.8 Misconceptions

  • "Arbitrage means any profit": No. It must be riskless and need no investment. Buying a stock and hoping is not arbitrage.
  • "Arbitrage opportunities last": In well-functioning markets they vanish within moments, because traders rush to exploit them and their trades change the prices.
  • "All assets have the same return": Only riskless and otherwise identical assets. Risky or illiquid assets earn more (premiums).

8. Asset Markets II: The Forest and Continuous Compounding (Lecture 6)

8.1 The problem

A forest's wood value V(t) grows with time (price of wood normalised to 1). When should it be cut?

Naive answerWait until V(t) is largest: V′(t) = 0. Wrong, because it ignores that cutting earlier lets you put the money in the bank to earn interest.
Correct rule: compare the forest's growth rate with the bank. Cut when V′(t)/V(t) = r. Before that, the forest is "paying" more than the bank, so keep it. After that, it pays less, so sell and bank the money.

8.2 Why V′/V is the forest's rate of return

Waiting one more year adds V′ to the value; as a fraction of what you hold (V) that is V′/V. It is the return from holding, comparable with r.

8.3 Continuous compounding (derivation)

Interest paid n times a year at rate r/n each time. After T years:

(1 + r/n)^(nT)
Take the log: nT·ln(1 + r/n). For large n, r/n is tiny and ln(1 + x) ≈ x, so ≈ nT·(r/n) = rT
Hence lim (1 + r/n)^(nT) = e^(rT)
Payments per year n12412365∞

More frequent payments give only slightly more. Continuous compounding is mathematically convenient: FV = C₀e^(rT) and PV = C_T·e^(−rT).

8.4 Proof of the cutting rule

Forest worth V(t) at time t; present value v(t) = V(t)e^(−rt)
Maximise: dv/dt = V′(t)e^(−rt) + V(t)(−r)e^(−rt) = 0 (product rule)
Factor: e^(−rt)[V′(t) − rV(t)] = 0. Since e^(−rt) > 0: V′(t) = rV(t)
⟹ V′(t)/V(t) = r ∎

8.5 The numerical example: V(t) = −1000 + 1000t − 10t², r = 10%

Naive: V′ = 1000 − 20t = 0 ⟹ t = 50; V(50) = −1000 + 50,000 − 25,000 = 24,000
Rule: (1000 − 20t) / (−1000 + 1000t − 10t²) = 0.1
Cross-multiply: 1000 − 20t = −100 + 100t − t²
Rearrange: t² − 120t + 1100 = 0  ⟹  (t − 10)(t − 110) = 0
t = 110 is after the peak (V(110) = −12,000 < 0, meaningless). t* = 10; V(10) = −1000 + 10,000 − 1,000 = 8,000

8.7 Effective annual rate under continuous compounding

8.8 Why the rule "growth = interest" is natural (marginal thinking)

At any moment you can either hold the forest for one more year or sell it and bank the money. Holding earns V′ (the extra wood value). Selling and banking earns r·V. So:

This is the same comparison as in all of finance: compare your asset's return with the market's return.

9. Perpetuities, Annuities and Bonds (Lecture 7)

Real assets pay many times (a stock pays dividends for ages; a home loan has 200+ monthly payments). Adding a hundred discounted terms by hand is painful, so we use shortcut formulas valid when the cash flow has a pattern and r is constant.

9.1 Perpetuity: C every period forever, first payment at t = 1

PV = C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + C/(1+r)³ + …
Geometric series: first term a = C/(1+r), ratio q = 1/(1+r), |q| < 1 since r > 0
PV = a/(1 − q) = [C/(1+r)] / [r/(1+r)] = C/r

Quick second proof: a perpetuity at time 1 is worth the same PV again, so PV = C/(1+r) + PV/(1+r). Then PV·r/(1+r) = C/(1+r), so PV = C/r. Intuition: put C/r in the bank: the interest each year is exactly C, and the principal is never touched.

Example10% rate, receive 20 a year forever from next year: PV = 20/0.1 = 200. If the first payment comes today (t = 0): PV = C + C/r = 20 + 200 = 220.

9.2 Growing perpetuity: C₁ next year, then growing at g

PV = C₁/(1+r) + C₁(1+g)/(1+r)² + C₁(1+g)²/(1+r)³ + …
a = C₁/(1+r), q = (1+g)/(1+r); needs q < 1, i.e. g < r
PV = a/(1−q) = [C₁/(1+r)] / [(r−g)/(1+r)] = C₁/(r − g)
ExampleC₁ = 20, g = 5%, r = 10%: PV = 20/(0.10 − 0.05) = 400. Growth doubled the value versus the flat 200.
If g ≥ rthe series does not converge (the formula would give a negative or infinite value). It would mean payments grow faster than the discounting, which cannot persist forever.

9.3 Annuity: C for T periods only

Trick: annuity = (perpetuity starting at t = 1) − (perpetuity starting at t = T+1).

Period12…TT+1T+2…

minus Perpetuity 2−C−C…
Left overCCCC00…
Perpetuity 1 today: C/r
Perpetuity 2 is worth C/r at time T (its payments start at T+1), so today it is [C/r]/(1+r)^T
PV = (C/r)[1 − 1/(1+r)^T]
Example: 50 a year for 3 years at 10%Long way: 50/1.1 + 50/1.21 + 50/1.331 = 45.45 + 41.32 + 37.57 = 124.34. Formula: 500[1 − 1/1.331] = 500 × 0.2487 = 124.34 ✓

9.4 Application: the quiz-show "millionaire"

David wins 50,000 a year for 20 years (total 1,000,000), first payment in a year, r = 10%. Is he a millionaire?

PV = (50,000/0.10)[1 − 1/1.1²⁰] = 500,000 × (1 − 1/6.7275) = 500,000 × 0.8514 ≈ 425,678

Today's value is under 500,000 (even a perpetuity would only be 500,000). His finance-student relative is right; the headline adds money from different years without discounting.

9.6 Picture: annuity = perpetuity minus perpetuity (T = 4)

Perpetuity A pays from t = 1 forever (value C/r). Perpetuity B starts at t = 5 (value C/r at t = 4, so C/r ÷ (1+r)⁴ today). Subtract B from A and the payments after t = 4 cancel, leaving exactly four payments.

9.7 Perpetuity checks and extras

  • Why the first payment must be at t = 1: the formula C/r values the stream one period before the first payment. If the first payment is at t = 0 add C (as in the note). If it starts at t = 3, then C/r is the value at t = 2, so discount that by (1+r)².
  • Intuition for C/r: deposit C/r in a bank at rate r. Each year interest is (C/r)·r = C, which you can withdraw while leaving the deposit untouched forever.
  • Sensitivity: as r falls, perpetuity value explodes. At r = 10%, C = 20: 200. At r = 5%: 400. At r = 1%: 2,000.
Delayed perpetuity: 100 a year forever, first payment at t = 4, r = 10%. Value at t = 3 is 100/0.1 = 1,000. Today: 1,000/1.1³ = 751.32.

9.10 Using the annuity formula backwards: loan instalments

Borrow L today, repay a level instalment C for T periods. The loan's value to the bank equals PV of the instalments, so L = C × A(r,T), and therefore C = L·r / [1 − (1+r)^−T].

Example (extension of the lecture formula): ₹10,00,000 home loan, effective 8% a year, repaid monthly for 20 years (T = 240). Monthly rate r = 1.08^(1/12) − 1 = 0.6434%. Instalment C = 10,00,000 × 0.006434 / (1 − 1.08⁻²⁰) = ₹8,192 a month. Total repaid = 1,965,961, so about 965,961 is interest. (Banks usually quote 8%/12 per month instead; the method is the same.)

Common mistakes

  • Adding cash flows from different dates without discounting.
  • Halving a rate for half a year (use the square root). Dividing 50% by 2 for a yearly rate.
  • Forgetting that the annuity and perpetuity formulas give the value one period before the first payment.
  • Using the bond's coupon rate as the discount rate.
  • Thinking a fast-growing firm is automatically a good investment (price already includes growth).
  • Choosing the forest's cutting time at the peak of V(t).
