Part 1 · Statistics you need

Everything in this week sits on four statistical ideas. If they are clear, the rest is arithmetic.

1.1 Average vs expected value

Definition: expected value

The expected value of an uncertain payoff is the probability-weighted average of all its possible outcomes:

E(X) = p1x1 + p2x2 + … + pnxn   where p1 + … + pn = 1

It works like an ordinary average, but with one difference in time: an average is used for past outcomes (data that already happened), while an expected value is used for future outcomes (things that have not happened yet).

Example: coin toss

Toss a fair coin. Heads pays 100, tails pays 200.

E(winnings) = 0.5 × 100 + 0.5 × 200 = 150

Notice 150 is never actually paid. The expected value is a long-run average, not a prediction of one toss.

1.2 Random variable

Definition: random variable

A random variable is a quantity whose actual value is not yet known. In the coin example, let w = winnings. Then w equals 100 with probability 0.5 and 200 with probability 0.5.

After the toss, w is known, so it is no longer random.

Trick question from the slides

Is the expected value of w a random variable? No. You can compute 150 before the toss. A random variable is uncertain; its expected value is a plain number.

1.3 Fair bet

Definition: fair bet

A bet is fair when its price equals its expected payoff. Then both the seller and the buyer expect to break even.

Example: fair price of a bet

The bet pays 200 with probability 1/4, 1000 with probability 1/4 and 800 with probability 1/2.

E = ¼(200) + ¼(1000) + ½(800) = 50 + 250 + 400 = 700

So the fair price is 700. Pay less and you expect a profit; pay more and you expect a loss.

1.4 Risk and standard deviation

Expected value tells you the reward. It says nothing about risk. Two bets can both have mean 700, but one may pay exactly 700 and the other may pay 0 or 1400. In finance, risk = how widely the outcomes are spread around the mean. The usual measure is the standard deviation (SD).

Definition: variance and standard deviation
Variance = Σ pi(xi − μ)²     SD = √Variance

Why square? Deviations above and below the mean would cancel each other (their probability-weighted sum is always zero). Squaring makes every deviation positive. Taking the square root at the end brings the answer back to the units of the payoff.

Step by step for the bet above (μ = 700)

Payoff xProb px − 700(x − 700)²p × (x − 700)²
2000.25-500250,00062,500
10000.2530090,00022,500
8000.510010,0005,000
Variance90,000
SD = √90,000300

So SD = √90,000 = 300. Reading it: a typical outcome lies about 300 away from the mean of 700.

Shortcut formula (derivation)

Expand the square inside the variance:

Σ p(x − μ)² = Σ p x² − 2μ Σ p x + μ² Σ p
Use Σ p x = μ and Σ p = 1:
= Σ p x² − 2μ² + μ² = E(X²) − μ²

Check on our bet: E(X²) = ¼(200²) + ¼(1000²) + ½(800²) = 10,000 + 250,000 + 320,000 = 580,000. Minus μ² = 490,000 gives 90,000. Same answer.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Do not forget the probabilities. A plain average of (200, 1000, 800) is 666.7, not 700.
  • Do not forget to take the square root at the end (90,000 is the variance, not the SD).
  • SD of a rate of return is found the same way, with returns in place of payoffs.

Part 2 · Interest rates and credit risk

2.1 Why loans are not like bonds

A government bond pays what it promises. Most other loans do not: the borrower may be unable to repay in full. In this part we price such loans. To keep things clean we start with risk-neutral lenders: people who care only about expected values, not about how wild the outcomes are. (Risk-averse people come in Part 6.)

Key words
  • Solvent borrower: assets are at least liabilities, so the promise can be kept.
  • Default: the borrower cannot pay in full and hands over whatever it has (the recovery).
  • Promised (quoted / stated) rate: the rate written in the contract, paid only if there is no default.
  • Expected rate of return: the probability-weighted rate the lender really expects, counting defaults.
  • Time premium: the safe rate (government bond rate).
  • Default (credit) premium: promised rate minus the expected rate the lender needs. Default risk is also called credit risk.

2.2 Example 1: Amar lends to Bhaskar

Amar can lend 1,000,000 for one year to Bhaskar, or buy a government bond paying a safe 10% (so 1,100,000 back). Amar believes: with 50% Bhaskar is solvent and repays in full; with 50% he defaults and repays only 750,000.

Step 1: what if Amar charges only 10%?

E(payoff) = 0.5 × 1,100,000 + 0.5 × 750,000 = 925,000
E(return) = 925,000 / 1,000,000 − 1 = −7.5%

Amar loses compared with the bond (+10%). A 10% rate is too low for a risky borrower.

Step 2: find the promised payment X that makes Amar break even with the bond

Derivation
Required expected payoff = 1,000,000 × 1.10 = 1,100,000
0.5 × X + 0.5 × 750,000 = 1,100,000
0.5 X = 1,100,000 − 375,000 = 725,000
X = 1,450,000
Promised rate = 1,450,000 / 1,000,000 − 1 = 45%

Check: in the default state the lender earns 750,000/1,000,000 − 1 = −25%. So E(r) = 0.5(+45%) + 0.5(−25%) = 22.5% − 12.5% = 10% ✓.

Step 3: split the 45%

Important: the default premium is NOT a bonus

Of the 45%, only 10% is "real" reward for waiting (time premium). The other 35% is paid only in good times and exists just to refund the losses in bad times. On average the lender ends up with the same 10% as the safe bond. Newspapers and bond tables quote the promised rate, which is why risky bonds always look higher-yielding than they really are.

2.3 Example 2: Aparna lends 200 to Jyoti

Aparna believes Jyoti repays in full with probability 98%, repays half the borrowed amount (100) with probability 1%, and repays nothing with probability 1%. The government bond pays 5%.

If the promised rate is only 5%

Promised payment = 200 × 1.05 = 210
E(payoff) = 0.98 × 210 + 0.01 × 100 + 0.01 × 0 = 205.8 + 1 = 206.80
E(rate) = 206.80/200 − 1 = 3.40%  (less than the 5% bond)

Find the promised payment X that gives an expected 5%

Derivation
0.98 X + 0.01 × 100 + 0.01 × 0 = 210
0.98 X = 209
X = 209/0.98 = 213.27
Promised rate = (213.27 − 200)/200 = 6.63%

Check: E(r) = 0.98(+6.63%) + 0.01(−50%) + 0.01(−100%) = 6.50% − 0.5% − 1% = 5.0% ✓.

Default premium = 6.63% − 5% = 1.63%. Promised rate = time premium (5%) + default premium (1.63%).

Expected realised default premium is zero

Measure each state's return above the 5% time premium:

StateProbReturnReturn − 5%
Repays in full98%+6.63%+1.63%
Repays half1%−50%−55%
Repays nothing1%−100%−105%
E = 0.98(+1.63%) + 0.01(−55%) + 0.01(−105%) = 1.60% − 0.55% − 1.05% = 0%

This is the core of risk-neutral pricing: in a perfect market with risk-neutral investors every security has the same expected rate of return. The default premium is exactly used up by expected defaults.

2.4 General formula

Pricing a risky loan in general

Let the loan be L, the safe rate rf, repayment probability p, promised rate rp, and let the other states pay recoveries Ri with probabilities qi (p + Σqi = 1). Break-even requires:

L(1 + rf) = p · L(1 + rp) + Σ qiRi
1 + rp = [ L(1 + rf) − Σ qiRi ] / (p · L)

Check with Amar: [1,100,000 − 0.5 × 750,000] / (0.5 × 1,000,000) = 725,000/500,000 = 1.45 ✓. Check with Jyoti: (210 − 1)/(0.98 × 200) = 209/196 = 1.0663 ✓.

Find the safe rate rf➜List the states and probabilities➜Write recovery in each default state➜Set expected payoff = L(1+rf)➜Solve for the promised payment X➜Promised rate = X/L − 1

2.5 What a real market adds

The promised rate in the real world has two more pieces beyond the time and default premiums:

  • Risk premium: extra expected return that risk-averse investors demand for putting up with uncertainty.
  • Imperfect-market premium (for example a liquidity premium): extra return for the trouble of finding a buyer later.
promised rate = time premium + default premium + risk premium + liquidity premium

In the risk-neutral perfect market of this part, the last two are zero.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Do not call the whole 45% "profit". Only the time premium is reward; the default premium only refunds expected losses.
  • The lender's expected return is 10%, not 45%. Quoted rates are promised rates.
  • In the default state the lender loses money relative to the loan: 750,000 back on 1,000,000 lent is −25%, not 0%.

Part 3 · Uncertainty in capital budgeting

Capital budgeting means choosing which projects to take. With uncertainty we must handle three things: the scenarios (states) that matter, their probabilities, and the value of the project in each scenario. The standard tool is a payoff table (state table).

3.1 Example 3: buying a building

Next year the building will be worth 60,000 (probability 1/4, the "Bad" state) or 100,000 (probability 3/4, the "Good" state). The market's expected rate of return for a project of this riskiness and one-year maturity is 20%. (This rate comes from supply and demand in the financial markets.) What is the fair price today?

StateProbabilityValue next year
Bad1/460,000
Good3/4100,000

Method 1: expected value, then discount once

E(value) = ¼(60,000) + ¾(100,000) = 90,000
PV = 90,000 / (1 + 20%) = 75,000

Method 2: discount each state, then take the expected value

StateProbValueDiscount factorPresent value
Bad1/460,0001/1.250,000
Good3/4100,0001/1.283,333
PV = ¼(50,000) + ¾(83,333) = 12,500 + 62,500 = 75,000
Why the two methods must agree (proof)
Method 2: PV = Σ ps · Cs/(1 + r)
The discount factor 1/(1 + r) is the same in every state, so pull it out of the sum:
PV = [1/(1 + r)] · Σ psCs = E(C)/(1 + r) = Method 1 ∎

The same state-by-state idea works when different states need different discount rates, but then the two methods can differ. In this course one expected rate is used.

Rates of return state by state

Suppose you pay 75,000.

Bad: (60,000 − 75,000)/75,000 = −20%    Good: (100,000 − 75,000)/75,000 = +33.3%
E(r) = ¼(−20%) + ¾(33.3%) = −5% + 25% = 20% ✓

The expected return equals the 20% cost of capital. That is exactly what "fair price" means: the price at which the project earns the market's expected rate.

Recipe: valuing an uncertain cash flow
Build the payoff table➜Compute the expected cash flow➜Divide by (1 + expected rate)t➜Check: expected return = expected rate

3.2 Example 4: a two-year factory

A factory is worth 500,000 or 1,000,000 with equal probability in two years, depending on product demand. Cost of capital: 6% per year.

E(value at year 2) = ½(500,000) + ½(1,000,000) = 750,000
PV = 750,000 / (1.06)2 = 750,000 / 1.1236 ≈ 667,497.33
Mistakes to avoid
  • Divide by (1.06)² for two years, not by 1.06 × 2.
  • Do not discount at the promised rate of some other asset. Use the expected rate for this type of project.

Part 4 · Debt and equity

Most projects are paid for by a mix of debt (a loan) and equity (the owner's own money). The payoff-table tool from Part 3 lets us value each piece separately.

4.1 Debt and equity are state-contingent claims

The building in Part 3 cost 75,000. Suppose we finance it with a mortgage of 70,000 and the owner puts in the remaining 5,000. One project has become two projects, and different people can own them:

Project 1: Mortgage lending

The bank lends 70,000 and receives a promised amount next year. It is paid first. This is debt, also called a loan or leverage.

Project 2: Residual ownership

The owner owns the building but must repay the loan first. The owner gets only what is left over. This is equity, also called levered ownership or stock.

Two legal ideas
  • Non-recourse loan: if the building is worth less than the promise, the lender takes the building but cannot go after the borrower's other assets.
  • Limited liability: an owner cannot lose more than was invested (so equity never pays less than 0). A shareholder cannot be made to pay more than the price of the shares.
Debt payoff = min(C, promise)     Equity payoff = max(0, C − promise)     Debt + Equity = C
Building value C➜Pay lender first: min(C, D)➜Owner keeps the rest: max(0, C − D)

4.2 Step 1: find the promised amount to the lender

What promise D makes the bank willing to lend exactly 70,000 when the market's expected return is 20%? In the bad state the bank only gets the building (60,000). In the good state it gets D.

Derivation
PV of what the bank expects = loan amount
70,000 = ¼ · 60,000/1.2 + ¾ · D/1.2
Multiply by 1.2: 84,000 = 15,000 + 0.75 D
0.75 D = 69,000
D = 92,000

The borrower promises 92,000 and pays it in the good state. This is a promised rate of 92,000/70,000 − 1 = 31.4%.

The bank's returns

Good state: (92,000 − 70,000)/70,000 = +31.4%    Bad state: (60,000 − 70,000)/70,000 = −14.3%
E(r) = ¼(−14.3%) + ¾(31.4%) = −3.6% + 23.6% = 20% ✓

The owner pays 75,000 − 70,000 = 5,000 and promises the bank 92,000 next year.

Equity: good (8,000 − 5,000)/5,000 = +60%    bad (0 − 5,000)/5,000 = −100%    E(r) = ¼(−100%) + ¾(60%) = 20%

4.5 Which claim is riskiest?

Debt is the safest because the owner's equity absorbs the first losses. Equity is the riskiest. The whole building sits in between. Taking the loan split one medium-risk project into one safer piece and one riskier piece.

Risk-neutral vs risk-averse world

With risk-neutral investors, every claim above has the same 20% expected return no matter how risky: the risk premium is zero. With risk-averse investors we would see a ladder: debt < whole project < equity in expected return, because riskier claims must pay more on average.

4.6 Why debt is called "leverage"

A lever lets a small push move a big weight, but the movement is also bigger. Financial leverage works the same way: a small amount of your own money controls a big asset, and gains and losses on your own money are magnified.

Real projects have more than two outcomes. The method is the same: write the payoff in every state, then take the expected value.

4.7 Five equally likely building values

The building can be worth 60,000, 70,000, 80,000, 90,000 or 100,000, each with probability 0.2. The expected rate is 20%.

E(value) = 80,000   ⟹   PV of the building = 80,000/1.2 ≈ 66,666.67
Q1. A loan promises 60,000. What does the lender expect?

The building is worth at least 60,000 in every state, so the promise is always kept. Expected receipt = 60,000. This loan is risk-free.

Q2. A loan promises 61,000. What does the lender expect?

With probability 80% the building is worth 70,000 or more, so the full 61,000 is paid. With probability 20% it is worth only 60,000, so the lender gets just 60,000.

E = 0.8 × 61,000 + 0.2 × 60,000 = 48,800 + 12,000 = 60,800
General rule (derivation): the expected receipt as a function of the promise x

Think of the promise as a stack of 10,000-wide slices. The lender collects a slice only if the building is worth at least the top of that slice. The first 60,000 is always collected. After that, each slice is collected with a smaller probability:

x between 60,000 and 70,000: E = 60,000 + 80%(x − 60,000)

Q3. How big a promise gives a loan of 50,000 today?

The lender needs an expected receipt of 50,000 × 1.2 = 60,000. A promise of 60,000 already does it (and is risk-free). So the borrower promises 60,000, a 20% rate.

Q4. What is the largest loan the building can support?

No promise can have an expected receipt above 80,000 (the expected building value). So the biggest loan today is 80,000/1.2 ≈ 66,666.67, which needs a promise of 100,000.

4.9 The common mistake: promised cash flow at promised rate

The trap

Some people take the promised cash flow and discount it at the promised rate. This double-counts the default premium and makes bad projects look good. The correct method is always: expected cash flow discounted at the expected rate.

Example 6 (slides)

The expected rate of return is 20%. A bond needs 100 invested. It promises 25 of interest, the principal is fully insured, but there is a 50% chance of default on the interest. Other risky bonds quote 11.4% more, so 31.4%. Should you invest?

Wrong way: promised flows at the promised rate

NPV = −100 + 100/1.20 + 25/1.314 = −100 + 83.33 + 19.03 ≈ +2.36 (looks good, invest)

Right way: expected flows at the expected rate

Expected interest = 0.5 × 25 + 0.5 × 0 = 12.5
NPV = −100 + (100 + 12.5)/1.20 = −100 + 93.75 = −6.25 (do not invest)

The correct NPV is negative. The wrong method turned a bad deal into an apparent gain of +2.36.

Promised cash flows➜× probabilities of getting them➜= expected cash flows➜÷ (1 + expected rate)➜− cost = true NPV

Part 5 · Banks, balance sheets and leverage

5.1 Balance sheet and financial intermediaries

Definitions
  • Balance sheet: a list of a bank's (or firm's) assets and liabilities at one point in time.
  • Assets = what the bank owns plus what others owe the bank (reserves at the central bank, loans, mortgages, government bonds).
  • Liabilities = what the bank owes (checkable deposits, money borrowed from other investors and banks).
  • Capital = what is left for the owners.
Assets = Liabilities + Capital

Banks are financial intermediaries: institutions that take funds from households and firms and use them to buy financial assets or make loans. "Nonbanks" such as mortgage companies, money-market funds and hedge funds are intermediaries too. They are useful because they build expertise about particular borrowers and lend with those borrowers' special features in mind.

Example: a bank has assets 100, liabilities 90, capital 10. Think of the owners as having put in 10 of their own money, borrowed 90 from others, and bought assets worth 100.

Capital ratio and leverage ratio
Capital ratio = Capital / Assets = 10/100 = 10%
Leverage ratio = Assets / Capital = 100/10 = 10  (= 1 ÷ capital ratio)

5.2 What leverage does: the trade-off

The bank chooses its leverage by weighing two effects:

  1. A higher leverage ratio gives a higher expected profit per unit of capital.
  2. A higher leverage ratio gives a higher chance of bankruptcy.

Effect 1: profit

Suppose assets earn 5% and liabilities cost 4% (expected).

Leverage 10Leverage 5
Assets100100
Liabilities9080
Capital1020
Expected profit = 100 × 5% − liabilities × 4%5 − 3.6 = 1.45 − 3.2 = 1.8
Profit per unit of capital1.4/10 = 14%1.8/20 = 9%
General formula (derivation)
Let Λ = leverage = A/E, rA = return on assets, rL = cost of liabilities.
Liabilities = A − E. Profit = A·rA − (A − E)·rL
Divide by E: profit per unit of capital = Λ·rA − (Λ − 1)·rL
= rL + Λ (rA − rL)

So every extra unit of leverage adds (rA − rL) = 1% to the return on capital. Check: Λ = 10 gives 4% + 10 × 1% = 14% ✓; Λ = 5 gives 4% + 5% = 9% ✓.

Leverage Λ251020
Profit per unit of capital6%9%14%24%
Fall in assets that wipes out the bank50%20%10%5%

Effect 2: solvency

Solvent and insolvent

A bank is solvent if its assets are worth more than its liabilities, and insolvent if they are worth less. The cushion is the capital:

Assets can fall by Capital/Assets = 1/Λ before the bank is insolvent

With leverage 5 the assets can fall from 100 to 80 before insolvency. With leverage 10 they can fall only to 90. High leverage = thin cushion.

Magnification of losses

If assets fall by 5, capital falls by 5. Leverage 10: capital 10 → 5, a 50% loss of owners' money. Leverage 5: capital 20 → 15, a 25% loss. Same asset loss, double the pain at double the leverage.

5.3 Deleveraging: when a good bank is hit

The bank decides leverage 5 is optimal: assets 100, liabilities 80, capital 20. Now assets fall to 90.

BeforeAfter the fallAfter recalling loans
Assets1009050
Liabilities808040
Capital201010
Leverage595
  • The bank is still solvent, but now riskier (leverage 9).
  • It can raise new capital or shrink its balance sheet.
  • Shrinking: it recalls loans worth 40 and uses the cash to repay liabilities by 40. Assets 50, liabilities 40, capital 10, leverage 5 again.
  • The cost: lending fell by 40. When many banks do this together, firms and households cannot borrow, which is a credit crunch.
Mistakes to avoid
  • Capital ratio is Capital ÷ Assets, leverage is Assets ÷ Capital. Do not divide by liabilities.
  • When assets fall, liabilities stay fixed. The whole loss lands on capital.
  • Higher leverage raises the expected profit per unit of capital, but not for free: it also raises risk.

Part 6 · Choice under uncertainty

Parts 1 to 5 priced assets for people who only care about expected values. Now we ask how a person with feelings about risk chooses, using insurance as the main example.

6.1 Uncertainty and rational responses

Things that are uncertain in an economy: tomorrow's prices, future wealth, whether goods will be available, and what other people will do now and later. Sensible responses include buying insurance (health, life, car) and holding a portfolio of state-contingent consumption: choosing how much you will consume in each possible future.

6.2 States of nature and contingent contracts

Definitions
  • State of nature: one of the possible futures. Here: accident (a) and no accident (na).
  • An accident happens with probability πa; no accident with πna = 1 − πa. An accident causes a loss L.
  • A state-contingent contract is carried out only if a particular state occurs, e.g. the insurer pays only if there is an accident.
  • A state-contingent consumption plan is a plan for what to consume in each state, e.g. take a holiday only if there is no accident.

6.3 The state-contingent budget constraint

Wealth is m. Each unit of accident coverage costs γ (the premium rate), paid whether or not an accident happens. Let Ca and Cna be consumption in the two states.

Without insurance: Ca = m − L and Cna = m. This is the endowment point.

Derivation of the budget line (buy K units of coverage)
No accident: you pay the premium and nothing comes back: Cna = m − γK
Accident: you lose L, pay the premium, and the insurer pays K: Ca = m − L − γK + K = m − L + (1 − γ)K
Solve the second equation for K: K = (Ca − m + L)/(1 − γ)
Put into the first: Cna = m − γ(Ca − m + L)/(1 − γ)
Combine over (1 − γ): Cna = [m(1 − γ) − γCa + γm − γL]/(1 − γ)
Cna = (m − γL)/(1 − γ) − [γ/(1 − γ)] · Ca

Slope = −γ/(1 − γ). It says: to get one more unit of consumption in the accident state, you must give up γ/(1 − γ) units in the no-accident state. (The lecture summary writes the same line as Cna = m − [γ/(1−γ)][Ca − (m − L)]. Expanding it gives the same equation.)

Worked example: m = 100, L = 50, γ = 0.2
Coverage KC_na = 100 − 0.2KC_a = 50 + 0.8K

Line: Cna = (100 − 10)/0.8 − 0.25 Ca = 112.5 − 0.25 Ca

Checks: Ca = 50 gives 112.5 − 12.5 = 100 ✓. Ca = 90 gives 112.5 − 22.5 = 90 ✓. Slope −0.25 means each rupee given up when there is no accident buys 4 rupees when there is one.

6.4 Preferences under uncertainty

To choose we need a way to compare plans like (Ca, Cna). Assume the person has sensible preferences, so we can describe them with a utility function, a score of happiness. Since how much you value consumption in a state also depends on how likely that state is, write it as U(ca, cna, πa, πna).

The simplest example is the expected value itself:

U = πa ca + πna cna

Why expected value alone is not enough: the game show (St Petersburg paradox)

A fair coin is tossed until the first head. If the first head comes on toss n, you win 2n rupees. How much do you expect to win?

Expected winnings
The first head arrives on toss n with probability (½)n, and then you win 2n.
n = 1: ½ × 2 = 1;   n = 2: ¼ × 4 = 1;   n = 3: ⅛ × 8 = 1; …
E(X) = 1 + 1 + 1 + … = ∞

The expected value is infinite, yet almost nobody would pay even a few hundred rupees to play. So people do not simply maximise expected money. They care about the satisfaction from money, and that rises more slowly as the amount grows.

6.5 Expected utility

Expected utility function

Put a function u(·) on consumption in each state and average with the probabilities:

U(c1, c2, π1, π2) = π1u(c1) + π2u(c2)

This is the average utility of the plan. The expected-value example above is the special case u(c) = c. If a state is certain (π = 1) then u(c) is just the utility of that sure consumption.

The gamble: should he take it?

A person has 10 units of wealth. A gamble gives a 50% chance of winning 5 and a 50% chance of losing 5, so final wealth is 15 or 5 with equal chance. Compare:

Not gamble: u(10)     Gamble: ½u(15) + ½u(5)

If he only cared about the expected value, both give wealth 10 on average and he would be indifferent. The answer depends on the shape of u.

TypeShape of uTestExample uCheck with wealth 5 / 10 / 15Attitude to a fair gamble
Risk averseStrictly concave (slope flattens). u'' < 0u(10) > ½u(15) + ½u(5)√w , ln w√10 = 3.162 > ½(√15 + √5) = 3.055Refuses it

Why concave means risk averse (intuition)

On a concave curve, each extra rupee adds less satisfaction than the one before. Gaining 5 adds less happiness than losing 5 takes away. So the 50-50 gamble lowers expected utility even though the average wealth is unchanged. The more concave u is, the more risk averse the person.

Extra (useful in exams): certainty equivalent

The certainty equivalent of a gamble is the sure amount that gives the same utility. For u = √w the gamble above has expected utility 3.0545, so CE = 3.0545² = 9.33. This is less than 10: the person would swap the gamble for a sure 9.33, so the risk costs him about 0.67. This extension is not in the slides but appears in assignment questions.

6.6 Indifference curves and the marginal rate of substitution

All plans (Ca, Cna) with the same expected utility are equally good. They lie on an indifference curve:

πa u(Ca) + πna u(Cna) = Ū

The slope of this curve is the marginal rate of substitution (MRS): how much Cna you must be given to accept a little less Ca and stay equally happy.

Derivation of the MRS
Along the curve Ū is constant, so dŪ = 0.
Differentiate: πau′(Ca) dCa + πnau′(Cna) dCna = 0
Move one term: πnau′(Cna) dCna = − πau′(Ca) dCa
dCna/dCa = − πa MU(Ca) / [πna MU(Cna)]    where MU(x) = u′(x)

The slope is negative (indifference curves slope down), and for a risk-averse person MU falls as consumption rises, so the curve is bowed toward the origin (convex). On the 45° line Ca = Cna the marginal utilities are equal, so the slope there is just −πa/πna.

Worked example: u = √c

u′(c) = 1/(2√c). So

MRS = πa/πna × [1/(2√Ca)] / [1/(2√Cna)] = (πa/πna) × √Cna/√Ca

With πa = 5% this is (1/19) √Cna/√Ca.

6.7 The consumer's optimum

The person chooses the best plan on the budget line. The best point is where an indifference curve just touches the line (tangency): the slope of the curve equals the slope of the budget line.

πaMU(Ca) / [πnaMU(Cna)] = γ/(1 − γ)

(Both slopes are negative; we drop the minus signs.) The left side is how the person values accident consumption relative to no-accident consumption. The right side is how the market prices it. At the best point they are equal.

6.8 Competitive insurance: fair price

If anyone can enter the insurance business, competition drives expected profit to zero. The insurer collects γK, pays K with probability πa, and pays nothing otherwise:

Expected profit = γK − πaK − πna·0 = (γ − πa)K = 0  ⟹  γ = πa

When the price of coverage equals the accident probability, insurance is actuarially fair.

Fundamental result: a risk-averse person buys full insurance when it is fair
Start from the optimum: πaMU(Ca)/[πnaMU(Cna)] = γ/(1 − γ)
Fair price: γ = πa and 1 − γ = πna, so the right side is πa/πna
Cancel πa/πna on both sides: MU(Ca) = MU(Cna)
Risk aversion means u is concave, so MU is a strictly decreasing function. A strictly decreasing function takes each value only once, so equal MU means equal consumption: Ca = Cna
Put Ca = Cna into the budget equations: m − L − γK + K = m − γK
So K − L = 0, i.e. K = L ∎

The person insures the whole loss and consumes the same in both states, with no risk left. Graphically the optimum sits on the 45° line.

Worked example: Shahid's apple farm

No landslide: profit 1,000,000. Landslide (probability 5%): profit only 40,000, a loss of L = 960,000. Insurance costs Rs 5 per Rs 100 of coverage, so γ = 0.05 = πa: fair.

Full insurance K = 960,000 costs 0.05 × 960,000 = 48,000. Then Cna = 1,000,000 − 48,000 = 952,000 and Ca = 40,000 − 48,000 + 960,000 = 952,000. Equal ✓.

With u = √c: no insurance U = 0.05√40,000 + 0.95√1,000,000 = 10 + 950 = 960. Full insurance U = √952,000 = 975.7. Better ✓.

6.9 Unfair insurance

If insurers make positive expected profit, then γK − πaK > 0, so γ > πa.

Result: less than full insurance
At the optimum πaMU(Ca)/[πnaMU(Cna)] = γ/(1 − γ)
Since γ > πa, we have γ/(1 − γ) > πa/πna
So MU(Ca)/MU(Cna) = [γ/(1 − γ)] × [πna/πa] > 1, that is MU(Ca) > MU(Cna)
MU is decreasing, so a larger MU means smaller consumption: Ca < Cna

The consumer buys less than full insurance. (If insurance were priced below πa, the same steps give Ca > Cna: over-insurance.)

Numerical illustration: same farm, but γ = 10% (unfair)

With u = √c the tangency condition is (π/(1−π)) √Cna/√Ca = γ/(1−γ). Solving: Ca/Cna = [π(1−γ)/((1−π)γ)]² = (0.045/0.095)² = 0.2244.

Budget line: Cna = (m − γL)/(1 − γ) − [γ/(1−γ)]Ca = 1,004,444 − 0.1111 Ca. Substituting Ca = 0.2244 Cna gives Cna ≈ 980,012 and Ca ≈ 219,892.

Coverage K = (Ca − 40,000)/0.9 ≈ 199,880, which is below the full 960,000 and leaves Ca < Cna. ✓ (This is an extra example; the slides only prove the direction.)

Fair price γ = πa➜MU(Ca) = MU(Cna)➜Ca = Cna➜Full insurance K = L
Mistakes to avoid
  • The premium γK is paid in both states. Forgetting it in the no-accident state is the most common slip.
  • Axes matter. With Ca on the horizontal axis the budget slope is −γ/(1−γ). If you swap the axes the slope becomes −(1−γ)/γ.
  • Risk averse + fair price = full insurance. Say both conditions in a proof.

Practice problems with solutions

Tap a problem to see the full solution. Try it on paper first. Problems 3, 5, 6, 7 follow the style of the Week 5 assignment.

Problem 1

A bet pays 100 or 300 with equal probability. Find its fair price and its standard deviation.

Solution

E = 0.5(100) + 0.5(300) = 200, the fair price. Deviations are −100 and +100, squared 10,000 each, so variance = 10,000 and SD = 100.

Problem 2

A fair die is rolled. If an odd number shows, you are paid double the number; if an even number shows, you are paid half the number. Find the fair price and the SD.

Solution

Payoffs for faces 1 to 6: 2, 1, 6, 2, 10, 3. Mean = 24/6 = 4. E(X²) = (4 + 1 + 36 + 4 + 100 + 9)/6 = 154/6 = 25.67. Variance = 25.67 − 16 = 9.67, SD = 3.11.

Problem 3

A friend asks for a 1-year loan of 500. The safe rate is 8%. You think he repays in full with probability 90% and repays nothing with probability 10%. What rate should you quote? What are the time and default premiums?

Solution

Needed: 500 × 1.08 = 540 in expectation. 0.9X + 0.1 × 0 = 540, so X = 600. Promised rate = 600/500 − 1 = 20%. Time premium 8%, default premium 12%. Check: 0.9(+20%) + 0.1(−100%) = 18% − 10% = 8% ✓.

Problem 4

Loan of 1,000. Safe rate 6%. Repays in full 95%, half the borrowed amount 3%, nothing 2%. Find the promised rate.

Solution

Needed 1,060. 0.95X + 0.03(500) + 0.02(0) = 1,060, so 0.95X = 1,045 and X = 1,100. Promised rate = 10%, default premium = 4%.

Problem 5

(Assignment style) The government bond pays 8%. You have 100,000. Raman repays in full with probability 90%, repays half the borrowed amount with probability 5%, and nothing with probability 5%. What interest rate should you ask? What are the time premium and default premium?

Solution

Needed: 108,000. 0.9 × 100,000(1 + r) + 0.05 × 50,000 = 108,000 → 90,000(1 + r) = 105,500 → 1 + r = 1.1722. Promised rate 17.22%. Time premium 8%, default premium 9.22%.

Problem 6

(Assignment style) Rakesh is risk-neutral. Option 1: government bonds paying 9%. Option 2: a project paying 50% if it succeeds and returning only 75% of the money if it fails. For which probability of failure is he indifferent?

Solution

Per unit invested: bond gives 1.09. Project gives 1.5 if it succeeds and 0.75 if it fails. With failure probability p: 1.5(1 − p) + 0.75p = 1.09 → 1.5 − 0.75p = 1.09 → p = 0.41/0.75 = 41/75.

Problem 7

(Assignment style) You build a road. Cost is 80,000 in good weather (60%) and 120,000 in bad weather (40%). You collect 110,000 afterwards. What is the expected rate of return?

Solution

Return by state: good weather 110/80 − 1 = +37.5%; bad weather 110/120 − 1 = −8.33%. Expected = 0.6(37.5%) + 0.4(−8.33%) = 22.5% − 3.33% = 19.17%. (Rate of return in each state first, then the expectation, as in the building example.)

Problem 8

A project is worth 80 or 120 next year with equal probability. The expected rate is 10%. Find its PV and check the expected return.

Solution

E = 100, PV = 100/1.1 = 90.91. Returns: 80/90.91 − 1 = −12%, 120/90.91 − 1 = +32%. E(r) = ½(−12%) + ½(32%) = 10% ✓.

Problem 9

A factory is worth 200 or 400 with equal probability in three years. The cost of capital is 8% a year. Find its PV.

Solution

E = 300. PV = 300/1.08³ = 300/1.2597 = 238.15.

Problem 10

A building is worth 40 (probability 0.4) or 100 (probability 0.6) next year. The expected rate is 10%. (a) Find its PV. (b) It is financed by a loan of 50. What is the promised payment and rate? (c) Find the equity's value and return in each state.

Solution

(a) E = 0.4(40) + 0.6(100) = 76. PV = 76/1.1 = 69.09.

(b) 50 = [0.4(40) + 0.6D]/1.1 → 55 = 16 + 0.6D → D = 65. Promised rate 65/50 − 1 = 30%. Lender returns: bad 40/50 − 1 = −20%, good +30%; E = 0.4(−20%) + 0.6(30%) = 10% ✓.

(c) Equity pays 0 (bad) or 100 − 65 = 35 (good). Expected = 21, PV = 21/1.1 = 19.09 (= 69.09 − 50 ✓). Returns: bad −100%; good 35/19.09 − 1 = +83.3%. E = 0.4(−100%) + 0.6(83.3%) = 10% ✓.

Problem 11

Five-state building (60k to 100k, each 0.2). What does a lender expect if the promise is 75,000? What is the most that can be borrowed today at 20%?

Solution

E = 60,000 + 80%(10,000) + 60%(5,000) = 71,000. Direct check: 0.2(60k) + 0.2(70k) + 0.6(75k) = 12 + 14 + 45 = 71k ✓. The maximum expected receipt is 80,000, so the maximum loan is 80,000/1.2 = 66,666.67.

Problem 12

A product pays 10,000 (probability 30%) or 50,000 (70%). The expected rate is 5%. A loan promises 30,000. Find the loan's price, promised rate and equity value.

Solution

Loan receives 10,000 (bad) or 30,000 (good). E = 3,000 + 21,000 = 24,000. Price = 24,000/1.05 = 22,857. Promised rate = 30,000/22,857 − 1 = 31.25%. Equity pays 0 or 20,000, E = 14,000, value = 13,333.33. Check: total E = 3,000 + 35,000 = 38,000 → 36,190.48 = 22,857.14 + 13,333.33 ✓.

Problem 13

You can invest 200 in a bond promising 280 next year (a quoted 40% rate). There is a 25% chance of default with nothing recovered. The expected rate for this risk is 10%. A friend says: NPV = 280/1.3 − 200 (using a 30% quoted rate for similar bonds). Who is right?

Solution

The friend discounts a promised cash flow at a promised rate: 280/1.3 − 200 = +15.38. The correct way: E(payoff) = 0.75 × 280 = 210; PV = 210/1.1 = 190.91; NPV = -9.09. Do not invest.

Problem 14

A bank has assets 200, liabilities 170 and capital 30. Assets earn 6%, liabilities cost 3.5%. Find the capital ratio, leverage ratio, profit per unit of capital and the fall in assets that makes it insolvent.

Solution

Capital ratio 30/200 = 15%; leverage 200/30 = 6.67. Profit = 12 − 5.95 = 6.05; per unit of capital 6.05/30 = 20.17% (check: 3.5% + 6.67 × 2.5% = 20.17% ✓). Insolvent if assets fall by more than 30, that is 15%.

Problem 15

A bank has assets 100, liabilities 90, capital 10. Assets fall to 95. (a) New leverage? (b) How much must it shrink to get back to leverage 10?

Solution

(a) Capital = 95 − 90 = 5, so leverage = 95/5 = 19. (b) With capital 5, leverage 10 needs assets of 50, so liabilities 45. It must recall and repay 45 (assets 95 → 50). Lending falls sharply, which is the credit-crunch mechanism.

Problem 16

m = 200, L = 100, γ = 0.25. Find the endowment point, the budget line, and the consumption if the person buys full insurance.

Solution

Endowment (Ca, Cna) = (100, 200). Full insurance K = 100 costs 25, so Ca = Cna = 200 − 25 = 175. Budget line: Cna = (200 − 25)/0.75 − (0.25/0.75)Ca = 233.33 − Ca/3. Check Ca = 175: 233.33 − 58.33 = 175 ✓. Slope −1/3.

Problem 17

u(w) = √w. A person with wealth 100 is offered a 50-50 gamble to win or lose 36. Does he accept?

Solution

Not gambling: u(100) = 10. Gambling: ½√136 + ½√64 = ½(11.662) + ½(8) = 9.831 < 10. √w is concave (risk averse), so he declines.

Problem 18

πa = 0.2. If insurance costs γ = 0.2, how much does a risk-averse person buy? If γ = 0.25, what changes and why?

Solution

γ = 0.2 = πa is fair, so MU(Ca) = MU(Cna), hence Ca = Cna and K = L (full insurance). With γ = 0.25 > πa the insurer makes expected profit, γ/(1 − γ) = 1/3 > πa/πna = 1/4, so MU(Ca) > MU(Cna) and Ca < Cna: less than full insurance.

Problem 19

In 3 to 4 lines: why is the default premium not extra compensation for risk?

Solution

The default premium is the gap between the promised rate and the expected rate. It only refunds the average loss from defaults. Averaged over states, the lender earns the same expected rate as on a safe bond (the expected realised default premium is zero). Compensation for bearing risk is a different thing, the risk premium, and it is zero when investors are risk-neutral.

Problem 20

Write the proof that a risk-averse consumer fully insures at a fair price (5 marks).

Solution

Outline to write: (1) state the budget equations Cna = m − γK and Ca = m − L + (1 − γ)K; (2) the tangency condition πaMU(Ca)/[πnaMU(Cna)] = γ/(1 − γ); (3) free entry gives zero profit so γ = πa; (4) cancel to get MU(Ca) = MU(Cna); (5) risk aversion means MU is strictly decreasing, so Ca = Cna; (6) substitute into the budget equations to get K = L.

Ten lines to remember
  1. Average is for the past, expected value for the future.
  2. Fair price = expected payoff.
  3. Risk = SD of outcomes.
  4. A risky lender must charge a higher promised rate so the expected rate equals the safe rate.
  5. Default premium ≠ reward for risk.
  6. Value = expected cash flow ÷ (1 + expected rate).
  7. Equity gets what is left after debt; both together equal the asset.
  8. Leverage raises expected profit per unit of capital and also risk.
  9. Concave utility means risk averse.
  10. Fair insurance + risk averse = full insurance.
