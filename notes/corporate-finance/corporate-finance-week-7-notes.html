How to read these notes

Weeks 5 and 6 taught you to price uncertain payoffs and to choose between a safe and a risky asset. Week 7 asks the big practical question: if there are many risky assets, how should I split my money among them? This is called portfolio theory (Harry Markowitz, 1950s). It is the base of the CAPM you saw in Week 6 and of every index fund.

The big idea in one paragraph

Describe every asset by just two numbers: its expected return (reward) and its standard deviation (risk). Mixing assets gives a portfolio with its own pair of numbers. Because assets do not move perfectly together, a mix can be less risky than any single asset in it. This free reduction in risk is called diversification. Markowitz’s method finds, for each level of reward, the mix with the least risk.

Part 1: One safe asset and one risky asset (CF_7A)

1.1 The problem

You have 100 to invest. There are two options and you may pick only one (mixing comes later):

  • X pays 105 with certainty.
  • Y pays 120 with probability 0.8 and 90 with probability 0.2.

1.2 Turn money into rates of return

The rate of return is (money received − money invested) / money invested.

rX = (105 − 100)/100 = 5%,   σX = 0 (no uncertainty)
rY = (120 − 100)/100 = +20%   or   (90 − 100)/100 = −10%

Expected return of Y (weighted average with the probabilities)

E(rY) = 0.8 × 0.20 + 0.2 × (−0.10) = 0.16 − 0.02 = 0.14 = 14%

Variance and standard deviation of Y

Variance is the probability-weighted average of the squared distance from the mean.

σY² = 0.8(0.20 − 0.14)² + 0.2(−0.10 − 0.14)² = 0.8(0.0036) + 0.2(0.0576) = 0.00288 + 0.01152 = 0.0144
σY = √0.0144 = 0.12 = 12%
Shortcut check

Variance = E(r²) − [E(r)]². Here E(r²) = 0.8(0.04) + 0.2(0.01) = 0.034, and 0.034 − 0.14² = 0.034 − 0.0196 = 0.0144. Same answer, less work when the mean is an ugly number.

Y gives 9 percentage points more on average but with a 12% spread. Is it worth it? It depends on the person.

1.2b Who picks what?

  • A risk-neutral person only looks at the average: picks Y (14% > 5%).
  • A risk-averse person might pick either. She dislikes the spread but likes the extra 9%.
  • Mean-variance preferences means the person ranks choices using only the mean and the standard deviation. That can be written as a utility function or drawn as indifference curves.

1.3 The mean-variance utility function

Mean-variance utility
U = E(r) − ½ A σ²

Here E(r) and σ are in decimals (14% is 0.14). A measures risk attitude:

  • A = 0: risk-neutral. Only E(r) matters.
  • A > 0: risk-averse. Variance is a penalty. A bigger A means more risk aversion.
  • A < 0: risk lover. Variance adds to utility.

The ½ is only a convenience: it makes later derivatives clean. The utility is measured in the same units as a rate of return, so a utility of 0.1256 means “as good as a sure 12.56%”.

1.4 Worked example: A = 2

U(X) = 0.05 − ½(2)(0)² = 0.05
U(Y) = 0.14 − ½(2)(0.12)² = 0.14 − 1 × 0.0144 = 0.1256

0.1256 > 0.05, so this person prefers Y. The 9% extra return beats the 1.44% penalty for risk.

1.5 Question: how good must X be to win?

Let X pay a certain rate rX. Then U(X) = rX (no variance penalty). Keep U(Y) = 0.1256.

U(X) > U(Y)  ⇔  rX > 0.1256 = 12.56%

So the safe asset must pay more than 12.56% to beat Y. The gap between 12.56% and 5% shows how strongly this person likes Y.

1.7 Indifference curves

An indifference curve collects all (σ, E) pairs that give the same utility Ū. Solve the utility formula for E:

Derivation: shape of an indifference curve
Ū = E − ½Aσ² ⇒ E = Ū + ½Aσ²
Slope: dE/dσ = Aσ. For A > 0 and σ > 0 this is positive, so the curve slopes upward: more risk must be paid for with more return.
Curvature: d²E/dσ² = A > 0, so the curve is convex (it gets steeper as σ grows).
At σ = 0 the slope is 0: near no risk, a tiny bit of risk costs almost nothing. Larger A gives a larger slope at every σ, i.e. steeper curves.

Part 2: Returns, short selling and portfolio return

2.1 Total return and rate of return

An asset is anything you can buy and sell. Buy at time 0 for X₀ and sell a year later for X₁.

Two ways to say the same thing
Total return R = X₁ / X₀     Rate of return r = (X₁ − X₀) / X₀ = R − 1

R is “how many rupees come back per rupee put in”. r is the gain per rupee. Example: X₀ = 100, X₁ = 105 gives R = 1.05 and r = 0.05.

2.2 Short selling

You can sell an asset you do not own. This is called short selling (shorting). It pays off when the price falls.

Borrow the asset from an owner➜Sell it now for X₀➜Wait➜Buy it back for X₁➜Return it to the lender

Cash flows: you receive X₀ at time 0 and pay X₁ at time 1. Your profit is X₀ − X₁. It is profitable if X₁ < X₀.

Example 1 (slides): Rita shorts 100 shares
Price is 10 per share. Rita borrows 100 shares and sells them: receives 100 × 10 = 1,000.
A year later the price has fallen to 9. She buys 100 shares for 900 and returns them.
Profit = 1,000 − 900 = 100.
Compare a normal buyer: bought at 10, sold at 9, rate of return r = (9 − 10)/10 = −10%, a loss of 100 on 1,000.
For Rita the outlay is −1,000 (she received money). Profit = outlay × r = (−1,000) × (−0.1) = +100. A negative return times a negative investment is a profit.
The slide writes total return as R = (−X₁)/(−X₀) = X₁/X₀ = 900/1,000 = 0.9, so r = −0.1, exactly the same as the long buyer. The sign of the investment is what flips the money.

Why shorting is dangerous

2.3 Portfolio return

Suppose n assets exist and you split an amount X₀ among them. Let X₀ᵢ be the amount in asset i, so ΣX₀ᵢ = X₀. If short selling is allowed, some X₀ᵢ can be negative.

Write each amount as a fraction of the total: X₀ᵢ = wᵢ X₀. The fractions wᵢ are the weights.

Σ wᵢ = 1 (budget constraint)    wᵢ > 0: long    wᵢ < 0: short    no short sales: wᵢ ≥ 0
Derivation: the portfolio return is the weighted average
Asset i has total return Rᵢ, so the money it returns is Rᵢ X₀ᵢ = Rᵢ wᵢ X₀.
Total money received by the portfolio: Σᵢ Rᵢ wᵢ X₀.
Total return of the portfolio: R = (Σᵢ Rᵢ wᵢ X₀) / X₀ = Σ wᵢ Rᵢ.
Now R = 1 + r and Rᵢ = 1 + rᵢ. So 1 + r = Σ wᵢ(1 + rᵢ) = Σ wᵢ + Σ wᵢ rᵢ = 1 + Σ wᵢ rᵢ.
Cancel the 1: r = Σ wᵢ rᵢ.
Result

Both the total return and the rate of return of a portfolio are the weighted sums of the individual returns. The weights are shares of the purchase cost, not the number of shares.

Part 3: Random variables, covariance and correlation

Next-year returns are uncertain, so we treat each return as a random variable: a quantity that takes one of several values, each with a probability. This part collects the statistics needed to describe a portfolio.

3.1 Mean and variance

Definitions

Let x take values x₁,…,xₙ with probabilities p₁,…,pₙ.

Mean: x̄ = E(x) = Σ xᵢ pᵢ
Variance: σ² = var(x) = E[(x − x̄)²] = E(x²) − x̄²

Standard deviation σ = √variance, the typical size of the surprise.

3.2 Covariance and correlation

Covariance
σ₁₂ = cov(x₁, x₂) = E[(x₁ − x̄₁)(x₂ − x̄₂)] = E(x₁x₂) − x̄₁x̄₂

Note cov(x, x) = var(x). It measures whether the two variables tend to be above their means together.

Derivation: variance of a sum
var(x + y) = E[((x − x̄) + (y − ȳ))²]
Expand the square: E[(x − x̄)²] + E[(y − ȳ)²] + 2E[(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)]
var(x + y) = σₓ² + σᵧ² + 2σₓᵧ. The cross term is what makes diversification possible.
Correlation coefficient
ρ₁₂ = σ₁₂ / (σ₁ σ₂)     always −1 ≤ ρ ≤ +1
ValueNameMeaning
ρ = +1perfectly correlatedmove exactly together (σ₁₂ = σ₁σ₂)
0 < ρ < 1positively correlatedif one is above its mean, the other likely is too
ρ = 0uncorrelatedσ₁₂ = 0, no linear link
−1 < ρ < 0negatively correlatedone tends to be high when the other is low
ρ = −1perfect negativemove exactly opposite (σ₁₂ = −σ₁σ₂)
Why the bound |σ₁₂| ≤ σ₁σ₂ holds
Variance can never be negative. Take the variable x₁/σ₁ + x₂/σ₂.
Its variance is 1 + 1 + 2σ₁₂/(σ₁σ₂) = 2 + 2ρ ≥ 0, so ρ ≥ −1.
Now take x₁/σ₁ − x₂/σ₂. Its variance is 2 − 2ρ ≥ 0, so ρ ≤ +1.

3.3 Returns as random variables

When you buy an asset its rate of return r is random. We summarise it with its mean r̄, its variance σ² and its covariances with other assets.

Example 3 (slides): a die decides the payoff

You put in 1 unit. A fair die is rolled and the unit becomes the payoff Q: face 1 → 0, face 2 → 4, face 3 → −1, face 4 → 2, face 5 → −1, face 6 → 3. Each face has probability 1/6.

Mean: Q̄ = (0 + 4 − 1 + 2 − 1 + 3)/6 = 7/6 ≈ 1.167.
E(Q²) = (0 + 16 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 9)/6 = 31/6.
Variance: σ² = E(Q²) − Q̄² = 31/6 − 49/36 = 186/36 − 49/36 = 137/36 ≈ 3.81.
Since the investment is 1 unit, Q equals the total return R. The rate of return is r = R − 1, so r̄ = 1/6.
Subtracting a constant (the 1) shifts the mean but does not change the variance, so σ²ᵣ is still ≈ 3.81.

3.4 Mean and variance of a portfolio

Take n assets with rates of return r₁,…,rₙ, means r̄ᵢ and weights wᵢ. The portfolio rate is r = Σ wᵢ rᵢ.

Derivation of the portfolio mean and variance
Mean. Expectation is linear: E(r) = E(Σ wᵢ rᵢ) = Σ wᵢ r̄ᵢ.
Variance. σ² = E[(r − r̄)²] = E[(Σᵢ wᵢ rᵢ − Σᵢ wᵢ r̄ᵢ)²] = E[(Σᵢ wᵢ(rᵢ − r̄ᵢ))²].
Square the sum: (Σᵢ aᵢ)² = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢ aⱼ with aᵢ = wᵢ(rᵢ − r̄ᵢ).
So σ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ wᵢ wⱼ E[(rᵢ − r̄ᵢ)(rⱼ − r̄ⱼ)] = Σᵢⱼ wᵢ wⱼ σᵢⱼ.
Split the double sum into i = j (variances) and i ≠ j (covariances): σ² = Σ wᵢ²σᵢ² + Σi≠j wᵢwⱼσᵢⱼ.
For two assets this is w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁₂.

Part 4: Diversification

Diversification means adding more assets to a portfolio so its variance falls. Fewer assets means more exposure to one bad outcome.

4.1 Case 1: uncorrelated assets

Assume many assets, all mutually uncorrelated, each with mean m and variance σ². Hold equal shares in n of them: wᵢ = 1/n.

Derivation
Portfolio return: r = (1/n) Σ rᵢ.
Mean: r̄ = (1/n)(n m) = m. The mean does not depend on n.
Variance: every covariance is 0, so only the n variance terms remain: var(r) = Σ (1/n)² σ² = n · σ²/n² = σ²/n.
Standard deviation = σ/√n. As n grows the variance goes to 0.

4.2 Case 2: positively correlated assets

Now each pair has covariance cov(rᵢ, rⱼ) = 0.5σ² for i ≠ j (so the correlation is 0.5). Equal weights 1/n again.

Derivation
var(r) = (1/n²) [ Σi=j σᵢⱼ + Σi≠j σᵢⱼ ].
There are n terms with i = j (each σ²) and n² − n terms with i ≠ j (each 0.5σ²).
var(r) = (1/n²) [ nσ² + 0.5(n² − n)σ² ] = σ²/n + 0.5σ²(1 − 1/n).
Multiply out: σ²/n + 0.5σ² − 0.5σ²/n = 0.5σ² + 0.5σ²/n.
As n → ∞ the second term vanishes but the first stays: variance never falls below 0.5σ².

That floor is the shared (common) risk. Adding assets removes only the risk that is specific to each asset, never the part all assets share.

Extra: any correlation ρ (same steps)

If each pair has correlation ρ, then cov = ρσ² and var(r) = σ²/n + ρσ²(1 − 1/n) = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/n. For ρ = 0 it is σ²/n, for ρ = 0.5 it is the formula above, and as n → ∞ the floor is ρσ². This is the “average covariance” limit in the summary’s self-test.

4.3 Lessons and a caution

  1. If returns are uncorrelated, taking n large reduces portfolio variance essentially to zero.
  2. If returns are positively correlated, it is harder, and there is a lower limit.
  3. Here all assets had the same mean, so diversifying cost nothing in expected return. In general diversifying can lower the mean as well as the variance, so we need a method that weighs both. That is the Markowitz model (Part 6).

Part 5: The feasible set

5.1 Plotting assets and portfolios

Each asset is a point on the mean–standard deviation diagram: σ across, E up. Any weights w₁,…,wₙ with Σwᵢ = 1 give a portfolio, which is another point. The set of all such points is the feasible set (or feasible region).

5.2 Two assets

Let w₁ = 1 − α and w₂ = α. As α moves from 0 to 1 you slide from “all asset 1” to “all asset 2”. Values outside 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 mean short selling one of the assets.

Mean: r̄(α) = (1 − α) r̄₁ + α r̄₂
σ(α) = √[ (1 − α)²σ₁² + α²σ₂² + 2α(1 − α)σ₁₂ ] = √[ (1 − α)²σ₁² + α²σ₂² + 2α(1 − α)ρ₁₂σ₁σ₂ ]

The mean is linear in α. The risk depends on ρ. Because −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1 we can find the two extreme shapes.

Upper bound: ρ = +1 (move together)
σ² = (1 − α)²σ₁² + α²σ₂² + 2α(1 − α)σ₁σ₂, which is a perfect square: [(1 − α)σ₁ + ασ₂]².
So σ(α) = (1 − α)σ₁ + ασ₂ (for 0 ≤ α ≤ 1).
Both σ and the mean are linear in α, so the portfolios lie on the straight line joining the two assets. No diversification gain: risk is just the weighted average of risks.
Lower bound: ρ = −1 (move opposite)
σ² = (1 − α)²σ₁² + α²σ₂² − 2α(1 − α)σ₁σ₂ = [(1 − α)σ₁ − ασ₂]².
So σ(α) = |(1 − α)σ₁ − ασ₂|. For small α this is (1 − α)σ₁ − ασ₂; for large α it is −(1 − α)σ₁ + ασ₂.
The two pieces meet where the inside is zero: (1 − α)σ₁ = ασ₂ ⇒ σ₁ = α(σ₁ + σ₂) ⇒ α* = σ₁ / (σ₁ + σ₂).
At α* the risk is exactly 0. The feasible set is two straight lines forming a V that touches the vertical axis.
Zero-variance portfolio (ρ = −1)

If two risky assets are perfectly negatively correlated, a mix with weight σ₁/(σ₁+σ₂) on asset 2 (and σ₂/(σ₁+σ₂) on asset 1) has no risk at all: a synthetic risk-free asset. For any other correlation in (−1, 1) the risk never reaches zero inside 0 ≤ α ≤ 1.

5.6 Minimum-variance set, GMV and efficient frontier

Definitions
  • Minimum-variance set: the left boundary of the feasible region. For each mean it holds the feasible point with the smallest σ.
  • Global minimum variance (GMV) portfolio: the point on that boundary with the smallest σ of all (the leftmost point of the region).
  • Efficient frontier: the upper part of the minimum-variance set (from GMV upward).
Why only the upper-left edge matters
Horizontal line (same mean, different σ): a risk-averse investor picks the leftmost point, the smallest σ. So only the left boundary is interesting.
Vertical line (same σ, different means): investors prefer more money (nonsatiation: everything else equal, more is better). So they pick the highest point.
Combine: risk-averse + nonsatiation ⇒ only the upper part of the left boundary survives. Portfolios on it are efficient: no other feasible portfolio has more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return.
Why the lower branch is useless

A point on the lower branch has the same σ as a point on the upper branch but a lower mean, so everyone prefers the upper one. In the summary: the efficient branch gives higher expected return for the same standard deviation.

Part 6: The Markowitz model

6.1 The problem

There are n assets with means r̄ᵢ and covariances σᵢⱼ. For a target mean r̄ (call it R̄), find the weights with the smallest variance that reach that mean.

Markowitz minimum-variance problem
min over w₁,…,wₙ:   ½ Σᵢ Σⱼ wᵢ wⱼ σᵢⱼ
subject to   Σ wᵢ r̄ᵢ = R̄   (target return)    and    Σ wᵢ = 1   (budget)

The ½ is for tidy derivatives. Minimising ½σ² gives the same weights as minimising σ².

6.2 Lagrangian and first-order conditions

Two constraints, so two multipliers: λ for the return constraint and μ for the budget constraint.

L = ½ Σᵢⱼ wᵢwⱼσᵢⱼ − λ [ Σ wᵢ r̄ᵢ − R̄ ] − μ [ Σ wᵢ − 1 ]
Derivation of the first-order conditions
Differentiate L with respect to one weight wk. In the double sum, wk appears in the terms with i = k and with j = k. Because σᵢⱼ = σⱼᵢ these two give equal contributions and cancel the ½.
∂/∂wk of ½ΣΣ wᵢwⱼσᵢⱼ = Σⱼ σkj wⱼ.
∂/∂wk of the return term = −λ r̄k, of the budget term = −μ.
Set to zero: Σⱼ σkj wⱼ − λ r̄k − μ = 0 for k = 1,…,n.

Check with n = 2 (slides). L = ½(w₁²σ₁² + 2w₁w₂σ₁₂ + w₂²σ₂²) − λ(w₁r̄₁ + w₂r̄₂ − R̄) − μ(w₁ + w₂ − 1). Then

∂L/∂w₁ = 0 ⇒ σ₁²w₁ + σ₁₂w₂ − λr̄₁ − μ = 0     ∂L/∂w₂ = 0 ⇒ σ₂²w₂ + σ₁₂w₁ − λr̄₂ − μ = 0

Together with the two constraints that is 4 equations for the 4 unknowns w₁, w₂, λ, μ.

General result: n + 2 linear equations in n + 2 unknowns
  1. Σⱼ σᵢⱼ wⱼ − λ r̄ᵢ − μ = 0, for i = 1,…,n (n equations)
  2. Σ wᵢ r̄ᵢ = R̄
  3. Σ wᵢ = 1

Because they are linear, a computer solves them instantly. (Short sales are allowed here: nothing forces wᵢ ≥ 0.)

6.3 Worked example 6: three uncorrelated assets

Part 7: One risk-free asset, the CAL and Tobin separation

This part comes from the lecture summary (W7_L5). The idea of the capital allocation line was introduced in Week 6; here it is tied to the efficient frontier.

7.1 Mixing a risk-free asset with a risky portfolio

Let a risky portfolio P have mean EP and standard deviation σP, and a risk-free asset pay Rf with σ = 0. Put a fraction y in P and 1 − y in the risk-free asset.

Derivation of the CAL
Mean: Ec = (1 − y)Rf + yEP = Rf + y(EP − Rf).
Variance: σc² = (1−y)²·0 + y²σP² + 2y(1−y)·0 = y²σP² (a constant has zero variance and zero covariance with anything).
So σc = yσP and y = σc/σP.
Substitute y into the mean: Ec = Rf + [(EP − Rf)/σP] σc. This is a straight line, the Capital Allocation Line (CAL).
Sharpe ratio
SP = (EP − Rf) / σP

The slope of the CAL: extra return per unit of risk. If y > 1 you borrow at Rf to invest more than your wealth in P. If y < 0 you short P.

7.2 How much to hold: choosing y

Maximise mean-variance utility along the CAL
U(y) = Ec − ½Aσc² = Rf + y(EP − Rf) − ½A y²σP².
dU/dy = (EP − Rf) − A y σP² = 0.
y* = (EP − Rf) / (A σP²). (Second derivative −AσP² < 0, so it is a maximum.)
Plugging back: U* = Rf + (EP − Rf)² / (2AσP²).
Extra: back to Part 1 (X is risk-free at 5%, Y has 14% and 12%)

Now suppose the person may mix them. Excess return = 9%, σ² = 0.0144.

  • A = 2: y* = 0.09/(2 × 0.0144) = 3.125. She borrows 212.5% of her wealth to buy Y.
  • A = 12.5 (the person who was indifferent in 1.6): y* = 0.09/(12.5 × 0.0144) = 0.5. Utility = 0.05 + 0.5(0.09) − ½(12.5)(0.25)(0.0144) = 0.0725 = 7.25%, which beats both pure X (5%) and pure Y (5%).

Moral: when mixing is allowed, nearly everyone holds some of the risky asset. The all-or-nothing question in Part 1 was a simplification.

7.3 The tangency portfolio

The CAL is steeper when P has a higher Sharpe ratio, so we want the risky portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio. On the diagram that is the point where a line from Rf just touches the efficient frontier: the tangency portfolio T.

Extra: tangency for Example 6 with Rf = 5%

Maximising the Sharpe ratio gives weights proportional to Σ⁻¹(r̄ − Rf). Here Σ is the identity, so wᵢ ∝ r̄ᵢ − Rf = (0.05, 0.15, 0.25). Divide by their sum 0.45: w = (1/9, 1/3, 5/9).

ET = 0.1/9 + 0.2/3 + 0.3(5/9) = 0.2444. σT = √(1 + 9 + 25)/9 = 0.6573. Sharpe = (0.2444 − 0.05)/0.6573 = 0.296. Compare the GMV portfolio: (0.2 − 0.05)/0.577 = 0.260. T has the better ratio. (A grid search over the whole frontier confirms T is the maximum.)

7.4 Tobin’s two-fund separation theorem

Theorem

The investment decision splits into two independent steps:

  1. Technical step (same for everyone). Using only prices, means, variances and covariances, find the single tangency portfolio T that maximises the Sharpe ratio.
  2. Personal step. Using your own risk aversion A, split money between the risk-free asset and T with y* = (ET − Rf)/(AσT²).

If the risk-free rate rises (essay topic 5)

The CAL starts higher on the vertical axis. The tangent point moves to a different place on the frontier (generally higher return and higher risk), and the new line has a different slope. The frontier itself does not change.

Question 4: n uncorrelated assets with the same mean

4A. Picture. Same mean means all assets lie on one horizontal line in the (σ, E) diagram. Every mix has the same mean, so the feasible set stays on that line. Mixing lowers σ below that of every asset, so the efficient set is a single point, the GMV, to the left of the leftmost asset.

4B. GMV weights
Minimise Σ wᵢ²σᵢ² subject to Σ wᵢ = 1. L = Σ wᵢ²σᵢ² − λ(Σ wᵢ − 1).
∂L/∂wᵢ = 2wᵢσᵢ² − λ = 0 ⇒ wᵢ = λ/(2σᵢ²).
Sum to 1: (λ/2) Σ 1/σₖ² = 1 ⇒ λ/2 = 1/Σ(1/σₖ²).
wᵢ = (1/σᵢ²) / Σₖ(1/σₖ²): weight inversely proportional to variance.

It goes to 0 (σ²/n).
7. What happens as n → ∞ with positive average covariance?
It approaches the average covariance (ρσ², which is 0.5σ² in the slides).
8. Define GMV.
The portfolio on the minimum-variance set with the smallest standard deviation of all feasible portfolios.
9. Why is the upper branch called the efficient frontier?
For the same σ it offers a higher mean than the lower branch, so it is the best risk-return trade-off.
10. Weights for n uncorrelated assets with identical variance σ²?
Equal weights 1/n (follows from wi ∝ 1/σi²).

8.4 Essay questions from the summary: answer outlines

  1. Short-sale constraints and the feasible set. With short sales allowed the weights can be any real numbers, so the two-asset curve continues beyond both end-points and the three-asset region becomes unbounded to the upper right (no upper limit on mean or σ). With wᵢ ≥ 0 the set is bounded: it is the region spanned by the assets themselves (a closed “cloud” whose corners are the assets) and many optimal weights are exactly zero.
  2. Convexity of σ(α) for ρ ∈ (−1, 1). View each centred return as a vector in a space where the length is the standard deviation. Then σ(α) = length of (1 − α)u + αv, where u, v are the centred returns. Length obeys the triangle inequality, so σ(tα + (1 − t)β) ≤ tσ(α) + (1 − t)σ(β). The inequality is strict unless u and v point along the same line, which happens only when ρ = ±1. So σ is strictly convex for |ρ| < 1, and the curve in the (σ, E) plane bends to the left of the straight line.
  3. Tobin and index funds. Everyone holds the same risky mix T (the market-like fund) plus cash. So one low-cost fund can serve all investors, and personal risk appetite is handled only by the cash split.
  4. Diversifiable vs systematic risk. With equal weights, var = σ²/n + ρσ²(1 − 1/n) → ρσ² (the average covariance). The part that disappears (σ²(1 − ρ)/n) is firm-specific risk. The part that remains (the average covariance) is market-wide risk: it cannot be diversified away. (This is the idiosyncratic vs systematic split from Week 6.)
  5. Higher risk-free rate. The CAL starts higher. The frontier is unchanged, but the tangent point shifts, and the Sharpe ratio of T generally falls because the numerator ET − Rf shrinks for a given T. Investors move along the new CAL according to their A.
