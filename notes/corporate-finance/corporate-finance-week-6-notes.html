How to read these notes

Week 5 told you how to value uncertain payoffs. Week 6 asks a different question: how does a person actually choose? Every answer in this week follows one recipe:

The recipe (used four times this week)
  1. What can the person afford? (a budget constraint)
  2. What does the person like? (a utility function and its indifference curves)
  3. Pick the best affordable option. At the best point the indifference curve just touches the budget line (tangency).

Part 1 does this with ordinary goods (no uncertainty). Part 2 does it with state-contingent goods, which gives insurance. Part 3 does it with a risk-free asset plus a risky one. Part 4 finds the price of risk and ends with the famous CAPM equation.

Colour code: blue definitions, yellow worked examples, purple derivations, red warnings, green key results. Practice problems with solutions, a revision sheet and a note on where the sources disagree are at the end.

Part 1 · Choice under certainty

First the simple world with no uncertainty. A person has some money, goods have prices, and the person wants the best bundle he can afford.

1.1 Budget set and budget line

Definitions
  • A consumption bundle (x1, x2) is x1 units of good 1 and x2 units of good 2.
  • Prices are p1 and p2. Income is m.
  • The budget set is every bundle the person can afford: p1x1 + p2x2 ≤ m.
  • The budget line is its edge, where all the money is spent: p1x1 + p2x2 = m (with x1, x2 ≥ 0).
Reading the budget line (derivation)
Solve p1x1 + p2x2 = m for x2:
x2 = m/p2 − (p1/p2) x1
Vertical intercept (x1 = 0): x2 = m/p2. This is the most of good 2 you can buy.
Horizontal intercept (x2 = 0): x1 = m/p1. This is the most of good 1 you can buy.
Slope = −p1/p2.

Meaning of the slope: to get one more unit of good 1 you must give up p1/p2 units of good 2. It is the market's exchange rate between the two goods, or the opportunity cost of good 1 in units of good 2.

1.2 Example: Rajesh (Question 1 in the slides)

Rajesh has income 40. Commodity 1 costs 10 a unit and commodity 2 costs 5 a unit.

Slope−2−p₁/p₂ = −10/5. Two units of good 2 are given up for one more unit of good 1

Two rules to remember
  • A price change pivots the line. The intercept for the good whose price did not change stays put. A fall in a price makes the budget set bigger.
  • An income change shifts the line in parallel, because the prices (and so the slope) have not changed.

1.3 Preferences: utility, marginal utility, indifference curves

Definitions
  • A utility function U(x1, x2) gives each bundle a number so that a bundle with a bigger number is preferred. Only the order of the numbers matters.
  • Marginal utility of a good is the extra utility from one more unit: MU1 = ∂U/∂x1, MU2 = ∂U/∂x2.
  • An indifference curve joins all bundles with the same utility, U(x1, x2) = Ū. It answers: "which other bundles would the person find just as good?"
  • The slope of an indifference curve is the marginal rate of substitution (MRS).
Derivation of the MRS
Along an indifference curve utility does not change: U(x1, x2) = Ū, so dŪ = 0.
Take the total differential: (∂U/∂x1) dx1 + (∂U/∂x2) dx2 = 0
Rearrange: MU1 dx1 = −MU2 dx2
MRS = dx2/dx1 = − MU1 / MU2

Meaning: the most units of good 2 the person would give up to get one more unit of good 1 and still be just as happy. (More of good 1 raises utility by MU1, so good 2 must fall by enough to lose MU1/MU2 units.)

Sign convention

The slides define the MRS as the slope, which is negative. Some books (and the lecture summary) define it as the absolute value MU1/MU2. Both mean the same thing. In these notes the MRS is the negative slope, as in the slides and as used in your assignment (for example −4/9).

Example 1: Rajesh, U = x₁x₂, the curve through (5, 4)
U(5, 4) = 5 × 4 = 20. The indifference curve is every (x1, x2) with x1x2 = 20, i.e. x2 = 20/x1.
MU1 = ∂(x1x2)/∂x1 = x2 and MU2 = x1.
MRS = −x2/x1  ⟹  at (5, 4): MRS = −4/5

At (5, 4) Rajesh is willing to give up at most 4/5 of a unit of good 2 for one more unit of good 1. Check with the curve x2 = 20/x1: its slope is −20/x12 = −20/25 = −4/5 ✓.

Question 2: Shalu, U = 4√x₁ + x₂

(a) At (36, 14) utility is 4√36 + 14 = 24 + 14 = 38. If good 1 falls to 25 she needs y with 4√25 + y = 38, so 20 + y = 38 and y = 18.

(b) MU1 = 4 × ½x1−½ = 2/√x1 and MU2 = 1.

MRS = −(2/√x1) / 1 = −2/√x1

For example at x1 = 36 it is −1/3, and at x1 = 25 it is −2/5. The curve gets flatter as she holds more of good 1: she values extra units of it less and less.

1.4 Choice: the best affordable bundle

Combine the two ideas. The person picks the bundle she likes most from those she can afford:

max U(x1, x2)   subject to   p1x1 + p2x2 ≤ m

(We assume well-behaved preferences and an interior answer where both x1 and x2 are positive.) The method is a Lagrangian.

Derivation of the tangency condition
Lagrangian: L = U(x1, x2) + λ[m − p1x1 − p2x2]
∂L/∂x1 = 0 ⟹ MU1 = λp1
∂L/∂x2 = 0 ⟹ MU2 = λp2
∂L/∂λ = 0 ⟹ p1x1 + p2x2 = m
Divide the first by the second: λ cancels.
MU1/MU2 = p1/p2  ⟺  slope of indifference curve = slope of budget line

This is the tangency condition. The third equation then pins down the actual numbers.

Why is tangency the right answer? (intuition)

The MRS is the personal exchange rate (what she is willing to give). The price ratio is the market exchange rate (what she must give). Suppose MRS (as a size) is 0.8 but the market asks only 0.5 units of good 2 for a unit of good 1. Then she would happily pay 0.8 but only has to pay 0.5, so she should buy more good 1. As she does, extra good 1 becomes less valuable to her and the MRS falls. She stops exactly when the two rates are equal. Also, from the first two conditions MU1/p1 = MU2/p2 = λ: the last rupee must give the same extra utility on every good, otherwise she could do better by moving money.

Example 2: Rajesh, U = x₁x₂, p₁ = 1, p₂ = 5, m = 300
MU1 = x2, MU2 = x1. Tangency: x2/x1 = 1/5, so x1 = 5x2.
Budget: x1 + 5x2 = 300 ⟹ 5x2 + 5x2 = 300 ⟹ 10x2 = 300.
x2* = 30,   x1* = 150

Check: 150 + 5 × 30 = 300 ✓. Utility = 150 × 30 = 4,500.

A neat pattern for U = x₁x₂ (extra)

Tangency gives p1x1 = p2x2: she spends half of her income on each good. So x1 = m/(2p1) and x2 = m/(2p2). Check: 300/2 = 150 and 300/10 = 30 ✓. This shortcut works for any problem with U = x1x2 (for example 1000 units, prices 50 and 100 give (10, 5)).

Question 3: Shalu, U = 4√x₁ + x₂, p₁ = 1, p₂ = 2, m = 24
MU1/MU2 = (2/√x1)/1 = p1/p2 = 1/2
2/√x1 = 1/2 ⟹ √x1 = 4 ⟹ x1 = 16
Budget: 16 + 2x2 = 24 ⟹ x2 = 4
Optimal bundle (16, 4), utility = 4 × 4 + 4 = 20

Notice that her choice of good 1 does not depend on income here. (This is a "quasilinear" utility function: extra income all goes to good 2.)

1.5 Choosing across time (Question 4)

Treat consumption in different periods as different goods. From Week 2 the budget constraint is the present-value line:

c1 + c2/(1 + r) = m1 + m2/(1 + r)

This is the same as the two-good problem with p1 = 1 (consumption now), p2 = 1/(1 + r) (price of next period's consumption in today's money) and income m = PV of all income.

Rajesh: U = c₁c₂, r = 10%, income 100 now and 121 next period
PV of income = 100 + 121/1.1 = 100 + 110 = 210. Budget: c1 + c2/1.1 = 210.
Tangency: MU1/MU2 = c2/c1 = p1/p2 = 1.1, so c2 = 1.1c1.
Budget: c1 + 1.1c1/1.1 = 2c1 = 210.
c1* = 105,   c2* = 115.5

He earns 100 now but spends 105 (borrows 5). Next period he earns 121 but spends 115.5, repaying 5 × 1.1 = 5.5 ✓. The MRS equals 1 + r: one rupee today is worth 1.1 rupees next period at the optimum.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Dividing the wrong way: tangency is MU1/MU2 = p1/p2 (good 1 on top on both sides).
  • Forgetting to use the budget line after tangency: tangency gives only the ratio; the budget gives the level.
  • In the time problem the price of period-2 consumption is 1/(1 + r), not (1 + r).

Part 2 · Choice under uncertainty

Now the best-choice recipe is applied to uncertain outcomes. We first need new "goods".

2.1 Contingent commodities and the expected utility hypothesis

Key ideas
  • Utility is defined over goods, U(x1, x2). In time problems, the same good on different dates counts as different goods, U(c1, c2).
  • With uncertainty we create contingent commodities: "money if event A happens" and "money if event B happens". In the insurance example the events are flood / no flood (or accident / no accident).
  • The state-contingent budget constraint (derived in Week 5) lists the contingent bundles that are affordable.
  • Expected utility hypothesis: with two events of probabilities π1, π2 and consumptions c1, c2, utility has the form U(c1, c2) = π1u(c1) + π2u(c2).

A person's attitude to gambles and insurance is then decided by how he feels about different combinations of contingent goods, and he picks the best affordable combination, exactly as in Part 1. A concave u (such as √c) means risk averse (see Week 5).

2.2 Question 1: Samson's factory and flood insurance

Samson will sell his factory next year to retire. If there is no flood it sells for 500,000. If there is a flood it sells for only 50,000. Flood insurance costs 0.10 per 1 unit of cover. The chance of a flood is 1/10. His expected utility is U(cF, cNF) = 0.1√cF + 0.9√cNF.

Part 1: no insurance

In each state he consumes the factory's value: (cF, cNF) = (50,000, 500,000). This is his starting point (the "endowment").

Part 2: with insurance paying x on a flood

The premium 0.1x is paid whether or not there is a flood.

Flood: cF = 50,000 − 0.1x + x = 50,000 + 0.9x
No flood: cNF = 500,000 − 0.1x
Part 3: derive the state-contingent budget constraint
From the no-flood equation: x = (500,000 − cNF)/0.1 = 5,000,000 − 10cNF
Put into the flood equation: cF = 50,000 + 0.9(5,000,000 − 10cNF) = 50,000 + 4,500,000 − 9cNF
So cF + 9cNF = 4,550,000
Divide by 10:
0.1cF + 0.9cNF = 455,000

Interpretation: the coefficients are the flood and no-flood probabilities. The line says the probability-weighted consumption is fixed at 455,000 (the factory's expected value is 0.1 × 50,000 + 0.9 × 500,000 = 455,000, so insurance at the fair price just reshuffles consumption between states). The slope is −0.1/0.9 = −1/9.

Part 4 and 5: the MRS and the optimum
MUF = 0.1 × 1/(2√cF),   MUNF = 0.9 × 1/(2√cNF)
MRS = −MUF/MUNF = −(1/9) · √cNF / √cF
Tangency: MRS = slope of budget line: (1/9)√cNF/√cF = 0.1/0.9 = 1/9
Cancel the 1/9: √cNF = √cF, so cNF/cF = 1

He wants the same consumption in both states, i.e. no risk at all.

Part 6: how much insurance?
Put cF = cNF in the budget line: (0.1 + 0.9)c = 455,000 ⟹ cF = cNF = 455,000
No-flood: 500,000 − 0.1x = 455,000 ⟹ 0.1x = 45,000 ⟹ x = 450,000
Premium = 0.1 × 450,000 = 45,000

Check the flood state: 50,000 + 0.9 × 450,000 = 455,000 ✓. His expected utility rises from 0.1√50,000 + 0.9√500,000 = 22.4 + 636.4 = 658.8 (no insurance) to √455,000 = 674.5.

Why full insurance? (the theorem again)

The price of cover (0.10) equals the probability of a flood (0.10): insurance is actuarially fair. A risk-averse person facing a fair price buys full insurance, so consumption is the same in every state. The insurance premium of 45,000 is also exactly the expected payout 0.1 × 450,000 = 45,000.

2.3 Question 2: Rishabh, the most he would pay for insurance

Rishabh has u(x) = √x. If he stays fit he gets a 1,000,000 cricket contract. If a career-ending injury happens (probability 10%) he takes a 10,000 job. A policy would pay him 1,000,000 − 10,000 on injury, so he would be sure to have 1,000,000 − p after paying price p.

Solution
Expected utility without insurance: 0.1√10,000 + 0.9√1,000,000 = 0.1(100) + 0.9(1,000) = 910
With insurance his utility is √(1,000,000 − p). He pays up to the price where he is just indifferent:
√(1,000,000 − p) = 910  ⟹  1,000,000 − p = 910² = 828,100  ⟹  pmax = 171,900
What this number means (link to certainty equivalent)

The sure amount 828,100 is his certainty equivalent: it gives him the same happiness as his risky prospect. His expected wealth is 0.1 × 10,000 + 0.9 × 1,000,000 = 901,000. A fair insurer would charge 0.1 × 990,000 = 99,000. But he would pay up to 171,900. The extra 72,900 = 901,000 − 828,100 is his risk premium (see 2.6). That gap between what he would pay and what it costs is why insurance exists.

2.4 Question 3: government insurance and risk taking

Investors have u = √x and each puts 100 into a fund. The manager can invest each 100 in Lottery 1 (64 or 225, each with probability 1/2) or Lottery 2 (0 with probability 3/4, 1,024 with probability 1/4). The manager always picks what gives investors the highest expected utility.

Part 1: no insurance
EU(L1) = ½√64 + ½√225 = ½(8) + ½(15) = 11.5
EU(L2) = ¼√1,024 + ¾√0 = ¼(32) = 8

Lottery 1 wins. Consuming the 100 for sure would give √100 = 10, which is above Lottery 2 (8) but below Lottery 1 (11.5). So a risk-averse investor would not even buy Lottery 2.

Part 2: government reimburses any loss up to 36

A "loss" means getting back less than the 100 invested. Lottery 1's bad payoff 64 is a loss of 36, fully repaid, so it becomes 100. Lottery 2's bad payoff 0 is a loss of 100, but only 36 is repaid, so it becomes 36.

EU(L1) = ½√100 + ½√225 = 5 + 7.5 = 12.5
EU(L2) = ¼√1,024 + ¾√36 = 8 + 4.5 = 12.5

The manager is now indifferent. Investors are better off than before (12.5 > 11.5), but the insurance has made the risky lottery just as attractive as the safe one. This is a distortion of risk-taking: someone else bears the downside.

Part 3: charge the actuarially fair premium

A fair premium P equals the expected payout, since it is charged in every state.

Lottery 1: expected payout = ½(36) + ½(0) = 18, so P = 18.
Lottery 2: expected payout = ¾(36) + ¼(0) = 27, so P = 27.
EU(L1) = ½√(100 − 18) + ½√(225 − 18) = ½(9.055) + ½(14.387) ≈ 11.72
EU(L2) = ¼√(1,024 − 27) + ¾√(36 − 27) = ¼(31.575) + ¾(3) ≈ 10.14

Now the safe lottery wins again. The riskier the lottery, the bigger the premium, so fair pricing makes investors pay for the risk they take and restores sensible choices.

2.5 Question 4: moral hazard and deductibles

Moral hazard

Moral hazard arises when a person's hidden action changes the chance or size of a loss and the insurer cannot see it. Once she is insured, she has less reason to take care.

An agent has income w = 10,000, an accident costs L = 6,400, and u = √w.

Part 1: expected utility with a 50% chance of loss
EU = ½√10,000 + ½√3,600 = ½(100) + ½(60) = 80
Part 2: would she pay 1,900 to cut the loss chance to 1/10?

The 1,900 is paid in both states, so she has 8,100 (no loss) or 3,600 − 1,900 = 1,700 (loss).

EU = 0.9√8,100 + 0.1√1,700 = 0.9(90) + 0.1(41.23) = 81 + 4.12 = 85.12

85.12 > 80, so yes, she takes the precaution.

Part 3: insurance priced as if she takes the precaution
A break-even (risk-neutral) insurer pays C on a loss that has probability 1/10, so the premium is P = C/10 per unit.
Buying z units: x1 = w − Pz (no loss), x2 = w − L − Pz + Cz (loss).
Eliminate z (the same steps as Samson): 0.9x1 + 0.1x2 = 7,460. [Check: after the precaution she has 8,100; the average loss is 0.1 × 6,400 = 640; 8,100 − 640 = 7,460.]
Maximise 0.9√x1 + 0.1√x2 subject to this. The MRS is −(0.9/0.1)·√x2/√x1 and the budget slope is −0.9/0.1. Setting them equal gives √x2 = √x1. Put x1 = x2 in the budget: x1 = x2 = 7,460.
Full cover C = 6,400 costs P = 640. Her expected utility is √7,460 ≈ 86.37.
Part 4: the insurer cannot see whether she took the precaution

The insurer prices the policy at 640 for full cover, assuming the low risk. If she buys it, she must choose whether to spend 1,900:

ChoiceHer consumptionExpected utility
Insured and takes precaution8,100 − 640 = 7,460 in every state√7,460 = 86.37
Insured and skips precaution10,000 − 640 = 9,360 in every state (the loss is repaid)√9,360 = 96.75

96.75 > 86.37. Because she is fully insured, a loss costs her nothing, so the 1,900 buys her nothing. She skips it. Then the true loss probability is 1/2 and the insurer's expected result is 640 − ½(6,400) = −2,560. The insurer will not offer this policy.

Part 5: why deductibles?

Unobservable precautions (locking windows, not smoking, testing fire alarms) change the probability of loss. A deductible (or co-payment) caps what the insurer pays, so the insured keeps some of the loss. She then still has a reason to take care, which keeps the number and size of claims down. The price of this is that she does not get complete protection from risk. Insurers must trade off risk sharing (more cover) against incentives (less cover).

2.6 Question 5: certainty equivalent and risk premium

Definitions
  • Certainty equivalent (CE): the sure amount that gives the same expected utility as a risky lottery: u(CE) = E[U], so CE = u−1(E[U]).
  • Risk premium (RP): the expected wealth a person would give up to get rid of the risk: RP = E[W] − CE.
Amar: u(x) = √x, π = 0.5, lottery pays x if event 1 and y otherwise

(a) A lottery paying 10,000 or 100 with equal chance: utility = ½√10,000 + ½√100 = 50 + 5 = 55.

(b) A sure 4,900 gives √4,900 = 70. Since 70 > 55 he prefers the sure 4,900 to the lottery, even though the lottery's average payoff is 5,050 (higher).

(c) General formula. Set √CE = ½√x + ½√y. Square both sides:

CE = ( ½√x + ½√y )²

(d) For x = 10,000, y = 100: CE = (50 + 5)² = 55² = 3,025.

(e) E[W] = 0.5(10,000) + 0.5(100) = 5,050, so RP = 5,050 − 3,025 = 2,025.

Reading the picture

On a concave curve the midpoint of the chord (expected utility 55) is always below the curve at the average wealth (utility 71.1). To find the sure wealth with utility 55, go across to the curve and down: that is 3,025. The horizontal gap from 3,025 to the average 5,050 is the risk premium. The more bent the curve, the bigger the premium.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Square at the end for √ utility: CE = (EU)², not √EU.
  • Do not compare expected wealth to expected utility. Compare utilities with utilities (55 vs 70) or wealth with wealth (3,025 vs 4,900).
  • Premium paid is paid in every state, including the good one.

Part 3 · Risky assets: mean, variance and diversification

So far a person's taste for risk was described by the shape of u(c) over all outcomes. A simpler approach: describe each risky asset by just a few numbers, usually its mean and risk, and let utility depend on those.

3.1 The mean-variance model

Mean, variance, standard deviation

If wealth w takes values w1, …, wS with probabilities π1, …, πS (Σπj = 1):

mean μw = E(w) = Σ πjwj    variance σw2 = Σ πj(wj − μw)2    σw = √variance

The mean is the reward; the variance (or SD) measures how much outcomes vary, i.e. the risk.

When is two numbers enough?

The two-number shortcut is exact when returns are normally distributed (a normal distribution is fully described by its mean and variance) or when utility is quadratic. Otherwise it is an approximation. This is the Markowitz mean-variance approach.

The investor's preferences are written U(μ, σ). Two common-sense rules:

  • A higher mean is better: U increases in μ.
  • Less risk is better: U decreases in σ.
Slope of the indifference curve (derivation)
Hold utility fixed: U(μ, σ) = Ū, so dŪ = 0.
(∂U/∂μ) dμ + (∂U/∂σ) dσ = 0
dμ/dσ = − (∂U/∂σ) / (∂U/∂μ)   > 0

Since ∂U/∂σ < 0 and ∂U/∂μ > 0, the slope is positive: to take on more risk the investor must be given more expected return. A steeper curve means more return is needed per unit of extra risk, i.e. a more risk-averse investor. The best direction is north-west (higher return, lower risk).

3.2 Budget constraint: one risk-free and one risky asset

The risk-free asset pays rf for sure. A risky stock pays ms in state s (probability πs), with mean rm = Σ msπs and standard deviation σm. A portfolio puts a fraction x of wealth in the stock and 1 − x in the risk-free asset. For the stock to be bought at all it must pay more on average: rm > rf.

Return and risk of the portfolio (derivation)
Average return: rx = x rm + (1 − x) rf. At x = 0 it is rf; at x = 1 it is rm; it rises with x.
Variance: σx2 = Σ πj (x mj + (1 − x)rf − rx)2
Substitute rx: the term (1 − x)rf cancels, leaving (x mj − x rm) = x (mj − rm)
= x2 Σ πj(mj − rm)2 = x2σm2
So the standard deviation is σx = xσm, giving x = σx/σm.
Substitute x into the return equation: rx = rf + x(rm − rf) =
rx = rf + [(rm − rf)/σm] · σx

This is a straight line in (σ, r) space, called the capital allocation line (CAL) (the "budget line" for risky assets). Its slope (rm − rf)/σm is the price of risk, also called the Sharpe ratio: extra expected return per unit of standard deviation.

Example: John (rf = 10%, risky asset A has mean 25% and σ = 10%)
Weight x in AReturnRisk (σ)

0.40.4 × 25 + 0.6 × 10 = 16%0.4 × 10 = 4%

Slope of the CAL = (25 − 10)/10 = 1.5: every extra 1% of risk earns 1.5% of expected return.

Mistakes to avoid
  • The risk of the portfolio is x times σm, not a weighted average that includes the risk-free asset (its σ is zero).
  • x > 1 means borrowing at the risk-free rate to buy more stock.
  • The CAL slope uses the excess return (rm − rf), not rm.

3.3 Choosing the portfolio

The investor picks the point on the CAL on the highest indifference curve. That point is where the curve just touches the line: the indifference-curve slope equals the CAL slope:

dμ/dσ (indifference curve) = (rm − rf)/σm

A more risk-averse investor (steeper curves) touches the CAL at a smaller x (more risk-free asset); a less averse one chooses a larger x.

A new asset appears: which is better?

Suppose a new risky asset Y has a higher mean (ry > rm) and higher risk (σy > σm). It is not obvious which is preferred. The test is the Sharpe ratio. Assume

(ry − rf)/σy  >  (rm − rf)/σm

Then the CAL through Y is steeper and lies above the old CAL at every risk level. Whatever amount of risk the investor wants, Y mixed with the risk-free asset gives a higher return, so he reaches a higher indifference curve and prefers Y.

Numbers: rf = 4%, M = (mean 10%, σ 8%), Y = (mean 22%, σ 15%)
Sharpe(M) = (10 − 4)/8 = 0.75    Sharpe(Y) = (22 − 4)/15 = 1.2

Y wins. At σ = 8%: mixing M and cash gives 4 + 0.75 × 8 = 10%, mixing Y and cash (x = 8/15) gives 4 + 1.2 × 8 = 13.6%.

John's question (assignment style): asset B has mean 30% and σ = 15%. Sharpe(B) = (30 − 10)/15 = 1.33 < 1.5 = Sharpe(A), so he prefers A even though B pays more.

3.4 How risky is an asset? It depends on correlation

The slides' example

Asset A is worth +10 with probability ½ and −5 with probability ½. Its expected value is ½(10) + ½(−5) = 2.5, so a risk-neutral buyer pays at most 2.5 for it.

Asset B is worth −5 when A is worth 10, and +10 when A is worth −5 (perfect negative correlation). Holding both, you get 10 − 5 = 5 for sure in every state. So the pair is worth 5, double what each is worth alone. B is valuable not because of its own payoff but because it cancels A's risk.

Lessons
  • Assets that move in opposite directions (negative correlation) are very valuable because they reduce overall risk.
  • How much an asset is worth depends far more on how it co-moves with other assets than on its own variation.
  • So the risk of an asset is measured relative to the rest of the market (the idea behind beta in Part 4).

Two-asset portfolios (from the lecture summary)

Weights wA, wB (adding to 1), means RA, RB, standard deviations σA, σB, correlation ρAB (covariance σAB = ρABσAσB):

Portfolio mean and variance
Rp = wARA + wBRB
σp2 = wA2σA2 + wB2σB2 + 2wAwBρABσAσB
The first two terms are each asset's own risk. The last is the interaction, which is positive if the assets move together and negative if they move opposite.
If ρ = 1: σp2 = (wAσA + wBσB)2, so σp equals the plain weighted average. No benefit.
If ρ < 1: the interaction term is smaller, so σp is strictly less than the weighted average. This is the benefit of diversification, and it costs no expected return.
If ρ = −1: σp = |wAσA − wBσB|, which is 0 when wA = σB/(σA + σB).

3.5 Case study: Safina's land (Question 1)

Safina owns Hill Land worth 5,000 with expected profit 500 a year (10%) and standard deviation 100. Profit rises by 100 for every inch of rain above average and falls by 100 for every inch below. She has another 5,000 to buy one more piece of land:

  • L1 (never floods): 500 a year whatever the rain, risk-free.
  • L2 (by the river): expected 500, standard deviation 100, but profit falls 100 per inch above average and rises 100 per inch below. It moves exactly opposite to Hill Land (ρ = −1).

Part 4 · Beta and the CAPM

4.1 Beta: the risk of an asset relative to the market

We have seen that what matters is how an asset moves together with everything else. So we measure the risk of asset i relative to the stock market as a whole. This is its beta:

Definition of beta
βi = (risk of asset i)/(risk of whole market) = cov(ri, rm) / var(rm)

where rm is the market's rate of return and ri the asset's. Beta is the sensitivity of the asset to the market:

  • β = 1: moves one-for-one with the market.
  • β > 1: amplifies market moves (aggressive).
  • 0 < β < 1: moves less than the market (defensive).
  • β = 0: unrelated to the market, like the risk-free asset.
Systematic and idiosyncratic risk

The part of an asset's risk that comes from market-wide forces is systematic risk (measured by β). It cannot be diversified away. The part that is firm-specific is idiosyncratic risk. It can be diversified away by holding many assets, so the market does not pay anyone for bearing it. Only β is paid for.

4.2 Equilibrium and the CAPM

In equilibrium, all assets must offer the same risk-adjusted rate of return. Otherwise investors would rush into the better one. To adjust for risk we need the price of risk and the amount of risk.

Derivation of the CAPM, step by step
Amount of risk in asset i = (its risk relative to the market) × (total market risk) = βiσm.
Price of risk = slope of the CAL = p = (rm − rf)/σm: the extra return investors demand per unit of risk (Part 3).
Cost of asset i's risk = price × amount = p βiσm = [(rm − rf)/σm] βiσm = βi(rm − rf). (σm cancels.)
Risk-adjusted return of asset i = ri − βi(rm − rf).
In equilibrium all risk-adjusted returns are equal. The risk-free asset has β = 0, so its risk-adjusted return is just rf. Hence for every asset i: ri − βi(rm − rf) = rf.
Rearrange:
ri = rf + βi (rm − rf)
The CAPM in words

Expected return = risk-free return + risk adjustment. The adjustment βi(rm − rf) is the extra return people demand to bear the market-related risk the asset carries. Plotting r against β gives a straight line, the security market line (SML). Checks: β = 0 gives rf; β = 1 gives rm.

Worked example: is an asset fairly priced? (β = 1.5, rf = 4%, rm = 10%)
Required return = 4% + 1.5 × (10% − 4%) = 4% + 9% = 13%

The asset is expected to give 15%, above the 13% the CAPM requires, so it sits above the SML: it is underpriced (its price is too low).

How the market fixes it: investors buy it, which pushes its price up. A higher price means a lower return on the same payoff. For instance, if it pays 115 next year, at 15% its price is 115/1.15 = 100; at the fair 13% the price is 115/1.13 = {pfair:.2f}. Buying continues until the return falls to 13%.

If instead it offered less than 13% it would be overpriced: investors sell, the price falls and the return rises back to the line.

Computing a beta (extra worked example)

Two equally likely states. The market returns +20% or −10%. Asset i returns +30% or −20%.

μm = ½(20) + ½(−10) = 5%;   μi = ½(30) + ½(−20) = 5%.
var(rm) = ½(15)2 + ½(−15)2 = 225, so σm = 15.
cov(ri, rm) = ½(25)(15) + ½(−25)(−15) = 375.
βi = 375/225 = 1.67

With rf = 2%, the CAPM return is 2 + 1.67 × (5 − 2) = 7%. The asset is expected to give only 5%, so it is overpriced.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Use excess market return (rm − rf), not rm, in the CAPM.
  • Beta is covariance divided by the market's variance (not its standard deviation).
  • Own variance does not appear in the CAPM: a very volatile asset with β = 0 earns only rf.
  • Above the SML = underpriced (return too high); below = overpriced.

Practice problems with solutions

Tap a problem to see the solution. Try it on paper first. Problems 6, 7, 9, 17 follow your weekly assignment style.

Problem 1

m = 60, p₁ = 4, p₂ = 3. Write the budget line, its intercepts and slope. Then (a) p₁ falls to 2, (b) income rises to 90 (prices back at 4 and 3).

Solution

4x₁ + 3x₂ = 60. Intercepts: x₁ = 15, x₂ = 20. Slope −4/3.

(a) 2x₁ + 3x₂ = 60: intercepts 30 and 20, slope −2/3. The line pivots around (0, 20).

(b) 4x₁ + 3x₂ = 90: intercepts 22.5 and 30, slope still −4/3. A parallel shift outward.

Problem 2

U = x₁²x₂. Find the MRS at (3, 6).

Solution

MU₁ = 2x₁x₂ = 2(3)(6) = 36. MU₂ = x₁² = 9. MRS = −36/9 = −4. She would give up at most 4 units of good 2 for one more unit of good 1.

Problem 3

U = x₁x₂, p₁ = 2, p₂ = 4, m = 80. Find the best bundle.

Solution

Tangency: x₂/x₁ = 2/4, so x₁ = 2x₂. Budget: 2(2x₂) + 4x₂ = 80, so 8x₂ = 80. x₂ = 10, x₁ = 20. (Shortcut: half of income to each good: 40/2 = 20 and 40/4 = 10.)

Problem 4

U = 2√x₁ + x₂, p₁ = 1, p₂ = 1, m = 10. Find the best bundle.

Solution

MU₁ = 2·½x₁−½ = 1/√x₁, MU₂ = 1. Tangency: 1/√x₁ = 1/1, so x₁ = 1. Budget: 1 + x₂ = 10, so x₂ = 9. Bundle (1, 9).

Problem 5

U = c₁c₂, r = 20%, income 50 now and 120 next period. Find the best (c₁, c₂). Does he borrow or save?

Solution

PV of income = 50 + 120/1.2 = 150. Tangency: c₂/c₁ = 1.2. Budget: c₁ + 1.2c₁/1.2 = 2c₁ = 150. c₁ = 75, c₂ = 90. He spends 75 against income 50, so he borrows 25, repays 25 × 1.2 = 30 and has 120 − 30 = 90 ✓.

Problem 6

A shop is worth 400,000 if there is no fire and 40,000 if there is one (probability 0.2). Insurance costs 0.2 per unit of cover. u = √c. Find the budget line, the optimal cover and the premium.

Solution

With cover x: c_F = 40,000 + 0.8x; c_NF = 400,000 − 0.2x. Eliminate x: 0.2c_F + 0.8c_NF = 328,000. The price 0.2 equals the probability 0.2, so insurance is fair and tangency gives c_F = c_NF = 328,000. Then 400,000 − 0.2x = 328,000, so x = 360,000 (full insurance of the 360,000 loss) and the premium is 72,000.

Problem 7

u = √x. A player earns 800,000 if fit and 20,000 if injured (probability 0.2). What is the most he would pay for a policy that guarantees 800,000 − p?

Solution

EU = 0.2√20,000 + 0.8√800,000 = 28.28 + 715.54 = 743.83. Set √(800,000 − p) = 743.83: 800,000 − p = 553,277, so pmax ≈ 246,723. (The fair price would be 0.2 × 780,000 = 156,000; the extra is his risk premium.)

Problem 8

u = √x. A lottery pays 36 or 100 with equal probability. Would you prefer it to a sure 70? Find CE and RP.

Solution

EU = ½(6) + ½(10) = 8, so CE = 8² = 64. E[W] = 68, so RP = 68 − 64 = 4. A sure 70 beats CE = 64 (utility √70 = 8.37 > 8), so take the sure 70.

Problem 9

u = √x. A lottery pays 3,600 or 400 with equal probability. Find the expected utility, CE and risk premium.

Solution

EU = ½(60) + ½(20) = 40. CE = 40² = 1,600. E[W] = 2,000. RP = 2,000 − 1,600 = 400.

Problem 10

Explain in a few lines why an insurer cannot sell full insurance when the insured's precautions are unobservable, and what it does instead.

Solution

With full cover a loss costs the insured nothing extra, so spending on precaution (like the 1,900 on smoke alarms) only wastes money; she stops. The loss probability rises (0.1 back to 0.5 in the example), and the insurer, which priced for the low risk, loses money (640 premium vs 3,200 expected claim). So the insurer uses a deductible or co-payment, forcing the insured to keep part of the loss and keep taking care. The cost is less risk sharing.

Problem 11

A stock returns 20% or 0% with equal probability. Find its mean and standard deviation.

Solution

Mean = ½(20) + ½(0) = 10%. Deviations ±10, so variance = ½(100) + ½(100) = 100 and σ = 10%.

Problem 12

rf = 5%. A risky fund has mean 15% and σ = 20%. Find the CAL slope, and return and risk for x = 0.5, x = 1.5, and the x that gives σ = 12%.

Solution

Slope = (15 − 5)/20 = 0.5. x = 0.5: return 5 + 0.5 × 10 = 10%, risk 0.5 × 20 = 10%. x = 1.5 (borrowing): return 5 + 1.5 × 10 = 20%, risk 30%. For σ = 12: x = 12/20 = 0.6, return = 5 + 0.6 × 10 = 11%.

Problem 13

rf = 4%. Fund M: mean 12%, σ = 16%. Fund Y: mean 20%, σ = 40%. Which is better?

Solution

Sharpe(M) = (12 − 4)/16 = 0.5. Sharpe(Y) = (20 − 4)/40 = 0.4. M is better. Check at σ = 16%: M gives 12%; Y mixed with cash (x = 0.4) gives 4 + 0.4 × 16 = 10.4%.

Problem 14

Two assets with σA = 20 and σB = 30, weights ½ each. Find σp if ρ = 0.5, and if ρ = −1. Compare with the weighted average.

Solution

Weighted average of σ = 25. ρ = 0.5: variance = ¼(400) + ¼(900) + 2(¼)(0.5)(20)(30) = 100 + 225 + 150 = 475, σp = 21.8, below 25. ρ = −1: 100 + 225 − 300 = 25, σp = 5. Lower correlation gives more diversification.

Problem 15

Two equally likely states. Market returns 12% or 2%. Asset i returns 18% or −2%. rf = 3%. Find β and say if the asset is fairly priced.

Solution

μm = 7, deviations ±5, var = 25. μi = 8, deviations ±10. Cov = ½(10)(5) + ½(−10)(−5) = 50. β = 50/25 = 2. CAPM return = 3 + 2(7 − 3) = 11%. The asset is expected to give 8% < 11%, so it is overpriced.

Problem 16

β = 0.8, rf = 3%, rm = 9%. What return does the CAPM require? If the asset is expected to give 7%, what happens?

Solution

Required = 3 + 0.8 × 6 = 7.8%. Expected 7% is lower, so the asset is overpriced: it sits below the SML. Investors sell, the price falls, and its expected return rises to 7.8%.

Problem 17

(Assignment style) John has rf = 10% and risky asset A (mean 25%, σ = 10%). He puts 40% in A. Find his portfolio mean, σ and the CAL slope. A new asset B has mean 30% and σ = 15%. Which does he prefer?

Solution

Mean = 0.4(25) + 0.6(10) = 16%. σ = 0.4(10) = 4%. Slope = (25 − 10)/10 = 1.5. Sharpe(B) = (30 − 10)/15 = 1.33 < 1.5, so he prefers A.

Problem 18

In Safina's problem, what if L2 had correlation +0.8 with Hill Land instead of −1? Which land should she buy?

Solution

With L2: σp = √(2,500 + 2,500 + 2(¼)(0.8)(10,000)) = √9,000 ≈ 94.9. With L1: 50. Same mean, lower risk with L1, so she now buys L1.

Problem 19

Why is an asset's own variance not the right measure of its risk when it is held in a portfolio?

Solution

Because what matters is how the asset moves with the rest of the portfolio. Example: A pays +10 or −5; B pays −5 when A pays 10 and +10 when A pays −5. Each is risky on its own, but together they pay 5 for sure. A risky asset that moves against the market lowers total risk; one that moves with it raises it. Beta captures this: only the part of risk that moves with the market (systematic) is rewarded.

Problem 20

State two conditions under which mean-variance preferences agree with expected utility.

Solution

(1) Returns follow a normal distribution, so the mean and variance describe the whole distribution. (2) The investor's utility is quadratic in wealth, so expected utility depends only on the mean and variance.
