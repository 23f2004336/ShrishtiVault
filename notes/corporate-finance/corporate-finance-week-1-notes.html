1. What gives an asset its value? (Lecture 1)

An asset is anything you can own that has value: a house, a painting, shares, bonds. Financial assets are called securities. The big question of asset pricing: what determines the price?

Why asset markets differ from goods marketsBuying fruit is only about eating it. Buying an asset has two motives: (1) benefit from owning it – live in the house, enjoy the painting, receive dividends; (2) resale / capital gain – sell later at a higher price. Both lie in the future, and the future is uncertain.
Two things decide a security's value: (1) the size of expected cash flows, (2) the uncertainty in forecasting them. Cash flow is just a common money yardstick for all future benefits.

Bonds

A bond promises fixed payments at fixed times. Government bonds have negligible default risk; corporate bonds do not.

Setup1-year zero-coupon bond, promises ₹10,000 in one year, price today = P.
Step 1 – return from buying and holding: r = (10,000 − P) / P
Step 2 – example, P = 9,000: r = 1,000 / 9,000 = 0.1111 = 11.1% per year
Step 3 – make P the subject: r = 10,000/P − 1 ⟹ 1 + r = 10,000/P ⟹ P = 10,000 / (1 + r)
Step 4 – prove inverse relation: dP/dr = −10,000 / (1 + r)² < 0  (denominator is a square, always positive, so the sign is always negative)

Meaning: the payout is fixed, so the only way the bond can offer a higher return is by being cheaper. If other interest rates in the economy rise, investors will only hold the bond if its return rises too → its price falls.

Corporate vs government bonds (risk–return)

  • Higher default risk → investors demand a higher interest rate → lower price for the same promised payments.
  • If the risky bond does not default you earn more; but there is a greater chance promised payments never arrive. Promised ≠ expected return.

More on bonds: worked examples

Price and rate are two ways of quoting the same thing. Using P = 10,000/(1+r):

Market rate r5%10%15%20%
Bond price P (₹)9,5249,0918,6968,333
Example 1 – rates riseRates go from 10% to 15%. Price falls from 9,091 to 8,696, a drop of (9,091−8,696)/9,091 ≈ 4.3%. Anyone who bought at 9,091 and must sell today loses money, even though the bond still pays 10,000.
Example 2 – safe vs risky bond (illustrative numbers)Both promise ₹10,000 in a year. Safe bond: investors demand 10% → price 9,091. Risky bond: investors demand 20% → price 8,333. If the risky bond defaults 10% of the time and then pays 0, its expected payout is 0.9×10,000 = 9,000, so its expected return is 9,000/8,333 − 1 = 8%, not 20%. The 20% is a promised yield, the 8% is closer to what investors expect on average.
Remember: promised return = what you get if no default. Expected return = probability-weighted. A high yield on a bond is a warning sign, not a gift.

Stocks vs bonds: a bond has a contractual payment schedule; a stock has no promised stream and no fixed time period. Firms expected to earn more get higher valuations → higher share price.

Systematic vs idiosyncratic risk

Why diversification kills idiosyncratic riskIf you hold 100 firms, one firm's lawsuit moves your portfolio by about 1%; some firms win, others lose and these average out. A recession hurts all firms together, so nothing averages out.
Required rate of return (market capitalization rate): the return that makes investors buy a share. Higher systematic risk → higher required return → lower price for the same expected earnings.
More systematic risk
↓
Higher required return
↓
Lower share price (given same expected earnings)

Why diversification removes idiosyncratic risk (derivation)

Suppose each stock's return is +10% or −10% (50/50), so one stock has mean 0 and standard deviation σ = 10%. Hold n stocks in equal weights.

Idiosyncratic case (independent firms): Var(average) = (1/n²)·n·σ² = σ²/n ⟹ SD = σ/√n
n = 1 → 10%  ·  n = 4 → 5%  ·  n = 100 → 1% (risk almost gone)
Systematic case (all move together, correlation = 1): SD(average) = σ = 10% for any n. Nothing is diversified.

This is the whole reason the market pays you only for systematic risk: idiosyncratic risk can be removed for free, so nobody will pay a premium for bearing it. Keep this idea – it returns in structured finance (Section 6).

Simple wordsIdiosyncratic = "my firm's bad luck". Systematic = "everyone's bad luck at once". Diversify across firms and the first cancels out. The second does not.

3. Markets for Financial Assets (Lecture 2)

3.1 Trading strategies (both are forms of speculation)

A momentum trader may pay more than fundamental value if she expects the price to rise further – profit comes from resale even if fundamentals never change. These strategies determine price behaviour and the possibility of bubbles and crashes.

3.2 Primary vs secondary market

Primary: company sells new shares to the public for the first time (IPO). Money goes to the company.
Secondary: existing shares traded on a stock exchange (BSE, NSE, SGX). Money moves between investors; company gets nothing.

Case study – Reliance Industries (RIL), one day in April 2020: price opened ≈ ₹1,175, fell to a low near ₹1,163, rallied to ≈ ₹1,183, closed ≈ ₹1,171. Volume was spread over the day with a big spike near the close (~15:00). Prices change constantly because beliefs change constantly.

3.3 Reservation prices and supply–demand

  • Seller's reservation price = lowest price a holder will accept.
  • Buyer's reservation price = highest price a buyer will pay.
Good news about RIL's profitsBuyers raise reservation prices → demand shifts right. Holders now want more to sell → supply shifts left. Price rises (slide: ₹1,162 → ₹1,167; volume 27k → 30k per hour).

3.4 Continuous double auction and the order book

Real exchanges do not run in hourly rounds. Anyone submits a limit order = (price, quantity).

  • Limit buy: "buy up to Q shares at a price no higher than p" (p = buyer's reservation price).
  • Limit sell: "sell up to Q at a price no lower than p".
  • Bids = buy orders, listed highest first. Asks = sell orders, listed lowest first.
New limit buy order arrives
↓
Is there an unfilled sell order at price ≤ my limit?
↓ yes / no ↓
Yes → trade now at the sellers' ask prices (best price for the buyer), lowest ask first
No → order joins the book and waits for a seller

Price dynamics: bad news (e.g. a rumour a director will resign) → a large sell order below the best bid eats the bids one by one → price falls. Good news → buy orders above the best ask eat the asks → price rises in steps. The market is never in a still equilibrium, but price is always adjusting to reconcile supply and demand, and reflects everyone, including those just holding.

Priority rule (price-time): better prices trade first; among equal prices, the order that arrived earlier trades first.

Why the volume spike near close? Many traders and funds want to settle positions before the market closes, so volume jumps even without news (the slide shows ~165k shares in one interval near 15:00).

4. Asset Market Bubbles (Lecture 3)

BubbleA sustained and significant departure of an asset's price from its fundamental value.

Index = weighted average of a basket of stocks (Sensex, NASDAQ Composite).

Case: the dot-com / tech bubble

  • 1995 → 2000: under 750 to over 5,000, ≈ 45% annual return.
  • Then lost two-thirds in under a year; bottom ≈ 1,100, almost 80% below peak.

Three features that make bubbles possible

1. Resale value
can speculate on price change, not just income
2. Ease of trading
switch buyer↔seller quickly
3. Ease of borrowing
cheap debt amplifies demand for a rising asset

Momentum feedback loop

Price rises
↓
Traders read it as a signal of more rises (momentum)
↓
More buying (often with borrowed money)
↓ back to top, until ↓
Sudden crash

Price changes are messages carrying information, but if everyone reads "up" as "more up", the message becomes self-reinforcing.

Do bubbles really exist? Three views

WhoClaim
Eugene Fama – Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)All public information is in prices almost instantly. "Bubble" is hindsight. Tech prices moved because news about firms changed sharply. Logic: if a formula predicted a crash next week, traders would sell today, so prices would fall today. People who say "too high" are right about half the time.
Robert Shiller – behaviouralBubbles are real: "irrational exuberance", prices driven by expectations of further rises. Simple public statistics like price/earnings ratio reveal them in real time. Leaning against the wind may lose short-run, but wins long-run versus a diversified basket of similar-risk assets.
Citibank CEO (2007)"As long as the music is playing, you've got to get up and dance." Nobody knows when others will stop; a manager who stops early underperforms rivals, so everyone stays → corrections come late and abruptly.

Fama vs Shiller with a concrete example

Suppose a stock trades at P/E = 40 while its long-run average is about 15.

Shiller: "40 vs 15 is a red flag. Prices are being driven by expectations of further rises. Sell or avoid; you may lose short-run, but win long-run."
Fama: "A high P/E may simply mean investors expect very high future earnings growth. Prices already contain all public information, so you cannot reliably tell it is a bubble until after it bursts."
Tech-bubble numbers, checked:
Annual return 1995→2000: (5,000/750)^(1/5) − 1 = 6.67^0.2 − 1 ≈ 0.46 → about 45–46% a year
Fall from peak: 1 − 1,100/5,000 = 0.78 → 78% (the slide says "almost 80%")

Why Fama's logic holds: if a reliable crash signal existed, traders would sell the moment they saw it, so prices would fall before the crash. A predictable crash would destroy itself. Why critics disagree: a trader who sells too early and is wrong underperforms (Prince's "dance"), and nobody knows when others will stop, so riding the bubble can be rational for the individual even if it is bad for the market.

5. The 2007–08 Financial Crisis (Lecture 4)

US house prices (Case-Shiller index) more than doubled from Jan 2000 (=100) to a peak ≈ 225 in mid-2006, then fell sharply. Analysts expected lower demand and slower growth; few foresaw a macro crisis, because they missed the effect on the financial system.

Two causes of the boom

  • Low interest rates → low mortgage rates → more demand → higher house prices.
  • Subprime mortgages → loans to riskier borrowers. While prices kept rising, nobody worried.

From 2006 prices fell → underwater mortgages (loan > house value) → many defaults. Mortgages were also riskier than borrowers understood.

Three weaknesses of financial intermediaries

High leverage
banks underestimated risk; managers paid for high-return bets; SIVs let banks sidestep leverage rules
Illiquid assets
complex MBS, hard to value, only sellable at fire-sale prices
Liquid (short-term) liabilities
wholesale funding: must be rolled over; not covered by deposit insurance
Key termsSIV: off-balance-sheet vehicle; borrows short-term from investors, holds securities, backed by a guarantee from the sponsoring bank. Securitization / MBS: pooling mortgages from many regions; sounds diversified, but lowered banks' care about repayment and misled rating agencies. Wholesale funding: short-term debt from other banks/investors (not checkable deposits).

Contagion chain

House prices fall (2006)
↓
Underwater mortgages, subprime defaults
↓
MBS values plunge; doubts on SIV and bank solvency
↓
Investors refuse to roll over short-term lending (run on wholesale funding)
↓
Capital falls → forced asset sales at fire-sale prices → capital falls more
↓
Credit freeze, higher borrowing rates, confidence collapse
↓
Less consumption and investment → aggregate demand and GDP fall

Policy responses

A tiny fall in asset values is enough to destroy a highly leveraged bank. Mortgage defaults made asset values fall by more than that.

The fire-sale spiral and funding run, step by step

Why deposits did not run but wholesale funding did: checkable deposits are insured, so depositors have no reason to panic. Wholesale lenders have no insurance and can simply decline to roll over. Hence the policy fixes below.

6. Rise and Fall of Structured Finance (Lecture 5)

Structured financePool assets (loans, bonds, mortgages) and issue prioritised claims called tranches against the pool. Many tranches end up safer than the average asset in the pool.

Rise and fall: US issuance was $25–40bn per quarter in Q1–Q3 2005, peaked near $100bn per quarter (Q4 2006 – Q2 2007), then fell to under $5bn per quarter in H1 2008. Growth was ten-fold in under a decade.

The tranching picture

Mortgages · Auto loans · Credit cards
→
SPV (pool, off the originator's balance sheet)
→
Senior (AAA) – loses last
Mezzanine (BBB)
Junior / equity – loses first

Losses flow from the bottom up (a waterfall). The junior tranche is the cushion ("overcollateralization") protecting the senior.

Two fatal flaws

Flaw 1 – Fragile to parameter errorsA AAA rating needs very precise estimates of default probabilities and correlations. Small errors can turn AAA into something that defaults with a reasonable chance.
Flaw 2 – Swaps diversifiable risk for systematic riskPooling assumes defaults are independent. But subprime defaults rise together in a downturn, so senior tranches fail exactly in bad times. Investors should have demanded much larger risk premia than for a similarly-rated corporate bond.

Credit rating agencies (CRAs)

  • Moody's, S&P, Fitch built a century of expertise on single-name corporate debt. Issuers wanted structured products rated on the same scale so rule-bound investors could buy them – an illusion of comparability.
  • Mid-2007: 37,000 US structured issues rated AAA. About 60% of structured products AAA vs under 1% of corporate issues (Fitch).
  • 2006: Moody's earned 44% of revenue from structured products (a conflict of interest, since issuers pay for ratings).
  • 2008: revenue vanished, rating-agency stocks fell about 50%. 27 of 30 Merrill Lynch asset-backed CDO tranches (AAA) became junk. In 2007 Moody's downgraded 31% of rated ABS CDO tranches and 14% of those originally AAA. Market effectively shut by mid-2008.

Loss-waterfall diagram (CDO pool in the simulation)

Pool loss grows from 0% upward
Each default of a 50%-recovery bond costs 0.5% of the pool (1 bond of 100). So 6% loss = 12 defaults; 12% loss = 24 defaults.
Senior: hit only if loss > 12% (> 24 defaults)
Mezzanine: hit if loss is 6–12%
Junior: wiped out first, 0–6%

Manufacturing AAA – extra insight

Expected pool loss = 100 bonds × 5% × 50% = 2.5%, fixed no matter what correlation is. Tranching does not remove this loss; it moves it all into the junior tranche (which absorbs the first 6%). Senior is safe only because it is far above the expected loss and because it takes many simultaneous defaults to reach it.

7. Manufacturing AAA Securities – the Maths (Lecture 6)

Two steps: (1) Pooling in an SPV. A pass-through (equal fractional claims) just inherits the average rating, so no credit enhancement. (2) Tranching – priority rules for absorbing losses. This is where the "magic" happens.

7.1 Two-bond model

SetupTwo identical bonds, face value 1, default probability PD, pay 1 if OK, 0 if default. Pool = 2. Issue Junior J (face 1) and Senior S (face 1). Assume independent defaults (correlation γ = 0).
Bonds defaultingProbabilityJunior paysSenior pays
0(1−PD)²11
12·PD(1−PD)0 (absorbs the loss)1
2PD²00
P(Senior defaults) = P(both default) = PD²
P(Junior defaults) = 1 − P(none default) = 1 − (1−PD)² = 2PD − PD²
With PD = 0.10: Senior = 0.01 = 1% (10× safer than the bonds!), Junior = 1 − 0.81 = 19%

Why does this work? For the senior to fail, both must fail. If defaults are independent, two bad events at once are rare (0.1 × 0.1). The risk has not vanished – it is concentrated in the junior tranche.

7.2 Three-bond model

Junior defaults if ≥1 bond defaults; Mezzanine if ≥2; Senior only if all 3 (PD = 0.10, independent).

Senior: PD³ = 0.001 = 0.1%
P(exactly 2) = C(3,2)·PD²·(1−PD) = 3 × 0.01 × 0.9 = 0.027
Mezzanine: 0.027 + 0.001 = 2.8%
Junior: 1 − (0.9)³ = 1 − 0.729 = 27.1%
Insight: with 2 bonds only 1 of 2 tranches (50%) is safer than 10%; with 3 bonds 2 of 3 tranches (66.7%) are. A bigger pool → a larger fraction of AAA-like paper.

7.3 CDO² (re-securitisation)

Take two junior tranches (each 19% default) from independent pools, pool them, and tranche again. The new senior defaults only if both juniors default:

0.19 × 0.19 = 0.0361 ≈ 3.6%

So "junk" (19%) is turned into something far safer. Note 3.6% is still above the original 1% senior, but shows how the machine keeps manufacturing safe-looking paper.

7.4 When correlation = 1

If the two bonds are perfectly correlated, they either both survive (90%) or both default (10%). A single default is impossible.

P(Senior default) = P(both default) = 10% = same as one bond
Key conclusion: credit enhancement depends on low default correlation. γ = 0 → senior 1%; γ = 1 → 10%; in between → between 1% and 10%.
Low correlation → defaults rarely coincide → very safe senior
Medium correlation → senior default between 1% and 10%
High correlation → senior ≈ underlying bond, no benefit

General N-bond rule and a 4-bond check

With N identical independent bonds, let X = number of defaults ~ Binomial(N, PD). Tranche i (i = 1 is junior) defaults if at least i bonds default: P(X ≥ i).

PD = 10%Tranche 1 (junior)234 (senior)Tranches safer than 10%

N = 4 working: P(X≥1) = 1−0.9⁴ = 0.3439; P(X≥2) = 1 − 0.6561 − 4(0.1)(0.9³) = 1 − 0.6561 − 0.2916 = 0.0523; P(X≥3) = 4(0.1³)(0.9) + 0.1⁴ = 0.0036 + 0.0001 = 0.0037; P(X≥4) = 0.0001. As N grows, the fraction of "safe" tranches approaches 100% – this is the "magic" and also why the whole thing collapses when independence fails.

Intermediate correlation (derivation)

For default indicators with default probability PD each, correlation γ = (P(both) − PD²) / (PD(1−PD)). Solve for the joint default probability:

P(both) = PD² + γ·PD(1−PD)
γ = 0 → 0.01  ·  γ = 1 → 0.01 + 0.09 = 0.10 ✓  ·  γ = 0.5 → 0.01 + 0.045 = 0.055

So Senior default probability = P(both) rises smoothly from 1% to 10% as γ goes 0 → 1: the "between 1 and 10 percent" in the lecture.

9. Why Rating Structured Products Is So Hard (Lecture 8)

A credit rating measures ability to meet payments, based on probability of default or expected loss:

Expected loss = P(default) × loss severity given default

Grades: investment grade = BBB− or better (low–moderate risk); speculative / junk = BB+ or below. Fitch history (10-year default prob. / annualised): AAA 0.19% / 0.02%; BBB− 7.26% / 0.75%; BB+ 10.18% / 1.07%; B 27.67% / 3.24%; C 95% / 29.96%.

Single-name: one firm, its own default probability and loss. Correlation is irrelevant.
Structured: need the entire joint distribution of payoffs, i.e. all pairwise correlations. Errors are magnified, especially in CDO².

Simulation study

  • 40 CDO pools × 100 bonds; 5-year PD = 5%, recovery 50% (loss given default 50%), each bond ≈ BB+.
  • Within-pool correlation 0.20; across pools 0.
  • Tranches by pool loss: Junior 0–6%, Mezzanine 6–12%, Senior >12%. CDO² pools the 40 mezzanine tranches.

Baseline result: from BB+ junk collateral: Senior = AAA, Mezzanine = BBB−; CDO² mezzanine and senior both = AAA.

Why the figures behave this way

  • Payoff vs correlation graph: collateral and senior payoffs stay near 1 as correlation rises, but the mezzanine payoff falls and the junior payoff rises (as in the 2-bond case: more all-or-nothing outcomes). The senior only starts to slip at high correlation.
  • Payoff vs default probability graph: junior and mezzanine payoffs collapse quickly as pD rises (cushions burn through), while senior declines slowly. Even a small rise in pD from 5% to 7.5% destroys the CDO mezzanine rating (BBB− → B+).
  • Why CDO² is worse: its collateral is mezzanine tranches, which are already the most sensitive pieces. Errors pass through two layers, and all 40 pools share the same mis-estimated parameters, so their errors are correlated.
  • Real-world link: many CDOs held subprime MBS, which were themselves tranches, so they were effectively CDO². Subprime history was short, so data to estimate pD and correlation was scarce.

10. Quick Revision Sheet

  • Value = size of expected cash flows + uncertainty. P = FV/(1+r); dP/dr < 0.
  • Risky bond → higher required return → lower price. Systematic risk is priced; idiosyncratic is diversified away.
  • Order book: bids (high→low), asks (low→high); trades at ask for buyers; spread = best ask − best bid.
  • Bubble = persistent price ≠ fundamental. Fama (no bubbles, EMH) vs Shiller (irrational exuberance, P/E) vs Prince ("keep dancing").
  • Crisis chain: house prices ↓ → defaults → MBS/SIV doubts → wholesale freeze → fire sales → credit crunch → GDP ↓.
  • Structured finance: pool + tranche. Senior default (2 bonds) = PD²; 3 bonds: 0.1%, 2.8%, 27.1%; CDO² senior 3.6%.
  • Correlation 0 → 1: senior default 1% → 10%; senior payoff 99 → 90; SD 42.43 → 60.
  • Two flaws: parameter sensitivity; systematic-for-idiosyncratic swap. CRAs misapplied single-name models.

Likely exam questions and what to write

  • "Prove price and interest rate are inversely related." r = (FV−P)/P → P = FV/(1+r) → dP/dr = −FV/(1+r)² < 0 → explain with competing assets.
  • "Why is idiosyncratic risk not priced?" Diversifiable (SD = σ/√n) so investors need no premium; systematic is not diversifiable so it sets the required return.
  • "Walk through a limit order of X shares at price p." Compare p with best ask/bid; fill level by level from the best price; leftover rests in the book; give the new best price and spread.
  • "Bubble debate." Definition + Fama (EMH, hindsight) + Shiller (P/E, irrational exuberance) + Prince (liquidity pressure) + three bubble-enabling features.
  • "Explain how the crisis spread." Use the 7-box chain plus leverage, illiquid assets, short-term liabilities.
  • "How can AAA be made from junk? Why did it fail?" Pool + tranche, show PD² = 1%; then correlation γ = 1 gives 10%, parameter sensitivity, systematic risk, CDO², CRA conflict.
  • Numerical: two-bond expected values and variance, 3-bond tranche probabilities, CDO² 0.19² = 3.61%.

Common mistakes

  • Saying correlation changes the expected pool value. It changes only the spread of outcomes.
  • Forgetting that buyers pay the ask, not their limit price.
  • Calling a high promised yield a high expected return.
  • Mixing the primary market (money to the company) with the secondary market (money between investors).
