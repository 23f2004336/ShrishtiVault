1. Maturity and Duration (Lecture 1)

1.1 Why a better measure than maturity?

We often need one number that says "how long is this project or bond?" The simplest is maturity: the date of the last payment.

MaturityThe date on which the final cash flow arrives. A bond paying 1 unit in 30 years has maturity 30 years.

But maturity looks only at the last payment. Take three bonds:

BondPaymentsMaturity
Bond 1 (zero-coupon)1 unit in year 3030 years
Bond 21 unit in year 1 and 1 unit in year 3030 years
Bond 3100 units in year 1 and 1 unit in year 3030 years

All three have maturity 30. But Bond 3 gives you nearly everything in year 1, so it is "really" a 1-year investment with a tiny 30-year tail. Maturity cannot see that. It ignores (1) when the intermediate payments occur and (2) how big they are.

Bar heights show the cash received. Bond 3's bar in year 1 is 100 times taller than its year-30 bar.

1.2 Duration: payment-weighted average of payment times

Duration = Σ t·C_t / Σ C_t   (each time t is weighted by C_t / total of all payments)

Think of it as a centre of gravity: put the cash flows as weights on a ruler marked in years. The balance point is the duration.

Bond 1: (30 × 1)/1 = 30 years
Bond 2: (1×1 + 30×1)/(1 + 1) = 31/2 = 15.5 years
Bond 3: (1×100 + 30×1)/(100 + 1) = 130/101 ≈ 1.29 years
Key facts: (i) a zero-coupon bond has duration = maturity (only one payment, so all the weight sits at T); (ii) duration is never more than maturity; (iii) earlier or bigger payments pull duration down. Bond 3, a 30-year maturity bond, behaves like a ~1.3-year investment.

1.3 Macaulay duration: weight by present values

Money received late is worth less today, so it should count less. Replace payments C_t with their present values:

Macaulay duration = Σ t·PV(C_t) / Σ PV(C_t)    with PV(C_t) = C_t/(1+r_t)^t

Here r_t is the annualised spot rate for maturity t (Week 3). Because it uses interest rates, Macaulay duration changes when interest rates change, unlike the payment-weighted one.

Slide example: 100 units in 1 year and 1 unit in 30 years; r₁ = 2%, r₃₀ = 5%
PV of year-1 flow: 100/1.02 = 98.04
PV of year-30 flow: 1/1.05³⁰ = 1/4.32194 = 0.231 ≈ 0.23
Total value = 98.04 + 0.23 = 98.27
Numerator = 1 × 98.04 + 30 × 0.231 = 104.98 (the slide rounds 30 × 0.23 to 6.90, giving 104.94)
Duration = 104.98/98.27 ≈ 1.07 years

Result 1.07 is lower than the earlier 1.29 because discounting shrinks the far-away payment from weight 1/101 (1.0%) to 0.23/98.27 (0.24%).

Payment-weighted: weight = C_t / ΣC (Bond 3: 99.0% at t=1, 1.0% at t=30) → 1.29 yrs
↓ discount each payment
PV-weighted (Macaulay): weight = PV_t / ΣPV (99.76% at t=1, 0.24% at t=30) → 1.07 yrs
Extra: same rates, Bond 2 (1 unit in year 1, 1 in year 30)PVs: 0.9804 and 0.2314. PV-weighted duration = (0.9804 + 30 × 0.2314)/(1.2118) = 6.54 years, much lower than the payment-weighted 15.5, because the year-30 unit is worth only 0.23 today.
Extra: a coupon bond (not in the lecture)3-year bond, face 100, 5% coupon, discount rate 5% (price = 100). Payment-weighted: (1×5 + 2×5 + 3×105)/115 = 2.87 years. PV-weighted: PVs are 4.76, 4.54, 90.70; duration = 2.86 years, below maturity 3 because the coupons arrive earlier.

2. Inflation and How It Is Measured (Lectures 2 + slides)

InflationA sustained rise in the general level of prices. Money loses purchasing power: each rupee buys less. If inflation is 10% on all goods, a ₹100 item costs ₹110 next year.

To measure inflation you need a price index: one number that summarises the price level.

2.1 Consumer Price Index (CPI)

CPI measures the cost of living for a "typical" household. When CPI rises, the household must spend more to keep the same standard of living.

Step 1 – Survey households: what is in the typical monthly basket (quantities)?
↓
Step 2 – Collect prices of every item in the base year and in the current year
↓
Step 3 – CPI = (cost of basket at current prices / cost of same basket at base-year prices) × 100
CPI = Σ p_C^i q_B^i / Σ p_B^i q_B^i × 100   (p_C = current price, p_B = base price, q_B = base-year quantity, i = item)

Base-year CPI is 100. CPI = 112 means the basket costs 12% more than in the base year. Inflation rate between two years: π = CPI_t/CPI_(t−1) − 1.

2.2 CPI is a weighted average of price changes (derivation)

CPI/100 = Σ p_C^i q_B^i / Σ p_B^i q_B^i
Multiply and divide each numerator term by p_B^i:  = Σ (p_C^i/p_B^i)(p_B^i q_B^i) / Σ p_B^i q_B^i
Define the weight w_i = p_B^i q_B^i / Σ_j p_B^j q_B^j (share of base-year spending on item i; the w's sum to 1)
CPI/100 = Σ w_i (p_C^i/p_B^i)

So CPI is the weighted average of the individual price changes, where each item's weight is its importance in the basket (the slide says base-year spending). A big-weight item such as food moves CPI most.

2.4 Limitations of CPI

CPI accurately measures the cost of a fixed list of goods, but not necessarily the cost of keeping the same standard of living.

1. Substitution bias
If one good's price shoots up, people buy less of it and more of cheaper substitutes. A fixed basket keeps the old quantities, so it overstates the rise in living costs.
2. New goods
New products (a new phone type) enter the basket late, so the benefit and early price falls are missed.
3. Unmeasured quality change
If a laptop costs 10% more but is 40% faster, part of the price rise is really better quality, not inflation.

2.5 Nominal GDP, real GDP and the GDP deflator

GDPThe market value of all final goods and services produced in the economy in a period. Market value = price × quantity.
  • Nominal GDP uses current-year prices, so it can change because of prices or quantities.
  • Real GDP uses fixed base-year prices, so it changes only when quantities change.
Slide economy: 50 chocolates and 20 shoes

In column 2 nominal GDP doubled while output did not change: all price. In column 3 nominal GDP quadrupled: half from output (real GDP doubled), half from prices (deflator doubled). The deflator isolates the price part.

GDP deflator P_t = Nominal GDP_t / Real GDP_t
Inflation rate = P_t/P_(t−1) − 1 = 2.0/1.0 − 1 = 100%

2.7 WPI and core inflation

Wholesale Price Index (WPI)
Prices paid to wholesalers by other producers (before retail). Weights: manufactured products 64.23%, primary articles 22.62%, fuel and power 13.15% (sum 100). Tracks producer cost pressures.
Core inflation
Measures the long-run trend in prices by removing volatile categories (usually food and fuel). In India this removes a big part of the basket (food alone is about 36–54% of CPI), so core is a much narrower measure.
Which to use? Household purchasing power → CPI. Overall output price level → GDP deflator. Producer cost pipeline → WPI. Underlying trend that monetary policy should respond to → core.

3. Real vs Nominal Interest Rates and the Fisher Equation (Lecture 3)

3.1 What is an interest rate, really?

An interest rate is the price of moving consumption across time: how much you must give up later to get one more unit now. With ₹100,000 at the start of the year you can spend it now, or save it and spend 100,000(1 + i) next year (or mix).

Nominal rate i
In units of currency. Borrow ₹1, repay ₹(1 + i). Rates in newspapers are nominal.
Real rate r
In units of goods. Borrow one basket of goods, repay (1 + r) baskets. Saving and investment decisions depend on this.

People consume goods, not paper notes. So what matters is how many goods you give up, and inflation changes that.

3.2 Deriving the Fisher equation (slides' one-good economy)

1. Price of the good today: P_t. To buy 1 unit you borrow P_t rupees.
↓
2. Next year you repay P_t(1 + i) rupees.
↓
3. Expected price next year: P^e_(t+1) = P_t(1 + π^e) (π^e = expected inflation)
↓
4. Goods you must give up = repayment ÷ price = P_t(1+i)/[P_t(1+π^e)] = (1+i)/(1+π^e)
↓
5. By definition this equals 1 + r
1 + r = (1 + i)/(1 + π^e)  (exact Fisher)
Rearranged: (1 + r)(1 + π^e) = 1 + i ⟹ 1 + r + π^e + rπ^e = 1 + i ⟹ r = i − π^e − rπ^e
If i and π^e are small (below about 5%), the product rπ^e is tiny: r ≈ i − π^e (approximate Fisher)
ConstraintThe approximation breaks down when rates or inflation are high (Section 4).

3.3 Case study: was borrowing cheaper in 1980 or 2007?

Q1 198015.11%14.20%0.91%0.80%
Q1 20075.30%2.40%2.90%2.83%

The slides' question: was it cheaper to borrow in 2007 (5.3%) than in 1980 (15.1%)? No: it was cheaper in 1980. A 1980 borrower paid back dollars that had lost about 14% of their buying power during the year, so the real cost was under 1%. A 2007 borrower faced a real cost above 2.8%. Looking only at nominal rates would give the opposite answer.

Plain exampleBorrow ₹100 for a year at 8% when inflation is 5%. Repay ₹108, which has the buying power of 108/1.05 = ₹102.86 of today's money. So the real cost is only 2.86% (approximation says 3%).
Negative real ratesDeposit rate 6%, inflation 8%: real rate = 1.06/1.08 − 1 = −1.85%. Your money grows in rupees but shrinks in what it can buy.

3.4 How do we know expected inflation π^e?

The nominal rate is known today, but the real rate depends on expected inflation, which is not observed. Five common ways to form it:

4. Inflation in Capital Budgeting: High Inflation and the Two Rules (Lecture 4)

4.1 Why the approximation is dangerous when inflation is high

Example 1A (slides): inflation 100% a year, a bond promising 700%
Approximate: r ≈ 700% − 100% = 600%  (wrong)
Exact: 1 + r = (1 + 7)/(1 + 1) = 8/2 = 4 ⟹ r = 300%
In words: ₹1 buys 1 basket today. Prices double, so a basket costs ₹2 next year. ₹1 invested returns ₹8 = 4 baskets. Giving up 1 basket today gives 4 baskets next year, a real return of (4 − 1)/1 = 300%. The shortcut overstated by 300 points (a factor of 2).

The error is about r·π: negligible at 8% and 5%, huge at 700% and 100%.

4.2 The two cardinal rules of NPV under inflation

Rule 1
Discount nominal cash flows with nominal rates.
Rule 2
Discount real cash flows with real rates.
Never mixNominal flows with real rates, or real flows with nominal rates. The units must match: rupees with rupee rates, baskets with basket rates.
Example 1B (slides): a project that gives 12 baskets next year (inflation 100%, nominal rate 700%, real rate 300%)
Real framework: PV = 12/(1 + 3) = 3 baskets (= ₹3 today since 1 basket costs ₹1)
Nominal framework: nominal cash flow = 12 baskets × ₹2 = ₹24; PV = 24/(1 + 7) = ₹3 today
Both give 3. (The slide's text says "discount nominal cash flows with real interest rates", but its arithmetic 24/8 uses the nominal rate 700%, i.e. Rule 1.)
What mixing would give24/(1 + 3) = ₹6 (twice too high) or 12/(1 + 7) = 1.5 baskets (half too low).

4.3 General proof that both methods agree

Let C_nom = C_real × (1+π)^t and (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + π)
PV = C_nom/(1+i)^t = C_real(1+π)^t / [(1+r)^t(1+π)^t] = C_real/(1+r)^t ∎
Multi-year check: 12 real baskets in each of years 1 and 2 (π = 100%, r = 300%, i = 700%)
Real: 12/4 + 12/16 = 3 + 0.75 = 3.75
Nominal flows: 12 × 2 = 24 in year 1; 12 × 4 = 48 in year 2. Nominal PV: 24/8 + 48/64 = 3 + 0.75 = 3.75 ✓
Practical rule: pick one framework and stay in it. Inflate the cash flows and use the nominal rate, or keep cash flows in today's prices and use the real rate. Convert the rate with the exact Fisher equation when inflation is high.

5.4 Forward rates: derivation and examples

Forward rateThe interest rate agreed today for a risk-free loan that starts at a future date t₁ and ends at t₂. Example: the 1-year rate from Dec 2022 to Dec 2023 is r₁,₂.

No-arbitrage requires that investing for 2 years in a 2-year bond equals investing for 1 year and then locking the next year's rate:

(1 + r̄₂)² = (1 + r̄₁)(1 + r₁,₂)  (i.e. 1 + r₀,₂ = (1 + r₀,₁)(1 + r₁,₂))
1 + r₁,₂ = (1 + r̄₂)²/(1 + r̄₁)
(1.0073)² = 1.014653; ÷ 1.0039 = 1.010712 ⟹ r₁,₂ ≈ 1.07%

Notice the 2-year annualised rate is 0.73%, the 1-year is 0.39%, but the forward rate for year 2 is 1.07%, roughly the 2-year yield plus the climb. A rising yield curve implies even higher forward rates.

6. Investment Horizon and Bond Payoffs (Lecture 6)

QuestionYou can buy (1) a 3-year zero-coupon bond at 0.97% a year or (2) a 1-year zero at 0.39%. You need your money back after 1 year. Can you buy the 3-year bond?

Yes: just sell it after 1 year, when it will be a 2-year bond. In a perfect, certain market you earn exactly 0.39%, the same as the 1-year bond. So there is no free gain from stretching maturity.

6.1 Proof

Face value F. Price today: P₀ = F/(1 + r̄₃)³ = F/(1.0097)³
Next year the bond has 2 years left and trades at the forward-based price: P₁ = F/[(1 + r₁,₂)(1 + r₂,₃)] = F/(1 + R₁,₃)²
No arbitrage: (1 + r̄₃)³ = (1 + r̄₁)(1 + R₁,₃)² ⟹ (1 + R₁,₃)² = (1 + r̄₃)³/(1 + r̄₁)
P₁ = F(1 + r̄₁)/(1 + r̄₃)³ = P₀(1 + r̄₁)
Return = (P₁ − P₀)/P₀ = r̄₁ = 0.39% ∎
Numbers (face 100)
P₀ = 100/1.0097³ = 97.15
Forward rates: r₁,₂ = 1.07%, r₂,₃ = 1.45% (derived from (1.0097)³ = 1.0039 × 1.0107 × (1 + r₂,₃))
P₁ = 100/(1.0107 × 1.0146) = 97.52  (the slide's 97.52)
Return = (97.52 − 97.15)/97.15 = 0.39% ≈ 0.39% (the small difference is rounding of the rates to 4 digits)
Intuition: the bond price "rolls up" at the one-year rate. The price today already includes the expected rate changes (forward rates), so if rates then evolve exactly as the forwards say, you earn the short rate, whatever bond you hold. The extra risk comes only from unexpected interest-rate changes: that is Section 7.

7. Interest-Rate Risk: Why Long Bonds Are Riskier (Lecture 6)

Government bonds have no default risk, but there is another risk: the market interest rate changes before the bond matures, and the bond's price moves. Equal-sized rate changes hit long-term bonds much harder.

7.1 The 30-year bond

Face 1,000, yield 1.90%: P = 1000/1.019³⁰ = 568.56
Yield rises 10 bp to 2.00%: P = 1000/1.02³⁰ = 1000/1.81136 = 552.07 ⟹ change = -2.90% ≈ −2.9%
Yield falls 10 bp to 1.80%: P = 1000/1.018³⁰ = 585.55 ⟹ change = +2.99% ≈ +3.0%

7.3 Why? A derivation (links to Section 1)

Zero-coupon bond: P = F(1 + y)^(−T)
dP/dy = −T·F(1 + y)^(−T−1)  ⟹  (dP/P)/dy = −T/(1 + y)
So % price change ≈ −[T/(1+y)] × (change in yield). For a zero-coupon bond T = duration, so % change ≈ −Duration/(1+y) × Δy
30-year, Δy = +0.10%: −30/1.019 × 0.001 = −2.94% ✓ (exact −2.90%)
1-year: −1/1.019 × 0.001 = −0.098% ✓

For coupon bonds the same formula holds with Macaulay duration in place of T. That is why duration, not maturity, is the measure of rate risk. (The exact result is slightly asymmetric: −2.90% for +10 bp but +3.00% for −10 bp, because the price-yield curve bends. This bend is called convexity.)

Conclusions from the slides: (1) if the bond is due soon you get your money back and can reinvest at the new rate, so rate changes matter little; (2) a long bond can gain or lose a lot; (3) only the shortest-term bond is effectively risk-free.

7.4 Term-varying cost of capital

The appropriate cost of capital should depend on how long-term the project is. Because the term structure usually slopes up, short projects have lower costs of capital, and long projects, even with no extra default risk, must deliver higher returns. The gap between long and short rates is the term premium (time premium).

8.1 What the curve tells us (and does not)

  • A steep curve often follows the end of a recession (rates low in the short run, expected to rise).
  • An inverted curve sometimes signals an upcoming recession. The slide calls the yield curve "a useful, though unreliable and noisy signal".
  • The curve is determined by supply and demand for savings and credit. Central banks influence but do not control it (they mainly set the short rate).
Exam wording: "Inverted curves have historically preceded recessions, but the signal is noisy; it is not a guaranteed forecast."

9. Why Does the Yield Curve Slope? Theories and Evidence (Lecture 7 + slides)

Running exampleOne-year annualised rate r̄₁ = 5%, two-year r̄₂ = 10% (upward-sloping). Implied forward rate: r₁,₂ = 1.10²/1.05 − 1 = 15.24%. Why would the market price year 2 at 15.24%?

Two main explanations (and they can both be true):

9.1 Explanation 1: Expectations hypothesis (higher future short rates)

Long-term yields are an average of today's short rate and expected future short rates. The forward rate is the market's prediction of next year's one-year rate:

(1 + r̄₂)² = (1 + r̄₁)(1 + E[r₁,₂])
Using the small-rate approximation (1 + r)² ≈ 1 + 2r:  r̄₂ ≈ [r̄₁ + E[r₁,₂]]/2

Under this view E[r₁,₂] = 15.24%: the market expects the one-year rate to jump from 5% to 15.24%. Inverted curve ⟹ the market expects short rates to fall (often central-bank cuts during a recession). The slide expresses the same check one year ahead: is (1 + r₁,₂)(1 + E[r₂,₃]) = (1 + r₁,₃)?

9.2 Explanation 2: Liquidity preference / term premium (compensation for risk)

Investors dislike the interest-rate risk of Section 7. To hold the long bond they must be paid more: a term premium TP:

r̄₂ ≈ [r̄₁ + E[r₁,₂]]/2 + TP₂    (TP₂ > 0 and grows with maturity)
Slide arithmetic: same shape next year, 100 unitsRolling two one-year bonds at 5% each: 100 × 1.05² = 110.25 → interest 10.25. Buying the 2-year bond at 10%: 100 × 1.10² = 121 → interest 21. The long bond earns ≈ 10.75 more. If future short rates are just 5% again, that extra is pure compensation for risk. In symbols: (1 + r₁,₂)(1 + E[r₂,₃]) < (1 + r₁,₃).
Splitting the 10% two-year yield (extra illustration)
If the market expects next year's rate to stay at 5%: r̄₂ = (5 + 5)/2 + TP = 5 + TP ⟹ TP = 5 points (all risk premium)
If it expects 8%: expectations part ≈ √(1.05 × 1.08) − 1 = 6.49%, so TP ≈ 10 − 6.49 = 3.51 points
If it expects 15.24%: TP = 0 (pure expectations)
Different beliefs about future rates give different premiums: you cannot read a premium off the curve without a view on expectations.

9.3 Explanation 3: inflation expectations and TIPS

Investors demand higher nominal rates when inflation is high; if they expect higher future inflation, the nominal curve slopes up. This applies only to nominal bonds. TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) pay the promised rate plus CPI inflation, so inflation should not make the TIPS curve slope up.

Nominal yield ≈ real (TIPS) yield + expected inflation + inflation-risk premium

So inflation expectations cannot explain the nominal slope; implied inflation actually falls with maturity here. Slide verdict: "in most of history they couldn't, but sometimes they can".

9.4 What does the evidence say?

  • Forward rates are poor predictors. A high forward rate does not predict a high future interest rate, so Explanation 1 alone is not enough.
  • Historically, buying 30-year bonds beat rolling one-month bonds for 30 years: investors were rewarded for holding long bonds.
  • Candidate reasons: (a) segmented markets / investor habitat (different investors prefer different maturities), but it is not clear why long-bond investors would be scarce, or why borrowers would not simply issue short bonds; (b) irrational short-term investors; (c) imperfections such as taxes and liquidity. The slides call (b) and (c) unlikely.
  • Most likely: long-term rates are higher as compensation for risk (the term premium).

9.5 Why rolling short bonds is "safe" (risk argument)

If you hold a one-month bond and rates double next month, you simply reinvest at the higher rate. A long-term bond locks you in and loses value. Thus rolling over short-term bonds insulates you from rate changes, and the long bond must pay more on average. This is the term premium in one sentence.

P1. A bond pays 50, 50 and 1,000 in years 1, 2, 3. Find its payment-weighted duration and (at a flat 6%) its Macaulay duration.
Payment-weighted: (1×50 + 2×50 + 3×1000)/1100 = 3150/1100 = 2.864 years
PVs at 6%: 47.17, 44.50, 839.62; total 931.29
Macaulay = (47.17 + 2×44.50 + 3×839.62)/931.29 = 2.851 years
Both are below the maturity 3 and very close to it, because 1,000 of the 1,100 arrives at year 3.
P2. Payments of 100 in year 1 and 100 in year 10. r₁ = 3%, r₁₀ = 6% (annualised). Macaulay duration?
PV₁ = 100/1.03 = 97.09; PV₁₀ = 100/1.06¹⁰ = 55.84; total 152.93
D = (1×97.09 + 10×55.84)/152.93 = 4.29 years
(Payment-weighted would be (1 + 10)/2 = 5.5.)

P16. Why is CPI not a perfect measure of the cost of maintaining a standard of living? Routine for numerical questions: (1) decide the object: duration, index, real rate, forward rate or bond price; (2) check units: nominal or real, annualised or holding; (3) write the formula, then substitute; (4) sanity check: duration below maturity, real rate below nominal when inflation is positive, forward rate above spot when the curve slopes up.

Common mistakes

  • Saying maturity measures how long money is tied up. It ignores earlier payments; use duration.
  • Using the approximate Fisher equation at high inflation.
  • Discounting nominal flows with real rates (or the reverse).
  • Confusing holding return, annualised yield and the individual annual (forward) rate.
  • Dividing a multi-year holding return by the number of years to annualise it.
  • Believing a high nominal rate means expensive borrowing: look at the real rate.
  • Reading an inverted curve as a certain recession forecast.
  • Quoting a term premium without saying what the market expects for future rates.
