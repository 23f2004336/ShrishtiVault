1. Present Value of Multi-Period Cash Flows (Lecture 1)

Time value of money (recap)A rupee today is worth more than a rupee later, because today's rupee can be invested to earn a positive return r. To compare cash at different dates, convert each to present value (PV), its worth today.

1.1 Deriving PV with a different rate each period

Let the rate for period 1 be r₁ and for period 2 be r₂. Invest PV today:

After 1 period: C₁ = PV(1 + r₁)  ⟹  PV = C₁/(1 + r₁)
Reinvest for period 2: C₂ = PV(1 + r₁)(1 + r₂)  ⟹  PV = C₂/[(1 + r₁)(1 + r₂)]
For T periods: PV = C_T / [(1+r₁)(1+r₂)…(1+r_T)]

Constant rate case (the benchmark: r₁ = r₂ = … = r): the product becomes (1+r)^T.

PV of one cash flow = C_T/(1+r)^T
PV of a stream = C₁/(1+r) + C₂/(1+r)² + … + C_T/(1+r)^T = Σ C_t/(1+r)^t

1.2 Why can we simply add present values? (value additivity)

In a perfect market you can trade each cash flow separately. If a stream is worth the sum of its parts to you, it is worth the same to anyone, so its price must equal the sum of the prices of the parts. Otherwise someone could buy the cheap way and sell the dear way and earn free money (arbitrage, Week 2). So PV(stream) = ΣPV(each flow).

1.3 Timing conventions: end of period vs beginning of period

Formulas assume the first payment is one period away (end-of-period, "ordinary"). If the first payment is received immediately (t = 0, 1, …, T−1: "annuity due", e.g. rent paid in advance), every payment is one period closer, so the PV is larger by exactly one period of compounding:

PV_due = PV_ordinary × (1 + r)
Example: 3 payments of 100, r = 10%

Beginning of period: 100 + 100/1.1 + 100/1.21 = 100 + 90.91 + 82.64 = 273.55

2. Application: Loan Repayment (EMI) (Lecture 1 + slides)

ProblemYou take a 30-year loan of ₹500,000, repaid in equal monthly payments, at a quoted interest rate of 7.5% a year. What is the monthly payment?

2.1 Step 1: what monthly rate does the bank really use?

Banks quote APR (annual percentage rate) by multiplying the monthly rate by 12, with no compounding in the quote. So the true monthly rate is 7.5%/12 = 0.625% (0.00625).

Effective annual rate = (1.00625)¹² − 1 = 7.763%, higher than the quoted 7.5% because monthly interest compounds.

2.2 Step 2: recognise the structure

A loan repaid in equal instalments is an annuity: the amount you receive today (₹500,000) equals the PV of the repayments. Payments begin next month (end-of-period), T = 30 × 12 = 360.

500,000 = (C/0.00625)[1 − 1/(1.00625)³⁶⁰]
(1.00625)³⁶⁰ = 9.421534;  1/that = 0.106140;  bracket = 0.893860
Annuity factor = 0.893860/0.00625 = 143.0176
C = 500,000/143.0176 = ₹3,496.07 per month for 360 months, first payment next month

Total repaid = 3,496.07 × 360 = ₹1,258,586, so about ₹758,586 is interest: you pay back more than twice what you borrowed. This is the price of 30 years of waiting.

2.3 Step 3: how much of each payment is interest, how much is principal?

Each month the bank charges interest on the balance you still owe. Whatever is left of your payment reduces the balance.

Interest_t = 0.625% × (balance at start of month t)
Principal_t = C − Interest_t
New balance = old balance − Principal_t

2.4 Why principal repayment grows by exactly (1 + r) each month (derivation)

P_t = C − r·B_(t−1)    and    B_t = B_(t−1) − P_t
P_(t+1) = C − r·B_t = C − r(B_(t−1) − P_t) = [C − r·B_(t−1)] + r·P_t = P_t + r·P_t
P_(t+1) = P_t (1 + r)
Check: 371.07 × 1.00625 = 373.39 ✓ (the slide's 373.39)

2.5 Early repayment: what must you pay?

If you want to close the loan right after paying month t, the bank wants the outstanding principal. This equals the PV of the payments still to come:

B_t = C × [1 − (1+r)^−(360−t)]/r
After 2 months: 3,496.07 × [1 − 1.00625⁻³⁵⁸]/0.00625 = ₹499,255.54 ✓ matches the slide's 499,255.54

The unaccrued interest for months 3–360 is wiped out. After 10 years (month 120) you would still owe ₹433,975, i.e. 87% of the loan after paying 419,529. That is the effect of interest-heavy early payments.

3. Application: Stock Valuation and the Cost of Capital (Gordon Growth Model)

A stock pays dividends for a very long time. Treat them as a growing perpetuity (Week 2: PV = C₁/(r − g)):

P₀ = Div₁ / (r − g)   (r > g)
SymbolMeaning
P₀share price today
Div₁dividend expected next year
rrequired return = cost of equity capital
gconstant yearly growth rate of dividends

This use of the formula is called the Gordon growth model. It is a quick estimate, not an exact value, since real rates and growth are not constant forever.

Example 1 (slide): mature businessProfit next year ₹1,000,000, growing 2% a year, cost of capital 8%: value ≈ 1,000,000/(0.08 − 0.02) = ₹16,666,667. The lecture calls it an approximation ("not worth more than…").
Example 2 (slide): share priceg = 5%, r = 10%, Div₁ = 10 → P₀ = 10/(0.10 − 0.05) = 200.

3.1 Reading the model backwards: finding the cost of capital

P₀ = Div₁/(r − g)  ⟹  r − g = Div₁/P₀  ⟹  r = Div₁/P₀ + g

Div₁/P₀ is the forward dividend yield (next year's expected dividend ÷ today's price). So: cost of capital = dividend yield + growth rate. It is the same split as Week 2's return = dividend yield + capital-gain yield, because under constant growth the price also grows at g.

Example 3 (slide): a firm with forward yield 2.2%The company paid 6.6 billion in dividends last year, almost the same as 4 years ago, so growth g ≈ 0%. Then r = 2.2% + 0% = 2.2%: the market values the firm as if its cost of capital were only about 2.2%. If instead analysts expected g = 5%, r would be 7.2%. So yield alone does not tell you r; you need a view on growth.

4. Why Is NPV the Right Rule? Separation of Decisions and Sensitivity (slides)

4.1 The "free money" argument

Perfect market, borrow/lend at 6%. Any project can be traded or financed at the market rate, so:

Positive NPV = free money. A project costing 1 and paying 1.07 next year: borrow 1 at 6% (repay 1.06), invest, keep 1.07 − 1.06 = 0.01 with no money of your own.
Negative NPV is a gift to others. Sell someone a project that costs them 1 and pays 1.05 at a time when the market pays 1.06: you pocket the 1 and invest it to get 1.06, owing only 1.05, so you earn 0.01 free.
Zero NPV: cost 1, pays 1.06. No free money from either side.

In a perfect world with correct inputs, NPV is the benchmark; other information is redundant. Positive-NPV projects should be rare, because everyone would rush to take them and competition would bid up returns (or bid down prices) until the gains disappear.

But in practice you must estimate cash flows and r, markets are imperfect (taxes, costs, disagreement, market power), so other rules can add useful information. NPV is still the benchmark.

4.2 Separation of investment and consumption decisions

QuestionDoes a person's wish to spend now or later change which projects they should accept? Cash in hand = 150. Project: cost 100 now, pays 200 next year. r = 10%. Person A wants money today; Person B wants to save for next year.
Person A (impatient)
Take the project, pay 100, then sell it in the market for 200/1.1 = 181.82. Cash today = 150 − 100 + 181.82 = 231.82. Without the project, A has only 150 to spend today. A is better off by 81.82 = NPV.
Person B (patient)
Take the project (pay 100, keep 50 in the bank). Next year: 200 + 50 × 1.1 = 255. Without the project: 150 × 1.1 = 165. B is better off by 90 next year (= 81.82 × 1.1).
Step 1 – Investment decision: take every positive-NPV project (same for everyone)
↓
Step 2 – Consumption decision: use the capital market (borrow or save) to move the resulting wealth to the date you want it
Separation of decisions: consumption preferences should not influence investment decisions. It works only when you can borrow and lend at the same rate. With different borrowing and lending rates (imperfect markets) it may fail.

4.3 Sensitivity (scenario) analysis: which mistake hurts more?

Inputs are estimates. Test how the PV changes if you get the cash flow or the discount rate slightly wrong.

Why? PV = C/(1+r)^T. A 10% error in C changes PV by 10% whatever T is. A rate error is raised to the power T: its effect ≈ T × (Δr)/(1+r). For T = 1 that is about 0.7%; for T = 30 it is about 30 × 0.8%/1.08 ≈ 22% (actual 19.9%).

Lessons: short projects: be careful with cash-flow estimates; the rate matters little. Long projects: both matter, and the rate matters a lot. In practice distant cash flows are harder to forecast than today's market discount rate.

5. Nominal vs Real Cash Flows and the Fisher Equation (Lecture 2)

5.1 Definitions

Nominal
Actual rupees at the time they are received. Bank rates and contracts are nominal.
Real
Measured in constant purchasing power (today's prices), i.e. what the rupees can actually buy.
Real cash flow: C_t(real) = C_t(nominal)/(1+π)^t    (π = expected constant inflation per period)

Example: ₹1,102.50 received in 2 years with 5% inflation buys what ₹1,102.50/1.05² = ₹1,000 buys today.

5.2 Deriving the Fisher equation step by step

1. Invest ₹1 of purchasing power today. It costs 1 rupee (prices at t=0 are the base).
2. At t=1 the investment pays ₹(1 + r_nom).
3. But prices have risen by (1+π), so the purchasing power of that payout is (1 + r_nom)/(1 + π). By definition this is the real growth, 1 + r_real.
4. (1 + r_real) = (1 + r_nom)/(1 + π)   ⟺   (1 + r_nom) = (1 + r_real)(1 + π) (exact Fisher)
5. Expand: 1 + r_nom = 1 + r_real + π + r_real·π
6. So r_nom = r_real + π + r_real·π
7. If r_real·π is tiny: r_real ≈ r_nom − π (approximate Fisher)

This is exactly the real-interest-rate result of Week 2, now with a name (Fisher).

5.3 The consistency rule (most tested)

Nominal cash flows → discount at the NOMINAL rate
Real cash flows → discount at the REAL rate
NEVER mix them

5.4 Proof that both methods give the same PV

Method 1: PV = C_nom/(1 + r_nom)^t
Method 2: substitute C_nom = C_real(1+π)^t and 1+r_nom = (1+r_real)(1+π):
PV = C_real(1+π)^t / [(1+r_real)(1+π)]^t = C_real(1+π)^t / [(1+r_real)^t (1+π)^t] = C_real/(1+r_real)^t ∎

The inflation factor (1+π)^t cancels. Inflation does not create or destroy value if you treat both numerator and denominator consistently.

6. Perpetuity and Annuity Formulas from First Principles (Lecture 2)

Week 2 derived these with infinite geometric series. Here is a second route that uses only the time-value idea (shifting a stream by one period multiplies its value by 1 + r). This method is useful if you forget the series formula in an exam.

6.1 Perpetuity: P = 1/r

Let P = PV of ₹1 paid every year forever, starting at t = 1.
Route 1: a stream starting today has the same payments as the stream starting next year plus an extra ₹1 now: value = 1 + P.
Route 2: the stream starting today is worth (1 + r) times the stream starting next year: value = (1 + r)·P.
Equate: 1 + P = (1 + r)P = P + rP  ⟹  1 = rP  ⟹  P = 1/r ∎   For payment C: PV = C/r
Stream starting at t=1: value P
↓ ×(1+r) (it is one period closer)
Stream starting at t=0: value (1+r)P = 1 + P

6.2 Annuity: A_n = (1/r)[1 − 1/(1+r)ⁿ]

A_n = PV of ₹1 a year for n years, starting at t = 1.

Route 1: shift to start at t=0 (payments at 0,…,n−1). Compared with A_n we add the t = 0 payment (+1) and remove the t = n payment (−1/(1+r)ⁿ): value = 1 + A_n − 1/(1+r)ⁿ
Route 2: the shifted stream is worth (1 + r)·A_n
Equate: 1 + A_n − 1/(1+r)ⁿ = A_n + rA_n
1 − 1/(1+r)ⁿ = r·A_n  ⟹  A_n = (1/r)[1 − 1/(1+r)ⁿ] ∎   For payment C multiply by C.
Check with n = 2, r = 10%Direct: 1/1.1 + 1/1.21 = 0.9091 + 0.8264 = 1.7355. Formula: (1/0.1)(1 − 1/1.21) = 10 × 0.17355 = 1.7355 ✓.
Why this works: the proof only uses two facts: moving a payment one period earlier multiplies its value by (1+r), and values add. No infinite sums needed.

6.3 Lecture practice problem: when to harvest a forest

Forest value V(t); continuous interest r. PV of harvesting at t is V(t)e^(−rt). Maximise:

d/dt[V e^(−rt)] = V′e^(−rt) − rVe^(−rt) = e^(−rt)[V′ − rV] = 0  ⟹  V′(t)/V(t) = r

Meaning: harvest when the forest's own growth rate falls to the interest rate (at that moment waiting earns less than the bank).

V(t) = −1000 + 1000t − 10t², r = 0.10, V′ = 1000 − 20t
1000 − 20t = 0.1(−1000 + 1000t − 10t²) = −100 + 100t − t²  ⟹  t² − 120t + 1100 = 0
t = [120 ± √(14,400 − 4,400)]/2 = [120 ± 100]/2  ⟹  t = 110 or t = 10
At t = 110, V′ = 1000 − 2200 = −1200 < 0 (forest shrinking) → reject. t* = 10, V(10) = 8,000
Naive peak: V′ = 0 at t = 50, V(50) = 24,000
Bank 8,000 for 40 more years at 10%: 8,000 × 1.1⁴⁰ = 8,000 × 45.259256 = 362,074 ≫ 24,000

Same lesson as Week 2: the best time to sell is when the asset's return equals the market return, not when its value peaks.

7. Compounding Frequency, APR vs EAR and Continuous Compounding (Lecture 3)

7.1 What if interest is paid more than once a year?

If the quoted annual rate (APR) is compounded n times a year, each sub-period earns APR/n, and there are n·t sub-periods in t years:

FV_t = C₀(1 + APR/n)^(n·t)    DF_t = 1/(1 + APR/n)^(n·t)
APR (annual percentage rate)
The quoted rate: sub-period rate × n. No compounding inside the quote.
EAR (effective annual rate)
What you really earn or pay in a year after compounding. EAR = (1 + APR/n)ⁿ − 1

8. Capital Budgeting Rules I: NPV, Payback and Discounted Payback (Lecture 4 + slides)

Capital budgeting = evaluating and choosing long-term projects to maximise the firm's value. We study four rules: NPV (benchmark), Payback, IRR, Profitability Index. First the benchmark.

8.1 NPV rule (recap and two extra properties)

NPV = −I₀ + Σ C_t/(1+r)^t   (I₀ = upfront investment, r = opportunity cost of capital, also called the hurdle rate)
NPVAction
> 0Accept: creates value
= 0Indifferent: earns exactly r
< 0Reject: destroys value

Value additivity of NPV: for independent projects, NPV(A + B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B). So each division can judge its own projects; the firm's total value is the sum. Example: NPVs of +30 and −10 give a combined +20; accepting only A gives +30, so reject B.

8.2 Payback period rule

DefinitionNumber of years until the cumulative undiscounted cash inflows equal the initial investment. Accept if payback ≤ a cutoff chosen by management.
Payback = T such that Σ_(t=1..T) C_t ≥ I₀ (interpolate within the year when needed)
Example (r = 10%, cutoff = 2 years)Project A: −100, +50, +50, 0, 0. Project B: −100, +20, +30, +80, +100.
Payback A: 50 + 50 = 100 by year 2 → 2.0 years ≤ 2 → accept
Payback B: after 2 years only 50. After year 3: 130. Interpolate: 2 + (100 − 50)/80 = 2.625 years > 2 → reject
NPV A = −100 + 50/1.1 + 50/1.21 = −100 + 45.45 + 41.32 = -13.22
NPV B = −100 + 18.18 + 24.79 + 60.11 + 68.30 = +71.38
Payback accepts the value-destroying project and rejects the value-creating one.

8.3 Three flaws of payback

  1. Ignores time value of money. It treats ₹1 in year 1 and year 3 as equal.
  2. Ignores everything after the cutoff. B's big years 3 and 4 are invisible to the rule. Long-term projects (infrastructure, R&D) are punished.
  3. Arbitrary cutoff. Nothing in finance tells you whether it should be 2 years or 5.

8.4 Discounted payback

Accumulate present values until they cover I₀. This fixes flaw 1 only.

8.5 Slide example: three projects at r = 5%

8.6 Why firms still use payback

  • Easy to explain: "how long to get my money back?"
  • Controls managers who may not be trusted with distant forecasts.
  • Useful for an entrepreneur with limited capital in an imperfect market, where the true cost of capital is high and quick cash return matters.
  • Often agrees with NPV in simple cases.
Verdict: payback is supplementary information, not the main criterion.

9. Capital Budgeting Rules II: Internal Rate of Return (Lecture 5 + slides)

9.1 Idea and definition

For a simple project (100 now → 115 next year) the rate of return is 15%, and if the market gives 10%, take it. IRR extends "rate of return" to projects with many cash flows.

IRRThe discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero: 0 = −I₀ + Σ C_t/(1+IRR)^t. With one outflow and one inflow, it equals the simple rate of return. For bonds, IRR is called yield to maturity (YTM).
Cost 100, pays 120 next year: −100 + 120/(1 + IRR) = 0 ⟹ 1 + IRR = 1.2 ⟹ IRR = 20%

9.2 IRR rule

Accept if IRR > r (the hurdle rate, i.e. cost of capital). Reject if IRR < r. Usually gives the same answer as NPV.

Slide example: cost 100,000; receipts 5,000 / 10,000 / 120,000 at years 1/2/3
Solve −100,000 + 5,000/(1+IRR) + 10,000/(1+IRR)² + 120,000/(1+IRR)³ = 0
No neat formula, so use trial and error or Excel's IRR function: IRR = 11.14%
Check at 11%: NPV = 364 (> 0 → accept). At 11.25%: NPV = -273 (< 0 → reject).
So with cost of capital 11% take it, with 11.25% don't, the same as NPV says.

9.3 Pitfall 1: lending vs borrowing projects

Investment ("lending")
Outflow first, then inflows. NPV falls as r rises. Accept if IRR > r.
Financing ("borrowing")
Inflow first, then outflows (you receive a loan). NPV rises as r rises. Accept if IRR < r.
Slide exampleReceive 100,000 now, then pay 5,000, 10,000 and 120,000 in years 1–3. Same IRR, 11.14%, but now it is a loan with an interest cost of 11.14%. Take it only if the prevailing market rate is above 11.14%, since then you are borrowing cheaper than the market. NPV check (value of the loan to you = −NPV of the payments at r): at r = 15% it is +9,189 (accept); at r = 8% it is -8,463 (reject).

Why: a loan is a good deal when its interest cost (IRR) is lower than what you'd pay elsewhere (r). Applying "IRR > r" blindly here gives the wrong decision.

9.4 Pitfall 2: multiple IRRs

If the cash-flow signs change more than once, NPV(r) can cross zero several times. By Descartes' rule of signs, the number of positive IRRs is at most the number of sign changes.

Lecture example: −100, +230, −132 (two sign changes)
0 = −100 + 230x − 132x², x = 1/(1+IRR) ⟹ 132x² − 230x + 100 = 0
x = [230 ± √(52,900 − 52,800)]/264 = (230 ± 10)/264 ⟹ x = 10/11 or 5/6
IRR₁ = 11/10 − 1 = 10%;  IRR₂ = 6/5 − 1 = 20%
NPV is positive only between 10% and 20%. A single "IRR > r" test is meaningless here.
Slide example: −100,000; +205,000; −102,000 (cleanup cost)IRRs of −15% and +20%. Check at 20%: −100,000 + 170,833 − 70,833 = 0 ✓. At −15%: −100,000 + 241,176 − 141,176 = 0 ✓. Spreadsheets may report only one root, so always plot NPV against r.

9.5 Pitfall 3: no IRR at all

  • Cash 100 today, 200 next period: all inflows. NPV is always positive for every r, never zero → no IRR (it is free money).
  • −10,000; +27,000; −20,000: the quadratic 20,000x² − 27,000x + 10,000 = 0 has discriminant 27,000² − 4·20,000·10,000 = 729M − 800M < 0 → no real root. NPV is negative at every r, so the project should never be taken.
When is IRR unique? If there is one negative cash flow followed only by positive ones, NPV falls steadily as r rises from very high (≈ −I₀) to low (large positive), so it crosses zero exactly once → one IRR. IRR is safe when there is only one positive or only one negative cash flow.

9.6 Pitfall 4: scale (mutually exclusive projects)

IRR is a percentage and ignores how big the project is. "Mutually exclusive" means you can only take one.

CaseProjectCash flowsIRRNPV

IRR picks the small projects (100%, 300%) but NPV picks the bigger value creators (47.62 and 81.82). Answer to "should one always prefer 100% IRR to 10% IRR?" is No.

Extra check (not in lecture): the extra investment of moving from Small to Large costs 90 more now and returns 160 more next year, an IRR of 78%, far above 10%, so the added investment is worthwhile. This "incremental IRR" gives the same decision as NPV.

9.7 Pitfall 5: rates that change over time

If short-term and long-term rates differ (Section 11), there is no single hurdle rate to compare one IRR against.

9.8 What IRR is good for

  1. It depends only on the project's own cash flows: no need for market rates to compute it.
  2. Helpful for judging profitability and managers' performance.
  3. The gap IRR − r shows the buffer: how wrong your cash-flow estimates can be before the decision flips.

10. Profitability Index and Capital Rationing (Lecture 5 + slides)

10.1 Definition

PI = PV(future cash inflows) / I₀ = (I₀ + NPV)/I₀ = 1 + NPV/I₀

It tells you the present value created per rupee invested. Standalone rule: accept if PI > 1 (identical to NPV > 0). It is meaningful only when the first cash flow is an outflow.

  1. Several budget constraints over time (money limited at t = 0, 1, 2…): need linear programming.

PI summary: good for ranking under a single-period budget; always check combinations. NPV remains the benchmark.

11. Time-Varying Rates of Return: Term Structure and Forward Rates (Lecture 6 + slides)

11.1 Why this matters

So far one rate r applied to every horizon. In reality it does not. Sept 2024: Indian government 1-year bond ≈ 6.8%, 30-year ≈ 7.1%. US 1-year ≈ 4.4%, 30-year ≈ 4.0%. In 2021: US 1-year ≈ 0.4% but 30-year ≈ 1.9%. Banks also offer different deposit rates for different durations. A company must compare money that pays back in 1 year with money that pays back in 30. Inflation expectations are one reason (Sec 5): expected higher inflation pushes nominal rates up.

11.2 Notation: spot and forward rates

SymbolMeaning
r₀,₁return from today (0) to year 1 = the 1-year spot rate
r₀,₂total (holding) return from today to year 2
r₁,₂return from year 1 to year 2 (a rate that starts in the future)
r̄_t (annualised)constant yearly rate that gives the same t-year return; the t-year spot rate
Spot vs forwardSpot rates are rates for loans that begin right now. Forward rates are rates for loans that begin in the future (agreed today).

11.3 Compounding different rates

1 + r₀,₂ = (1 + r₀,₁)(1 + r₁,₂)
Example 130% in year 1 and 20% in year 2: 1 + r₀,₂ = 1.30 × 1.20 = 1.56 → 56% (not 50%).
Example 2: invest from year 1 to year 41-year returns of 30% (yr 1→2), 40% (yr 2→3), 50% (yr 3→4):
1 + r₁,₄ = 1.30 × 1.40 × 1.50 = 2.73 ⟹ r₁,₄ = 173%
100 invested one year from now becomes 273 in year 4.

11.4 Annualised rate of return

Is 10% over 3 years better than 20% over 6 years? To compare you need per-year figures. The annualised rate r̄_t is the single constant yearly rate that, compounded t times, gives the actual holding return:

(1 + r̄_t)^t = 1 + r₀,t   ⟹   1 + r̄_t = (1 + r₀,t)^(1/t)
Example (3-year return 173%)Wrong: 173%/3 = 57.7% a year. Check: 1.577³ = 3.92 → 292% total, far more than 173%. Right: (2.73)^(1/3) = 39.76% a year; check 1.3976³ = 2.73 ✓.
Example 3: 100 today → 240 in 30 yearsHolding return = 140/100 = 140%. 1 + r̄₃₀ = 2.4^(1/30) = 1.02961 ⟹ r̄₃₀ ≈ 2.96% a year.
10% over 3 years vs 20% over 6 years?1.10^(1/3) − 1 = 3.23% a year; 1.20^(1/6) − 1 = 3.09% a year. The 3-year deal is slightly better per year.
Analogy: compounding is like adding returns, annualising is like averaging them. (Two years of 1% compound to 2.01%, annualise to 1%.) But because returns multiply, the proper average is the geometric one (root), not the arithmetic one (sum ÷ n).

11.5 Why the arithmetic average misleads

11.6 NPV with time-varying rates

NPV = C₀ + C₁/(1 + r₀,₁) + C₂/(1 + r₀,₂) + … = C₀ + C₁/(1 + r̄₁) + C₂/(1 + r̄₂)² + …

Each flow is discounted with the rate for its own horizon (the spot rate / annualised rate for that maturity).

Example 6Invest 24; receive 20 in 1 year and 16 in 5 years. 1-year rate 5%; 5-year annualised rate 6%.
NPV = −24 + 20/1.05 + 16/1.06⁵ = −24 + 19.048 + 11.956 = 7.00
(The slide quotes ≈ 7.01; rounding.) Positive → accept. Note the 5-year flow uses 6%, not 5%.

11.7 The term structure (yield curve)

The term structure of interest rates is the relation between the spot rate and the time to maturity. The picture of spot rates against maturity is the yield curve.

ShapeMeaningReal example
Normalr₁ < r₂ < r₃: long rates higherIndia 2024 (6.8% vs 7.1%); US 2021

11.8 Two equivalent ways to discount

Spot-rate method
PV = C₁/(1+r₁) + C₂/(1+r₂)² + … each flow with its own spot rate.
Forward-rate method
PV = C₁/(1+r₁) + C₂/[(1+r₁)(1+f₁,₂)] + C₃/[(1+r₁)(1+f₁,₂)(1+f₂,₃)]. Chain the one-year rates.

They give the same answer because (1+r₂)² = (1+r₁)(1+f₁,₂) (derived next).

11.9 Forward rates (derivation by no-arbitrage)

Forward rate f_(t₁,t₂)The break-even interest rate agreed today for a loan that starts at a future date t₁ and ends at t₂.

You have ₹1 and a 2-year horizon. Two ways:

Strategy A
Buy a 2-year zero-coupon bond. After 2 years: (1 + r₂)².
Strategy B
Buy a 1-year bond (1 + r₁), then at t=1 lend the proceeds for one more year at the forward rate f₁,₂ (locked in today). After 2 years: (1 + r₁)(1 + f₁,₂).
If there is no arbitrage, A = B: (1 + r₂)² = (1 + r₁)(1 + f₁,₂)
f₁,₂ = (1 + r₂)²/(1 + r₁) − 1
General: (1 + r_t)^t = (1 + r_(t−1))^(t−1)(1 + f_(t−1,t)) ⟹ f_(t−1,t) = (1 + r_t)^t/(1 + r_(t−1))^(t−1) − 1
Example: r₁ = 5%, r₂ = 6%f₁,₂ = 1.06²/1.05 − 1 = 1.1236/1.05 − 1 = 7.01%. With r₃ = 7%: f₂,₃ = 1.07³/1.06² − 1 = 1.225043/1.1236 − 1 = 9.03%.

Why must it be exactly this? Suppose the market offered a forward rate of 7.5% instead of 7.01%. Borrow ₹1 for 2 years at 6% (owe 1.1236). Lend it for 1 year at 5% (get 1.05 at t=1) and lock in lending that for year 2 at 7.5%: 1.05 × 1.075 = 1.12875. Profit = 1.12875 − 1.1236 = 0.00515 per ₹1 with no risk. Everyone would do this until the forward rate falls back to 7.01%.

11.10 Yield to maturity (YTM) vs spot rates

YTM yThe single discount rate that makes PV of all of a bond's payments equal its market price: P₀ = Σ C_t/(1+y)^t. It is the bond's IRR.

Spot rates are actual market prices for single-dated cash flows. YTM is a kind of average of spot rates, weighted by when the cash arrives.

Example: 2-year bond, coupon 5, face 100; spot r₁ = 5%, r₂ = 6%Price = 5/1.05 + 105/1.06² = 4.762 + 93.450 = 98.21. Solving 98.21 = 5/(1+y) + 105/(1+y)² gives y = 5.98%, between 5% and 6% and closer to 6% because most cash (105) comes at year 2.

11.11 Why does the curve slope? Two theories

1. Pure Expectations Hypothesis (PEH)
Forward rate = expected future spot rate: f_(t−1,t) = E[1-year rate at t−1]. Investors are risk-neutral and don't care about maturity. So an upward curve means the market expects rates to rise. (In the example, f₁,₂ = 7.01% means the market expects next year's 1-year rate to be about 7.01%.)
2. Liquidity Preference (term premium)
Long bonds are more price-volatile and less liquid, so risk-averse investors demand extra: f_(t−1,t) = E[1-year rate] + TP_t, with TP growing with maturity. The curve slopes upward even if future rates are expected to stay flat.
Numbers: expected 1-year rate flat at 5%, term premium on year 2 = 0.5%PEH: f₁,₂ = 5% → r₂ = √(1.05 × 1.05) − 1 = 5%. With a premium: f₁,₂ = 5.5% → r₂ = √(1.05 × 1.055) − 1 = 5.250%, an upward-sloping curve although no rise is expected.
Exam point: to infer expectations from the yield curve you must separate the expected change in rates from the term premium. A rising curve alone does not prove rates are expected to rise.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the APR as the monthly-compounded effective rate (or dividing a long-run return by years).
  • Mixing nominal cash flows with real rates (or the reverse).
  • Applying "IRR > r" to a borrowing project, or trusting an IRR when signs change more than once.
  • Ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR or PI.
  • Using the arithmetic average of returns instead of the annualised (geometric) return.
  • Using one rate for all maturities when the yield curve is not flat.

Likely exam questions and what to write

  • Compute EMI and the first-month split; explain early repayment. Annuity equation with monthly rate, then interest = rate × balance, balance = PV of remaining payments.
  • Derive perpetuity and annuity without series. Shift argument: 1 + P = (1+r)P; add and subtract end payment for annuity.
  • Derive Fisher and show nominal and real discounting agree. Substitute C_nom = C_real(1+π)^t and 1+r_nom = (1+r_real)(1+π).
  • Derive continuous compounding. m = n/r substitution and Euler's limit.
  • Why is payback flawed? Give a numerical counter-example. Project A vs B (payback 2.0 vs 2.625, NPV −13.22 vs +71.38).
  • Pitfalls of IRR. Lending vs borrowing, multiple IRRs (10% and 20%), no IRR, scale.
  • PI and capital rationing. Rank by PI, fund until budget is exhausted; limitations.
  • Derive the forward rate; PEH vs liquidity preference. Two strategies, no arbitrage, f₁,₂ = 7.01% example.
  • Annualised vs average return. Examples 4 and 5 (0% vs 25% average; −2.02% annualised).
