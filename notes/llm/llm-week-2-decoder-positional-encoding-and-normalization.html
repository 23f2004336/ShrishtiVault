1. Quick recap from Week 1

  • A sentence of $T$ words is a matrix of embeddings $H$ ($d_{model}=512$ each).
  • Self-attention: $q=W_Qh,\ k=W_Kh,\ v=W_Vh$ and $Z=\text{softmax}\!\big(\tfrac{Q^TK}{\sqrt{d_k}}\big)V^T$. Output is $T\times d_v$.
  • Multi-head: 8 heads of size 64, concatenated to 512 and mixed by $W^O$.
  • Encoder layer = multi-head attention + position-wise FFN ($512\to2048\to512$); the encoder is a stack of $N=6$ layers, giving outputs $e_1..e_T$ of size $T\times512$.
ConventionsThe slides continue with columns-as-vectors notation ($Q^TK$). Where a formula is written for row-vectors ($QK^T$), it is the same computation. $T$ = source length, $T_{dec}$ = target length.

2.3 Final output

The top decoder layer produces a vector of size 512 for each target position. 3. Teacher forcing

3.1 Why is the target sentence an input to the decoder?

Usually, to predict word $t$ the decoder uses its own previous prediction as input. The drawback: if the first prediction is wrong, the next prediction is conditioned on a wrong word and is likely wrong too (because of conditional probability), and so on. Errors accumulate ("garbage in, garbage out"). An untrained model is wrong most of the time, so learning would be very slow.

Teacher forcingDuring training, feed the decoder the correct previous word from the ground truth at every step, whatever the model predicted. The "teacher" forces the right context.

Slide example (English target): ground truth "I enjoyed the film transformers"; the decoder $D$ at each step receives the true previous word, not its own guess.

4. Masked (causal) self-attention

4.1 The setting

Masked self-attention is the same as encoder self-attention: $Q=W_QH,\ K=W_KH,\ V=W_VH$ where $H$ now holds the target word embeddings ($\langle Go\rangle$, Naan, transfarmar, ...). The one important difference: implement teacher forcing without leaking the future, by adding a mask.

In the score matrix $A=Q^TK$ (row $t$ = query of word $t$, column $j$ = key of word $j$), entry $(t,j)$ is how much position $t$ wants to look at $j$. For a language model, position $t$ may look only at $j\le t$.

4.2 The mask matrix

Slide questions: how do we create the mask, and where do we put it: input, output, or in between? Answer: add a matrix $M$ to the scores, before the softmax.

$$M_{t,j}=\begin{cases}0 & j\le t\\ -\infty & j>t\end{cases}\qquad Z=\text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{Q^TK}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\Big)V^T$$

$M$ is triangular: zeros on and below the diagonal, $-\infty$ above (slide: "one half all zeros and the other half all negative infinity"). Example for the 5 target words:

4.3 Why this makes the weights exactly zero

$$\alpha_{t,j}=\frac{\exp(e_{t,j}+M_{t,j})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T}\exp(e_{t,k}+M_{t,k})},\qquad j>t:\ \ \exp(e_{t,j}-\infty)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha_{t,j}=0$$

So the value vectors $v_j$ of future words receive weight 0 and contribute nothing to $z_t$, while the remaining weights still sum to 1 (the denominator only counts allowed positions).

Why not mask the input or output?Setting an embedding to 0 does not remove a word: its score becomes $e=0$ and $\exp(0)=1$, still a non-zero weight. Zeroing the weights after softmax breaks the sum-to-1 property. Adding $-\infty$ to the scores before softmax is the clean way. (In code, $-\infty$ is a very large negative number such as $-10^9$; every row keeps its diagonal entry, so a row is never all $-\infty$.)

4.5 Masking is needed in every decoder layer

Suppose layer 1 masks correctly, so $S^{(1)}_{t+1}$ contains information from words $1..t+1$ (its own word included). If layer 2 did not mask, position $t$ in layer 2 could attend to $S^{(1)}_{t+1}$, and through it learn about word $t+1$: the future leaks in indirectly. Therefore the causal mask is required in the masked self-attention of every decoder layer.

Masks in the encoder tooThe slide notes the encoder also uses masking in practice, but for a different reason: to mask the padding tokens in sentences shorter than the longest sentence in a batch (length $<T$). Padding positions get $-\infty$ so nobody attends to them. This is a padding mask, not a causal mask.

5. Multi-head cross-attention (encoder-decoder attention)

5.1 Idea

Masked self-attention lets the decoder relate target words to each other. But translation needs the source. Cross-attention is exactly the Week 1 seq2seq attention, upgraded to Q/K/V: each target position asks a question about the source and receives a weighted mix of source information.

7. Why we need positional encoding

7.1 The problem

In an RNN, word order was built into the hidden states: $h_t$ is computed after $h_{t-1}$. In a Transformer, all words enter at once and are combined by dot products. Neither the encoder nor the decoder has any information about which word came first. The slide states that the output of self-attention is permutation-invariant (more precisely, permutation-equivariant: shuffling the input shuffles the output identically).

Example: "I am home today" versus "I today home am". Without extra information the contextual vector for "home" is the same in both; only its position in the output list moves.

7.2 Proof

Let $P\in\mathbb R^{T\times T}$ be a permutation matrix (each row and column has a single 1), so $P^TP=I$. Use row-vector form with $H\in\mathbb R^{T\times d}$ and $Q=HW_Q$, etc. Shuffle the input: $\tilde H=PH$.

  1. Projections are row-wise linear maps, so they permute rows: $\tilde Q=PHW_Q=PQ$, $\tilde K=PK$, $\tilde V=PV$.
  2. Scores: $\tilde Q\tilde K^T=(PQ)(PK)^T=PQK^TP^T$. The rows and columns of the score matrix are permuted the same way.
  3. Softmax acts on each row independently and is unaffected by column order, so $\text{softmax}(PSP^T)=P\,\text{softmax}(S)\,P^T$. Therefore $\tilde A=PAP^T$.
  4. Output: $\tilde Z=\tilde A\tilde V=(PAP^T)(PV)=PA(P^TP)V=PAV=PZ$.
$$\tilde Z=PZ\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{shuffled input}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{identically shuffled output, same numbers}$$

The model literally cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog". Since word order carries meaning, we must inject it explicitly.

The planGive each position $p$ its own vector $PE_p\in\mathbb R^{512}$ and add it to the word embedding: $X_{final,p}=X_{word,p}+PE_p$. Now the same word at two positions gets two different inputs, which breaks the symmetry. (The slide shows the position vector being added to the 512-d embedding, element by element.)

8. How could we build a positional vector? Candidate methods

The slide asks: how do we fill the elements of $p_j$? Could it be a constant? One-hot? Learned? Each has a flaw.

9. Sinusoidal positional encoding

9.1 Definition

Hypothesis (slide): embed a unique pattern of features for each position $j$, and the model will learn to attend by relative position. The features are sines and cosines:

$$PE_{(p,2i)}=\sin\!\Big(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\Big),\qquad PE_{(p,2i+1)}=\cos\!\Big(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\Big)$$
  • $p$ = position of the word ($p=0,1,2,\dots,T-1$; the slide calls it $j$).
  • $i=0,1,\dots,\tfrac{d_{model}}2-1$ = index of a pair of dimensions (for $d_{model}=512$: $i=0..255$, which is the range written on the slide).
  • Even dimensions $2i$ use sin; odd dimensions $2i+1$ use cos of the same angle.
  • Define the frequency $\omega_i=\dfrac{1}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}$, so the pair is $(\sin\omega_ip,\ \cos\omega_ip)$.

9.2 Position 0

At $p=0$: $\sin0=0$, $\cos0=1$, so $PE_0=[0,1,0,1,\dots,0,1]$, alternating 0's and 1's (slide). This vector is added to the first word of every sentence.

9.3 Tiny worked example (created for practice, $d_{model}=4$)

Two pairs: $i=0$: $\omega_0=10000^0=1$; $i=1$: $\omega_1=10000^{-2/4}=0.01$. Then $PE_p=[\sin p,\ \cos p,\ \sin0.01p,\ \cos0.01p]$.

9.4 Fast and slow waves: wavelength

The wave $\sin(\omega_ip)$ repeats every $\lambda_i=\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_i}=2\pi\cdot10000^{2i/d_{model}}$ positions.

  • Low $i$ (near 0): denominator $\approx10000^0=1$, wavelength $\approx2\pi\approx6.3$: a high-frequency wave that changes rapidly between neighbouring words.
  • High $i$ (near $d/2$): denominator $\to10000$, wavelength up to $\approx2\pi\cdot10000\approx62{,}800$: a low-frequency wave that changes slowly and separates far-apart positions.
  • Wavelengths form a geometric progression from $2\pi$ to $10000\cdot2\pi$.
Sine curves for four dimension pairs (computed for $d_{model}=512$): fast waves resolve neighbours, slow waves resolve long distances
Analogy: a clockThe seconds hand spins fast, the minute hand slower, the hour hand slowest. Reading all hands together gives a unique time. Likewise, the many sine/cosine pairs together give each position a unique fingerprint, and it works for any length because nothing is learned or capped.

9.6 Key properties (with proofs)

(a) Bounded. Every entry lies in $[-1,1]$, so $PE$ can be added to embeddings without overwhelming them (unlike a raw counter). Also, since $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ for each pair, $\|PE_p\|^2=d_{model}/2=256$ for every position, so all positional vectors have the same length 16.

(b) Relative position is a linear operation. Using the angle-addition formulas, the pair at position $p+k$ is a fixed rotation of the pair at position $p$:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega(p+k)\\ \cos\omega(p+k)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega k & \sin\omega k\\ -\sin\omega k & \cos\omega k\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega p\\ \cos\omega p\end{pmatrix}$$

The rotation matrix depends only on the offset $k$, not on $p$. So $PE_{p+k}=R_k\,PE_p$ for a fixed matrix $R_k$. Attention (which is built from linear maps) can therefore learn to look "$k$ words back" by learning a suitable weight matrix. This is why the hypothesis says the model "will learn to attend by relative position".

(c) Distance depends only on the offset, and is symmetric. For one pair, $(\sin a-\sin b)^2+(\cos a-\cos b)^2=2-2\cos(a-b)$. Summing over all pairs with $a=\omega_ip$, $b=\omega_i(p+k)$:

$$\|PE_p-PE_{p+k}\|^2=\sum_{i=0}^{d/2-1}2\big(1-\cos(\omega_ik)\big)$$

The right side does not contain $p$: the distance between two positions depends only on how far apart they are. And because $\cos$ is even, replacing $k$ by $-k$ changes nothing: distance to $p+k$ equals distance to $p-k$ (symmetry). For $k=0$ it is 0.

The slide plots the distance from position 0 to every other position: it rises quickly at first (nearby positions are close, and distance increases as we move away) and then flattens, since the slow waves change little. Computing the exact formula above for $d_{model}=512$ gives distance 3.7 at $p=1$, 12.8 at $p=10$ and 17.0 at $p=100$:

Distance from PE0 to PEp (computed from the formula). The vertical scale on the lecture slide is different because of its plotting code; the shape is the point.

By contrast, one-hot vectors always have distance $\sqrt2$, independent of position, so they carry no notion of "nearer".

Do not confuseThe distance matrix in section 8 (0,1,2,3,4) is the wish-list. The real PE distances are not equal to word counts; they grow smoothly with the offset and flatten for large offsets. What matters is that they depend only on the offset and are symmetric.

9.7 Practical notes

  • PE is added (not concatenated) to the embedding, keeping size 512.
  • The input word embeddings are learned during training (no pretrained Word2Vec), which is an extra set of weights; the positional function can also be parameterised (learned) instead of sinusoidal.
  • PE is added at the bottom of both the encoder input and the decoder input.

10. Training deep Transformers: residual connections

10.1 How deep is the network?

Slide's rough count: think of the Transformer as attention layers and hidden (linear) layers. One encoder layer = 1 attention layer + 2 hidden layers (the two FFN matrices) = 3. One decoder layer = 2 attention layers + 2 hidden layers = 4.

$$6\times3\ (\text{encoder})+6\times4\ (\text{decoder})=18+24=\mathbf{42}\ \text{layers deep}$$

(The summary counts sub-layers: $6\times2=12$ in the encoder and $6\times3=18$ in the decoder, and notes over 42 weight operations when the FFN's two matrices are counted.) Two questions arise: how do we make gradients flow across 42 layers? and how do we speed up training? Answers: residual connections and normalization.

10.3 Why it fixes vanishing gradients

Backpropagation multiplies gradients layer by layer. With residuals:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\text{Output}}\Big(I+\frac{\partial\,\text{SubLayer}(x)}{\partial x}\Big)$$

The identity $I$ passes the upstream gradient through untouched. Even if $\partial\text{SubLayer}/\partial x$ is tiny, the gradient does not vanish. Across $L$ layers the product $\prod_l(I+J_l)$ expands to $I+\sum_lJ_l+\dots$, which always contains the direct term $I$: a clear path from the loss to the earliest layer, regardless of depth. (Contrast with Week 1's RNN product of Jacobians without $I$, which shrinks or explodes.)

A second benefit: each sub-layer only has to learn a correction to its input, which is easier to optimise.

11. Normalization: Batch Norm vs Layer Norm

11.1 Why normalize?

As layers change during training, the scale and distribution of activations drift, which slows and destabilises learning. Normalization re-centres and re-scales activations to keep them well behaved and to speed up training.

11.2 Batch normalization (BN)

Let $x_i^j$ = activation of neuron $i$ for training sample $j$ in layer $l$. Three variables are involved ($l$, $i$, $j$): the layer, the neuron, and the sample. BN normalizes neuron $i$ across the $m$ samples of the batch:

$$\mu_i=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_i^j,\qquad\sigma_i^2=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}(x_i^j-\mu_i)^2,\qquad\hat x_i=\frac{x_i-\mu_i}{\sqrt{\sigma_i^2+\epsilon}},\qquad\hat y_i=\gamma\hat x_i+\beta$$

$\epsilon$ is a tiny constant preventing division by zero; $\gamma,\beta$ are learned scale and shift so the network can undo the normalization if that is better. In the lecture picture, an accumulator stores the activations of the batch as a grid and the statistics are taken down each column (neuron), one neuron at a time.

11.3 Why BN is a poor fit for Transformers/NLP

  • Depends on batch size $m$. Mean and variance estimates are accurate only if $m$ is large; small $m$ gives high error. With $m=1$: $\mu_i=x_i$, $\sigma_i=0$, so $\hat x_i=0$ for every input: all information is erased. Inference on a single sentence is therefore a problem.
  • Variable sequence lengths and padding distort the statistics (sentences in a batch have different lengths).
  • Empirical degradation: naive BN was found to hurt performance on language tasks. A systematic study confirmed this and proposed a modified BN called PowerNorm.

Numeric illustration (created for practice): a neuron has activation 1, 2, 3 in a batch of 3. $\mu=2$, $\sigma^2=\tfrac{(1+0+1)}3=0.667$. Fine. In a batch of 1 with activation 5: $\mu=5$, $\sigma^2=0$, $\hat x=0$.

11.4 Layer normalization (LN)

LN takes the statistics across the hidden units of one token, not across the batch. In the Transformer, for token $t$ of sample $b$ with $d_{model}$ features:

$$\mu_{b,t}=\frac1{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{b,t,i},\qquad\sigma_{b,t}^2=\frac1{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(X_{b,t,i}-\mu_{b,t})^2$$ $$\text{LN}(X_{b,t})=\gamma\odot\frac{X_{b,t}-\mu_{b,t}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{b,t}^2+\epsilon}}+\beta,\qquad\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb R^{d}\ \text{(learned)}$$

Each token is normalised on its own, so the result does not depend on batch size or on the other sentences. It works with batch size 1 (needed for RNNs, and for autoregressive decoding one token at a time) and is unaffected by padding.

12. Putting Add & Norm in the layer: PostNorm vs PreNorm

The slide "the complete layer": add a residual connection and layer norm after every multi-head attention, feed-forward network and cross-attention block. There are two ways to arrange the pieces.

PostNorm: $X_1=\text{LN}(X+\text{MHA}(X)),\quad X_2=\text{LN}(X_1+\text{FFN}(X_1))$
PreNorm: $X_1=X+\text{MHA}(\text{LN}(X)),\quad X_2=X_1+\text{FFN}(\text{LN}(X_1))$
Why PreNorm is popular in modern LLMsIn PostNorm the residual signal passes through a LayerNorm in every layer, which can damp the backpropagated gradient in deep stacks. PreNorm normalizes only the branch that goes through the sub-layer and leaves the main skip path (the "highway") untouched, so gradients flow cleanly. This allows stable training without a learning-rate warm-up schedule.

13.1 One full training step, in words

  1. Embed source words, add $PE$, run the 6 encoder layers to get $E$.
  2. Take the target sentence, shift right (start with <Go>), embed, add $PE$.
  3. Decoder layer: masked self-attention (with $M$) → cross-attention to $E$ → FFN, each followed by Add & LayerNorm.
  4. Linear + softmax at every position at once; compare with the true next words; sum the cross-entropy; backpropagate through everything (residual paths keep gradients healthy).
  5. At inference, generate one word at a time, feeding each output back as the next input, until an end token.

14.4 Practice questions

  1. Why does the decoder have three sub-layers? Masked self-attention (relate target words), cross-attention (use the source), FFN (per-word non-linearity).
  2. What is teacher forcing and what problem does it solve? Feeding true previous words in training; stops one early mistake from corrupting all later predictions and speeds up learning.
  3. Where is the causal mask applied and why is $-\infty$ used? Added to the scaled scores before softmax; $\exp(-\infty)=0$ gives exact zero weight while the other weights still sum to 1.
  4. Why mask in every decoder layer? Otherwise layer-2 position $t$ can read layer-1 state $t+1$, which already contains word $t+1$: indirect leakage.
  5. Which sequence supplies Q, and which K and V, in cross-attention? Score matrix shape? Q: decoder; K, V: encoder output; $T_{dec}\times T_{enc}$.
  6. Prove that self-attention alone cannot see word order. Show $\tilde Z=PZ$ for input $PH$ (section 7.2).
  7. Why do one-hot and counter encodings fail? One-hot: all distances $\sqrt2$, fixed length. Counter: unbounded values. Learned: cannot extrapolate.
  8. Why does the sinusoidal PE encode relative position? $PE_{p+k}=R_kPE_p$ with $R_k$ a rotation depending only on $k$; distances depend only on $k$ and are symmetric.
  9. How do residual connections stop vanishing gradients? Gradient contains $I+\partial\text{Sub}/\partial x$, so the identity path always survives.
  10. Why Layer Norm, not Batch Norm? BN needs large batches, breaks at batch size 1, is hurt by padding/variable length, and degrades NLP performance; LN normalizes per token, independent of batch.
  11. PreNorm vs PostNorm? PreNorm keeps the residual path clean, stable for deep models without warm-up.
