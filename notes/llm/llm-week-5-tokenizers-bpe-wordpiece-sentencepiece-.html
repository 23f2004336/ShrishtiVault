1. Why tokenization matters

1.1 A language model only sees numbers

A neural network cannot read letters. It multiplies matrices. So text must first become a list of integers (token IDs), and each ID selects a row of an embedding table. The tokenizer defines two things at once:

  • The vocabulary $V$: the fixed list of pieces the model knows. $|V|$ is its size.
  • The rule that turns any string into a sequence of pieces from $V$.

1.2 Where $|V|$ costs us

Two parts of the model grow with $|V|$:

$$\text{Embedding table}=|V|\times d\ \text{parameters},\qquad \text{Output layer (softmax) cost per token}=O(|V|\cdot d)$$

Why the second one? At every position the model must give a probability to every vocabulary item. The final layer multiplies the hidden vector ($d$ numbers) by a $d\times|V|$ matrix, which takes $|V|\cdot d$ multiplications, and then the softmax sums $|V|$ exponentials. A bigger vocabulary means a bigger table and slower, more memory-hungry output computation.

And one part grows with sequence length $N$: self-attention compares every token with every other token, so cost is $O(N^2)$ per layer. A tokenizer that produces more tokens for the same text is therefore expensive too. A good tokenizer balances the two: small enough $|V|$, short enough $N$.

The central trade-offSmall vocabulary → long sequences (costly attention, little meaning per token). Large vocabulary → short sequences but a huge embedding table and output layer, and many rarely seen tokens that never get trained well.

2. Word-level tokenization and why it fails

The most natural idea: split on spaces and punctuation, make every distinct word a token. The slides list five serious problems.

2.1 Problem 1: parameter explosion

English alone has hundreds of thousands of word forms (run, runs, running, ran, runner, unrunnable ...). Suppose $|V|=500{,}000$ and embedding size $d=768$.

$$500{,}000\times768=384{,}000{,}000\ \text{parameters just for the input embeddings}$$

(With $d=1024$ as in the lecture summary this is $512{,}000{,}000$.) The output layer is the same size again if not tied. Compare a 32,000-piece subword vocabulary: $32{,}000\times768\approx24.6$ million. The softmax cost per token shrinks by the same ratio, $500{,}000/32{,}000\approx15.6$ times. Many of those words are rare, so their embeddings are barely trained.

2.2 Problem 2: out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words

A fixed word list can never contain tomorrow's words: new names, product names, slang, typos, other languages. Anything missing becomes [UNK], and the model loses all information about it ("The patient was given [UNK]" could be a harmless vitamin or a dangerous drug).

2.3 Problem 3: misspellings

"definately", "recieve", "gooood": each wrong spelling is a different string, so each is either [UNK] or a nearly untrained rare entry.

2.4 Problem 4: morphological redundancy

enjoy, enjoyed, enjoying, enjoyable, enjoyment are five unrelated rows in the table. The model has to learn from scratch that they share a meaning. A tokenizer that kept enjoy + a suffix would share information automatically.

2.5 Problem 5: languages without spaces

Japanese, Chinese and Thai do not put spaces between words. "Split on whitespace" then gives a whole sentence as one "word". A word segmenter would be needed first (and word boundaries are themselves debatable).

Summary of word levelFertility is the best possible (1 token per word, short sequences) but the vocabulary is huge, still incomplete, and wasteful on related word forms.

3. Character-level tokenization and why it fails

The opposite extreme: the vocabulary is just the characters (about 100 for English with punctuation, a few thousand for Chinese). There is no OOV problem at all and the embedding table is tiny. But:

3.1 Sequences get much longer

An English word averages roughly 5–6 characters including the space. A 20-word sentence becomes about $20\times6=120$ tokens.

3.2 Attention cost explodes (derivation)

Self-attention computes one score for every pair of positions, an $N\times N$ matrix, so its cost is proportional to $N^2$. Compare the same sentence at the two levels:

$$\frac{\text{cost}_{\text{char}}}{\text{cost}_{\text{word}}}=\frac{120^2}{20^2}=\left(\frac{120}{20}\right)^2=6^2=36$$

Cutting each word into 6 pieces makes attention 36 times more expensive. In general if every word becomes $f$ tokens, attention becomes $f^2$ times more expensive. This is why fertility matters (section 4).

One 20-word sentence at three levels: tokens, attention cells, embedding parameters. Subword is the middle path (fertility 1.5 assumed).

3.3 Other problems

  • Context starvation: with a fixed context window (say 2048 tokens), characters cover far less text than words do, so the model "sees" much less of the document.
  • Low information per token: the letter "t" means almost nothing. The model must spend many layers just to assemble letters into words before it can reason about meaning.
  • Hard long-range dependencies: two related words are now about 6 times farther apart in token positions.

4. Fertility and the subword idea

4.1 Definition of fertility

$$\text{fertility}=\frac{\text{number of tokens produced}}{\text{number of words in the text}}$$
TokenizerTypical fertilityMeaning
Word level1.0One token per word
Subword (good)about 1.5 – 3.0Common words stay whole, rare words split into a few pieces
Character level5 – 6Every letter is a token

Lower fertility is cheaper (shorter sequences) but needs a bigger vocabulary. The numbers depend on the language (non-English text usually has higher fertility with an English-trained tokenizer) and on the vocabulary size, so treat them as rough guides.

4.2 The subword principle

Core idea (from the slides)Do not split frequent words into smaller pieces. Split rare words into meaningful subword pieces. A frequent word like "the" gets its own token; a rare word like "unrunnable" becomes "un" + "run" + "nable".

Why this solves the earlier problems:

  • No OOV: in the worst case any word can be spelled with single characters, which are always in the vocabulary.
  • Sharing: enjoy, ##ed, ##ing are reused across many words.
  • Controlled size: we choose $|V|$ (typically 30K–50K for English, 100K–250K for multilingual models) and the algorithm finds the best pieces for that budget.
The vocabulary-size spectrum. BPE and WordPiece start from characters and merge upward; Unigram / SentencePiece starts from a big seed and prunes downward. Both aim at the middle.

5. The pre-processing flow

Before learning any pieces the training text goes through a fixed flow (slides 12–13):

Raw corpus
↓
1. Normalize (lower-case? Unicode NFKC, strip accents)
↓
2. Pre-tokenize (split on spaces/punctuation; count word frequencies)
↓
3. Train the subword model (learn merges or token probabilities until |V| is reached)
↓
4. Save vocabulary + rules
Training flow. At inference time step 3 is replaced by "apply the learned rules to new text".

Normalization

Makes visually equal strings identical: "é" written as one character or as "e"+accent, full-width vs normal letters, curly vs straight quotes. Optionally lower-casing (which merges "The" and "the" but loses information about names).

Pre-tokenization

A first rough split, usually on whitespace and punctuation. Important consequence: merges never cross a pre-token boundary, so a piece never spans two words (in plain BPE/WordPiece). The training corpus is stored as word types with counts ("know" appears 3 times), which makes training fast: we count pairs once per distinct word and multiply by its frequency.

Post-processing

Adds special tokens (<cls>, <sep>, <stop>), truncates, pads and converts pieces to IDs.

6. Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)

6.1 The idea in one sentence

Start with every word spelled as single characters. Repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols in the corpus and merge it into one new symbol. Stop after a chosen number of merges. The list of merges (in order) is the tokenizer.

Why it worksFrequent letter sequences ("th", "ing", "est") are merged first, so they become single tokens. Rare sequences are never merged and stay as characters. Frequent whole words eventually become single tokens too. Frequency decides what deserves its own token, exactly the subword principle.

6.2 Algorithm

Input: corpus, number of merges M
↓
1. Pre-tokenize into words; write each word as characters + end marker </w>; keep word counts
↓
2. Initial vocabulary = all distinct symbols (characters and </w>)
↓
3. Count every adjacent pair, weighted by word frequency
↓
4. Pick the most frequent pair (A, B); create new symbol AB; replace every "A B" by "AB" in all words; record the merge
↓
5. Repeat 3–4 until M merges are done (or no pair occurs more than once)
↓
Output: ordered merge list + vocabulary of size (initial symbols + M)

The end-of-word marker </w> is a symbol added after every word. It lets the tokenizer distinguish "est" inside a word (estimate) from "est" at the end of a word (newest). Without it, the last letter of one word and the first letter of the next would look the same as in-word pairs, and token meaning at word ends would be lost.

$$|V_{\text{final}}|=|\text{initial symbols}|+\text{number of merges}$$

Each merge adds exactly one new symbol (the old ones stay in the vocabulary), so the vocabulary size is fully controlled by one number, the merge count.

6.3 The code from the slides

import re, collections

def get_stats(vocab):                      # count adjacent pairs
    pairs = collections.defaultdict(int)
    for word, freq in vocab.items():
        symbols = word.split()
        for i in range(len(symbols) - 1):
            pairs[symbols[i], symbols[i+1]] += freq
    return pairs

def merge_vocab(pair, v_in):               # apply one merge
    v_out = {}
    bigram = re.escape(' '.join(pair))
    p = re.compile(r'(?<!\S)' + bigram + r'(?!\S)')
    for word in v_in:
        w_out = p.sub(''.join(pair), word)
        v_out[w_out] = v_in[word]
    return v_out

for i in range(num_merges):
    pairs = get_stats(vocab)
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
    vocab = merge_vocab(best, vocab)

Reading the code. The corpus is a dictionary: key = word written with spaces between symbols, value = frequency. get_stats walks over every word, looks at each neighbouring symbol pair and adds the word's frequency. merge_vocab glues the chosen pair together using a regular expression. The odd-looking (?<!\S) and (?!\S) are "not preceded / not followed by a non-space" guards; they make sure we only match whole symbols. For example merging (e, s) must not touch the "es" hidden inside a symbol such as "res". max(pairs, key=pairs.get) returns the first pair that reaches the maximum, so ties are broken by order of appearance. (Other implementations break ties differently, which is why two correct BPE runs can differ slightly.)

6.6 Using the learned merges on new text (inference)

Training produced an ordered list of merges. To tokenize a new word:

  1. Split it into characters and add </w>.
  2. Look through the merge list in the order they were learned. Whenever the pair of the current merge appears, apply it.
  3. Stop when no learned merge applies. The remaining symbols are the tokens.

Example with the Sennrich merges.

  • lowest → l o w e s t </w> → (e,s) → (es,t) → (est,</w>) → (l,o) → (lo,w): result lowest</w>. The word "lowest" never occurred in training, yet it splits into two known meaningful pieces.
  • newer → n e w e r </w> → (n,e) → (ne,w): result newer</w>. The pair (e,r) was not among the 10 merges, so e, r stay separate. With more merges it would become newer</w>.
Order mattersThe merge rank decides, not the left-to-right position. For "hello", if both (h,e) and (e,l) were learned, the one learned earlier is applied first. Doing it in the other order could give he|l|lo instead of h|el|lo. This is why the merge list must be stored in order.

6.7 Properties of BPE

  • Greedy and deterministic: at each step the single best pair; same input always gives the same tokens.
  • No OOV: every string can be written with the base characters.
  • Frequency-driven: frequent words become tokens, rare words get split.
  • BPE-Dropout: during training of the language model, randomly skip some merges so the same word gets different segmentations. This acts as regularization and makes the model robust to odd spellings.
  • Used in GPT-1: $|V|=40{,}478$ (about 40,000 merges plus base characters and special tokens). GPT-2/3 use a byte-level version so that even unseen Unicode characters can always be represented.

6.8 Languages without spaces (Japanese example)

BPE needs pre-tokens. For Japanese, Chinese or Thai, there are no spaces, so first apply a word segmenter (for Japanese tools such as Juman++ or MeCab) to cut the sentence into words, then run BPE inside each word. The drawback is the extra dependency: the tokenizer is only as good as the segmenter, and different languages need different segmenters. SentencePiece (section 8) removes this requirement.

6.9 A "hello lol" check

The slides finish BPE with the string "hello lol": common chunks like "hello" end up as one token, informal strings like "lol" may be split into "lo" + "l", showing again that frequent things are compact and rare things are spelled out.

7. WordPiece

WordPiece (used by BERT, with $|V|\approx30{,}000$: exactly 30,522 in BERT-base) follows the same merge loop as BPE. The only change is the rule that decides which pair to merge.

7.1 Notation: the ## prefix

Pieces that continue a word (are not at its start) are written with ##. So "unaffable" may become un ##aff ##able. This replaces BPE's </w> marker: instead of marking word ends, WordPiece marks word continuations. When decoding, remove "##" and glue the piece to the previous one.

7.2 The score

$$\text{score}(A,B)=\frac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)\times\text{count}(B)}$$

BPE would merge the pair with the largest $\text{count}(AB)$. WordPiece merges the pair with the largest score. The score is high when A and B appear together much more often than they appear apart.

7.3 Why this score? (derivation from the likelihood)

WordPiece's goal is to choose merges that increase the likelihood of the training data under a unigram-style model. Let $N$ be the total number of tokens in the corpus. Model every token independently with probability equal to its relative frequency: $p(X)=\text{count}(X)/N$.

Step 1. Before the merge each occurrence of the pair A B costs $\log p(A)+\log p(B)$ in the log-likelihood. After the merge it becomes one token costing $\log p(AB)$. There are $\text{count}(AB)$ such places, so the gain is

$$\Delta\mathcal L\approx\text{count}(AB)\,\big[\log p(AB)-\log p(A)-\log p(B)\big]=\text{count}(AB)\,\log\frac{p(AB)}{p(A)\,p(B)}$$

Step 2. The quantity inside the log is the pointwise mutual information (PMI) of the pair: it is positive when A and B co-occur more than chance, zero if independent. Substitute $p(X)=\text{count}(X)/N$ (and $p(AB)=\text{count}(AB)/N$):

$$\frac{p(AB)}{p(A)p(B)}=\frac{\text{count}(AB)/N}{\text{count}(A)\,\text{count}(B)/N^2}=N\cdot\frac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)\,\text{count}(B)}$$

Step 3. $N$ is the same for every candidate pair, so ranking pairs by the ratio $\dfrac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)\,\text{count}(B)}$ ranks them by their PMI. That is exactly the WordPiece score. (The exact likelihood gain also multiplies by $\text{count}(AB)$, but the slides use the plain ratio; it favours pairs of rare pieces that nearly always occur together.)

IntuitionBPE asks "which pair is most common?" and so merges things like "e"+"s" (both letters are everywhere). WordPiece asks "which pair sticks together most strongly compared with how common its parts are?" Two very common letters get a low score because they are each seen a lot on their own.

7.4b Inference with WordPiece

WordPiece tokenizes a new word with greedy longest-match-first: take the longest prefix that is in the vocabulary, cut it off, then continue with the rest using "##" pieces. Example: "unaffable" → "un" (longest known prefix) + "##aff" + "##able". If no piece at all matches, the whole word becomes [UNK].

8. SentencePiece and the Unigram language model

8.1 Two ideas with one name

SentencePiece is a tool (a library). It does two things that fix BPE/WordPiece's weak spots:

  • It reads raw text with no pre-tokenization. Spaces are not special separators; they are turned into a visible symbol ▁ and treated like any other character. "Hello world" becomes ▁Hello ▁world. So Japanese, Chinese and Thai need no external word segmenter, and decoding is lossless (join the pieces and turn ▁ back into spaces).
  • It can train with the Unigram LM algorithm (the default idea in this lecture), which works top-down. (It can also run BPE.)

8.2 Unigram model: probability of a segmentation

Every token $t$ in the vocabulary has a probability $p(t)$ with $\sum_t p(t)=1$. A segmentation $x=(x_1,\dots,x_m)$ of a word is a list of tokens whose concatenation is the word. Assuming tokens are independent (the "unigram" assumption):

$$P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p(x_i),\qquad x^*=\arg\max_{x\in S(X)}P(x)$$

$S(X)$ is the set of all ways to cut word $X$ into vocabulary tokens. The best segmentation $x^*$ is the most probable one.

Worked example from the slides ("knowing"). Three candidate cuts:

Fewer, bigger tokens usually give a higher product, because each extra token multiplies by a number below 1.

Using the slide's token probabilities, $P(\text{k, now, ing})=63/4096=0.0154$, $P(\text{know, ing})=336/4096=0.0820$ and $P(\text{knowing})=768/4096=0.1875$. Taking natural logs: $-4.175,\ -2.501,\ -1.674$. The single token "knowing" wins. Logs are used because a product of many small numbers underflows and sums are easier to handle (the log turns $\prod$ into $\sum$ and keeps the same winner since log is increasing).

8.3 How many segmentations are there? (derivation)

A word with $K$ characters has $K-1$ gaps between neighbouring characters. For each gap we make an independent choice: cut here or not. That gives

$$|S(X)|\le 2^{K-1}$$

"whereby" has $K=7$ so at most $2^6=64$ cuts, but only cuts where every piece is in the vocabulary are valid. With the slide vocabulary 13 of them are valid. A 20-letter word has up to $2^{19}\approx524{,}000$ cuts. We cannot list them all, which is why we need dynamic programming (Viterbi, section 8.5).

8.4 Training with EM (Expectation–Maximization)

The chicken-and-egg problem. To pick the best segmentations we need token probabilities. To estimate token probabilities we need segmentations. The segmentation is a hidden variable. This is exactly the setting where EM works.

Likelihood. For a corpus of words $X_1,\dots,X_n$, since we do not know which segmentation produced each word, we add the probability of all of them:

$$\mathcal L=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\log P(X_s)=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\log\sum_{x\in S(X_s)}P(x)$$

E-step. With current probabilities, compute for each word the posterior of each segmentation and use it to get expected counts of each token:

$$P(x\mid X_s)=\frac{P(x)}{\sum_{x'\in S(X_s)}P(x')},\qquad c(t)=\sum_{s}\ \sum_{x\in S(X_s)}P(x\mid X_s)\cdot n_t(x)$$

where $n_t(x)$ is how many times token $t$ appears in segmentation $x$. (If a word occurs $f$ times, multiply by $f$.)

M-step (derivation). Choose probabilities that maximize $\sum_t c(t)\log p(t)$ subject to $\sum_t p(t)=1$. Use a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p(t)}\Big[\sum_{t'}c(t')\log p(t')-\lambda\big(\textstyle\sum_{t'}p(t')-1\big)\Big]=\frac{c(t)}{p(t)}-\lambda=0\ \Rightarrow\ p(t)=\frac{c(t)}{\lambda}$$

Summing over all $t$ and using $\sum p=1$ gives $\lambda=\sum_{t'}c(t')$. So

$$p(t)=\frac{c(t)}{\sum_{t'}c(t')}$$

In words: the new probability of a token is its (expected) count divided by the total of all counts. Each EM round never decreases $\mathcal L$.

Hard vs soft EMThe lecture describes the simple version: in the E-step use only the best (Viterbi) segmentation of each word and count tokens in it. This "hard EM" is cheaper. The derivation above is the full "soft" EM that weights all segmentations; the M-step formula (count / total) is identical.

8.5 The Viterbi algorithm (finding $x^*$ without listing everything)

Goal. Find the best segmentation of a word of $K$ characters $C_1C_2\dots C_K$.

Define $dp[j]$ = the best (largest) log-probability of any valid segmentation of the first $j$ characters. Base case $dp[0]=0$ (empty prefix, log 1).

Recurrence (derivation). Any segmentation of the first $j$ characters ends with some last token that covers characters $i+1$ to $j$. Before that token, the prefix of length $i$ was segmented on its own. Because tokens are independent, log-probabilities add, so the best total with that last token is $dp[i]+\log p(C_{i+1..j})$. We try every possible start $i$ and keep the best:

$$dp[j]=\max_{\substack{0\le i\lt j\\ C_{i+1..j}\in V}}\Big(dp[i]+\log p\big(C_{i+1..j}\big)\Big),\qquad \text{parent}[j]=\arg\max i$$

Why is keeping only the best prefix enough? Because the best way to finish at $j$ via last token $C_{i+1..j}$ always uses the best prefix of length $i$ (a worse prefix could only make the sum smaller). This "optimal substructure" is what lets us avoid the $2^{K-1}$ search.

Backtrace. Start at $j=K$, read parent[K] to find the last token, jump to that $i$, repeat until 0, then reverse the list.

Cost. For each $j$ we try at most $j$ starts, so $O(K^2)$ lookups. If tokens have maximum length $L$, only starts with $j-i\le L$ matter, giving $O(K\cdot L)$, which is linear in the word length.

8.6 Building the vocabulary: seed, prune, repeat

1. Seed vocabulary (big, e.g. 100K–1M): all single characters + the most frequent substrings (from a BPE run or an Extended Suffix Array)
↓
2. E-step / M-step (EM): estimate token probabilities p(t) from counts (hard version: counts from Viterbi segmentation)
↓
3. Compute loss drop for every token: how much would the total log-likelihood fall if this token were deleted (and Viterbi re-segments with the rest)?
↓
4. Prune the x% (usually 20–25%) of tokens with the smallest drop. Never remove single characters (needed to spell anything)
↓
5. Repeat 2–4 until |V| reaches the target (e.g. 32,000). Final EM pass to fix probabilities

8.8 Key properties

  • Probabilistic: it gives a probability for every segmentation, not just one. So we can sample a segmentation from $P(x)$ during training (subword regularization), like BPE-dropout.
  • Language-agnostic: no whitespace assumption, no external segmenter.
  • Top-down: starts from a rich vocabulary and keeps what the data supports.
  • Used in: T5, ALBERT, XLNet, many multilingual models (via SentencePiece).

9. Morfessor (a different, linguistic view)

Morfessor splits words into morphemes (meaningful parts: prefix, stem, suffix) rather than frequency chunks. The slide example is the long word sesquipedalophobia (fear of long words). It uses a Bayesian objective:

$$\hat M=\arg\max_{M}\ P(M\mid D)=\arg\max_M\ P(D\mid M)\,P(M)$$
  • MLE (maximum likelihood) alone would only maximize $P(D\mid M)$, the fit to the data, and tends to memorize every whole word as one token.
  • MAP (maximum a posteriori) also uses a prior $P(M)$ that favours small, simple morpheme lexicons. Prior knowledge pushes towards reusable parts.
  • It can use a small annotated set (semi-supervised) to guide the segmentation, which helps in low-resource languages.

Takeaway: BPE/WordPiece/Unigram optimise for compression or likelihood; Morfessor optimises for linguistic sense. Subword tokenizers often discover morphemes (like "ing", "est") by accident because those are frequent.

Choosing the vocabulary size

  • Too small: long sequences (high fertility, slow attention, little meaning per token).
  • Too large: big embedding table and softmax, rare tokens rarely trained, so poor embeddings.
  • Rules of thumb: 30K–50K for one language, 100K–250K for multilingual (more scripts need more room). Measure fertility per language on real text and compare.
  • The vocabulary and the model are tied: a model must always be used with the tokenizer it was trained with.

Practice questions

  1. Give two problems of word-level and two of character-level tokenization. Word: parameter explosion, OOV. Char: ~6× longer sequences, 36× attention.
  2. If a sentence has 20 words and fertility 3, how many tokens? By what factor does attention cost grow vs word level? 60 tokens; $3^2=9\times$.
  3. Why add </w> in BPE? To separate word-final pieces (newest) from word-internal ones.
  4. BPE vocabulary size after 45 merges starting from 22 symbols? 67.
  5. Why does WordPiece use count(AB)/(count(A)count(B))? It is proportional to PMI, which is the per-occurrence likelihood gain of the merge.
  6. Compute the WordPiece score for (n,g) when count(ng)=7, count(n)=13, count(g)=7. $7/(13\cdot7)=0.077$.
  7. How many ways can a 10-letter word be segmented at most? $2^9=512$.
  8. Write the Viterbi recurrence and its cost. $dp[j]=\max_i(dp[i]+\log p(C_{i+1..j}))$; $O(K\cdot L)$.
  9. Derive $p(t)=c(t)/\sum c$. Maximize $\sum c\log p$ with $\sum p=1$ via Lagrange; $c/p=\lambda$.
  10. Why are single characters never pruned in Unigram? They guarantee that any string can still be segmented (no OOV).
  11. What does SentencePiece's ▁ do? Makes the space a normal symbol, so no pre-tokenizer is needed and decoding is lossless.
  12. Why is a bigger |V| not always better? Bigger embeddings/softmax, and rare tokens are poorly trained.
