0. Roadmap of the week

Week 3 gave us a trained language model (GPT). Week 4 answers two follow-up questions.

  1. Given a trained language model, how do we actually produce text from it? The model only gives a probability for the next word. We must choose words one by one. The way we choose is called a decoding strategy: exhaustive search, greedy search, beam search (all deterministic), and top-k, temperature, top-p (all stochastic).
  2. What if we need to understand text in both directions (fill in blanks, find names, answer questions)? GPT only reads left to right. BERT uses the Transformer encoder with masked language modelling and next-sentence prediction, then adapts to tasks such as classification and extractive question answering.

2. The decoding problem

2.1 What the model gives us

At step $t$, given the context $(x_1,\dots,x_t)$, the Transformer outputs a vector of raw scores called logits $u_{t+1}\in\mathbb R^{|V|}$, one per vocabulary word. A softmax turns them into probabilities:

$$P_\theta(x_{t+1}=v\mid x_1,\dots,x_t)=\text{softmax}(u_{t+1})_v=\frac{\exp(u_{t+1,v})}{\sum_{w\in V}\exp(u_{t+1,w})}$$

The model never "writes" a word by itself; it only supplies this probability list. Decoding is the rule we use to turn the list into an actual word, and repeating it gives a sentence.

3. Exhaustive search

3.1 The objective

We would like the single most probable sequence. By the chain rule the probability of a whole sequence is the product of the step probabilities, so the best sequence is

$$X^*=\arg\max_{X\in V^T}\ P(x_1,\dots,x_T)=\arg\max_{X\in V^T}\ \prod_{t=1}^{T}P(x_t\mid x_1,\dots,x_{t-1})$$

Exhaustive (brute-force) search tries every sequence, computes its probability, and returns the largest.

3.2 Why it is impossible

There are $|V|$ choices for step 1; for each, $|V|$ choices for step 2; and so on:

$$\#\text{sequences}=|V|\times|V|\times\cdots\times|V|=|V|^{T}$$

4. Greedy search

4.1 The rule

To avoid the $|V|^T$ explosion, make a locally best choice at every step: take the single most probable word and move on. Only one path is explored, so it needs just $T$ model runs.

$$x_{t+1}=\arg\max_{w\in V}P(x_{t+1}=w\mid x_1,\dots,x_t)$$

Greedy picks the highest entry in each column: I → like → cold → coffee → ⟨stop⟩ ("I like cold coffee"). Its probability:

$$0.4\times0.35\times0.45\times0.35\times0.5=0.011025\approx0.011$$

4.3 Two limitations

  1. No variety. If the first word is "I", the output is always "I like cold coffee". Deterministic.
  2. Not necessarily the most probable sequence. Suppose we instead pick the second most likely word at step 1 ("coffee", 0.25). The later distributions change (the context is different). The slide's second table gives: coffee 0.25 → like 0.55 → cold 0.65 → water 0.8 → ⟨stop⟩ 0.5:
$$0.25\times0.55\times0.65\times0.8\times0.5=0.03575\approx0.036\quad(\text{slide rounds to }0.035)$$

"coffee like cold water ⟨stop⟩" has probability 0.036, about 3.2 times that of the greedy sequence (0.011). A slightly worse first choice opened up much better continuations. Greedy cannot see this because it never looks ahead.

Same lesson with the 3-word tableGreedy picks A first (0.5), then the best follow-up B (0.5): "A B" = 0.25. But the optimum was "B A" = 0.32. Locally best ≠ globally best.

Slide's question: why not follow this at every step (try alternatives)? That is exactly beam search.

5. Beam search

5.1 Idea

A compromise between greedy (1 path) and exhaustive (all paths). Keep the best $k$ partial sequences (the beam, with beam size / width $k$) at every step. At each step, extend each of the $k$ sequences by every possible word, score the $k\times|V|$ candidates by their sequence probability, and keep only the top $k$. This costs $k\times|V|$ scores per step instead of $|V|^t$.

  1. t = 1: keep the $k$ most probable first words.
  2. t = 2: extend each by all words, score $P(x_1)P(x_2\mid x_1)$, keep the top $k$ overall (they may come from the same parent).
  3. Repeat. At the end you have $k$ complete sequences; output the one with the highest probability.

5.2 Worked example from the slide ($k=2$)

Step 1: $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.4$, $P(C)=0.1$. Keep A and B (C is dropped). Step 2 candidates (using the slide's conditional probabilities):

5.3 Ending and backtracking

Each beam path ends at ⟨stop⟩ (or the maximum length). The slide shows a word lattice ("A train engine is making the noise", "A train engine oil is costly", ...): after the last step, backtrack from the best end node to recover the sentence.

5.4 Length normalization

Every extra word multiplies by a number below 1, so a longer sentence almost always has a smaller probability than a shorter one, even if it is better. To compare fairly the slide says: divide the probability of the sequence by its length. In log form (lecture summary):

$$\text{Score}(x_1..x_T)=\frac{1}{T^{\alpha}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P(x_t\mid x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}),\qquad\alpha\in[0.6,1.0]$$

$\alpha=1$ is the plain per-word average log-probability; smaller $\alpha$ penalises length less. We work in logs because a product of many small numbers underflows, and $\log$ turns products into sums.

Worked example (created for practice):

Hypothesislengthprobabilitylog Pscore, α=1
H1 (short)40.02−3.912−0.978
H2 (long)80.004−5.521−0.690

Raw probability prefers H1 (0.02 > 0.004). After normalising, H2 wins (−0.690 > −0.978): it is more probable per word.

5.5 Strengths and weaknesses

  • Better than greedy at finding high-probability sequences; very suitable for translation and summarization, where there is essentially one right answer.
  • Both greedy and beam search are prone to degenerate (repetitive) text, and neither is creative. Beam search costs more than greedy (latency of greedy is lower).

6. Why probable is not the same as human: surprise

Slide: "We are surprised when something is creative! Surprise = uncertainty." Humans do not always say the most likely next word. Their word choices vary (high variance); beam search always picks the high-probability path (low variance, predictable, repetitive).

Information viewSurprisal of a word is $-\ln p$: a word with $p=0.55$ has surprisal 0.60; a word with $p=0.04$ has 3.22. The average surprisal of a distribution is its entropy $H=-\sum_ip_i\ln p_i$. Beam search always picks low-surprisal words; human text has varied probability, dynamic entropy and natural surprise.
ProfileBeam searchHuman text
Chosen word probabilityAlways highVaried
PredictabilityHigh, repetitiveNatural surprise, creative

Giving other fairly probable tokens a chance leads to a variety of sequences. That is the motivation for random sampling: instead of always taking the maximum, draw the next word from the distribution, while cutting off the nonsense tail. The next sections show the three tools.

7. Top-k sampling

7.1 Algorithm

  1. Rank words by probability: $p_1\ge p_2\ge\dots\ge p_{|V|}$.
  2. Truncate: keep the top $k$, $V_k=\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$; discard the rest.
  3. Renormalize so the kept probabilities sum to 1: $p_i'=\dfrac{p_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_j}$.
  4. Sample: draw $r\sim U[0,1)$ and pick the word whose sub-interval of $[0,1)$ contains $r$.

7.2 Slide example ($k=2$)

At step 1 the two most probable words are "I" (0.4) and "coffee" (0.25). Their sum is 0.65, so after renormalizing:

$$P(\text{I})=\frac{0.4}{0.65}=0.615\approx0.61,\qquad P(\text{coffee})=\frac{0.25}{0.65}=0.385\approx0.39$$
Split [0, 1) in proportion to the renormalized probabilities; a uniform random number r picks the word (here r = 0.25 falls in "I")

Because the choice is random, one run may give "I ⟨stop⟩" and another "coffee like cold coffee ⟨stop⟩". Words with tiny probability (like, water, ...) can never be chosen, which removes incoherent text, yet the output varies between runs.

7.3 The problem: what is the right k?

The shape of the distribution changes from step to step:

  • Flat (after "She said I never ..."): many words are equally good (thought, knew, had, saw, said, ...). A small $k$ throws away good options and reduces creativity.
  • Peaky (after "I ate the pizza while it was still ..."): essentially "hot" (maybe "cooling", "warm"). A large $k$ pulls in poor words and may produce meaningless sentences.

Numbers (created for practice): peaky distribution hot .55, cooling .20, warm .10, on .06, heating .05, going .04. With fixed $k=5$ the kept words sum to 0.96; the two poor ones (on, heating) carry $0.11/0.96\approx11.5\%$ of the sampling chance. In the flat case below the same $k=5$ is fine.

8. Temperature (Solution 1)

8.1 Definition

Divide the logits by a temperature $\tau>0$ (the slide writes $T$) before the softmax:

$$P(x=u_l)=\frac{\exp(u_l/\tau)}{\sum_{l'}\exp(u_{l'}/\tau)}$$

$\tau=1$ gives the model's original distribution. Low temperature gives a skewed (peaky) distribution = less creativity. High temperature gives a flatter distribution = more creativity.

8.2 Why it works (derivation)

For any two words $i,j$ the ratio of probabilities is

$$\frac{P_i}{P_j}=\frac{e^{u_i/\tau}}{e^{u_j/\tau}}=\exp\!\Big(\frac{u_i-u_j}{\tau}\Big)$$

The logit gap $u_i-u_j$ is divided by $\tau$. If $\tau<1$ the gap is magnified, so the favourite dominates even more; if $\tau>1$ the gap is shrunk and the ratio moves towards 1 (all words equal). Limits: $\tau\to0$ gives probability 1 to the largest logit = greedy/argmax; $\tau\to\infty$ gives the uniform distribution (maximum entropy). The ranking of words never changes; only how sharp it is.

9. Top-p (nucleus) sampling (Solution 2)

9.1 Idea

Fix the problem of a fixed $k$ by making the cut-off dynamic: choose the smallest set of top words whose total probability reaches a threshold $p$ (e.g. 0.6 or 0.9). The slide says it is "similar to top-k with k being dynamic".

9.2 Algorithm (derivation)

  1. Sort: $p_1\ge p_2\ge\dots\ge p_{|V|}$.
  2. Cut-off: $k^{(p)}=\min\{k:\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}p_i\ge p\}$, the smallest $k$ whose running total reaches $p$.
  3. Nucleus: $V^{(p)}=\{x_1,\dots,x_{k^{(p)}}\}$.
  4. Renormalize: $p'_i=\dfrac{p_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{k^{(p)}}p_j}$ for $i\le k^{(p)}$ and $0$ otherwise.
  5. Sample $x_{t+1}$ from $p'$.

11. Why we need BERT

In GPT the representation of a word is built left to right only: to predict the word after "i like to ___" (play / watch / go / read) the model sees just the left context. That fits text generation. But other tasks need the whole sentence:

  • Named entity recognition (NER): in "Nothing has shipped its new OS to Nothing Phone 2", is "Nothing" a company or a product? You must read both sides.
  • Fill in the blanks: "i ___ to read a ___ [eos]" can only be solved with words from both directions.

Of the three families of Week 3 (encoder-only, decoder-only, encoder-decoder), BERT is encoder-only: Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. The decoder cannot be used because it is inherently unidirectional (its causal mask hides the right side).

$$\text{BERT attention: }A_{t,j}=\text{softmax}_j\!\Big(\frac{Q_tK_j^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\ \text{ for all }j\in\{1..T\}\quad(\text{no mask }M)$$

12. Masked language modelling (MLM)

12.1 The problem and the idea

If every word can see every other word, plain next-word prediction becomes trivial (the answer is in the input). So BERT hides some words and asks the model to recover them, like fill-in-the-blank. It is similar to the CBOW idea of Word2Vec (predict a word from the context on both sides). The slides cast it as

$$P\big(y_i=x_i\ \big|\ x_1,\dots,x_i=[\text{mask}],\dots,x_T\big)=\ ?$$

Note the assumption: the masked words are predicted independently of each other (each masked position gets its own softmax, ignoring the other masked words). We still need to decide how to mask the words.

12.2 Why not just mask the attention weights?

In the decoder we added $-\infty$ in the attention mask. Could we do the same here to hide the chosen words? The slide's answer is no: we want the model to learn (attend to) what the blanks are, so the blank must stay visible as a position. Think of the [mask] token as noise that corrupts the input text, and the model has to recover the original token. This is the denoising objective of auto-encoders, which is why MLM is also called a denoising pre-training objective.

(An extra reason, my own reasoning: with a plain attention mask the word's own embedding would still be present at its own position, so the answer would leak through that position.)

12.3 Using a special token

Add [mask] to the vocabulary and give it its own embedding. The sentence "[mask] enjoyed the [mask] transformers" is fed to the encoder; the output vectors at the masked positions go through the output layer $W_v$ with a softmax over the vocabulary.

12.4 Loss: only the masked positions

$$\mathcal L_{MLM}=\frac1{|M|}\sum_{i\in M}-\log\hat y_i$$

$M$ = the set of masked positions; $\hat y_i$ is the probability the model gave to the true word at position $i$. With masks at positions 1 and 4: $\mathcal L=\tfrac12(L_1+L_4)$, $L_1=-\log\hat y_1$, $L_4=-\log\hat y_4$. Non-masked positions contribute nothing.

12.5 How much to mask? About 15%

  • Mask too much: too little context remains and the model cannot learn good representations.
  • Mask too little: few words are predicted per sentence, so gradients are small and convergence is slow and inefficient.
  • BERT masks 15% of the tokens (sampled uniformly). The trade-off can change with model size, masking scheme and optimizer (paper: "Should you mask 15% in MLM?"). Example: a 20-token input has about 3 masked tokens.

12.6 The 80–10–10 rule

Of the selected 15% of tokens:

ShareWhat happensExample (target "like")
80%Replaced by [mask]I [MASK] to read a book
10%Replaced by a random wordI apple to read a book
10%Left unchangedI like to read a book

In all three cases the model must predict the original word "like". Why? The special [mask] token never appears in downstream data (no words are masked when we fine-tune or use the model). If BERT only ever saw [mask] at prediction time it could learn "predict only where I see [mask] and ignore all other tokens". Random and unchanged words (20% of the time) force it to build a good contextual representation for every token, because any token might be wrong or might need predicting. This reduces the mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning.

13. Next sentence prediction (NSP) and the input format

Question (slide): is MLM alone enough for tasks like question answering, where the relation between sentences matters? Not obviously, so BERT adds a second objective.

13.1 NSP

The input is a pair of sentences (A, B) with a label:

  • 50% of the time B really follows A in the corpus (label IsNext).
  • 50% of the time B is a random sentence from the corpus (label NotNext).

Special tokens: [CLS] at the start, [SEP] between the sentences. The final hidden vector of [CLS] goes to a classifier: $\mathcal L_{cls}=-\log\hat y$. Pre-training with NSP improves QA and similar tasks significantly.

Embeddings:

$$\text{token}=30000\times768=23{,}040{,}000\qquad\text{segment}=2\times768=1{,}536\qquad\text{position}=512\times768=393{,}216$$ $$\text{total}\approx23.4\text{M}$$

One layer (ignoring biases): attention $3\times(768\times64)\times12=1{,}769{,}472\approx1.7$M plus output linear $768\times768=589{,}824\approx0.6$M; FFN $768\times3072+3072\times768+(3072+768)=4{,}722{,}432\approx4.7$M. Layer total ≈ $2.36+4.72=7.08$M ≈ 7M.

$$12\text{ layers}\approx12\times7=84\text{M}\qquad\text{total}\approx23.4+84=107.4\text{M}\ \ (\text{paper: }110\text{M})$$
Exact figures: embeddings 23.43M + attention 28.31M + FFN 56.67M = 108.4M
Why 107.4 vs 108.4 vs 110?The slide rounds each layer down to exactly 7M (true value 7.08M, so 12 layers = 84.98M, not 84M). Exact arithmetic gives 108.4M. With the real vocabulary 30,522 the embeddings grow to 23.84M and the total to 108.8M. The paper's 110M also counts the small parameters the slide leaves out: layer-norm scales and shifts, attention biases, and the pooler layer on [CLS].

Compare with GPT-1 (117M): BERT-base has a smaller vocabulary (30K vs 40K), so a smaller embedding table, and the same 12 layers of width 768.

Two approaches (slide): BERT as a feature extractor, and fine-tuning BERT.

15.2 Extractive question answering

Given a question and a paragraph, the answer is a span of the paragraph. Pre-train on unlabelled text, then fine-tune on labelled (question, paragraph, answer-span) data.

Slide example: Question "What is unique about the mission?" Paragraph "What sets this mission apart is the pivotal role of artificial intelligence (AI) in guiding the spacecraft during its critical descent to the moon's surface." Answer: "role of artificial intelligence (AI) in guiding the spacecraft"; the answer starts at paragraph token 9 and ends at token 17.

15.3 Start and end vectors

Introduce two learnable vectors $S$ (start) and $E$ (end), each the same size as $h_i$ (768). The probability that token $i$ starts the answer, and that token $i$ ends it, are softmaxes over the paragraph tokens:

$$s_i=\frac{\exp(S\cdot h_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{25}\exp(S\cdot h_j)}\qquad\qquad e_i=\frac{\exp(E\cdot h_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{25}\exp(E\cdot h_j)}$$

$S\cdot h_i$ is a dot-product score of how "start-like" token $i$ is. Only two new vectors ($2\times768=1{,}536$ parameters) are added.

Toy start and end distributions over 9 paragraph tokens (made-up numbers): start peaks at 4, end peaks at 6

15.4 Choosing the span

Pick the start $i$ and end $j$ with $j\ge i$ that maximize $s_i\times e_j$. In the slide: start $i=9$, end $j=17$ gives the span "role of ... spacecraft". If the best end comes before the start ($j, the span is invalid and the model returns an empty string, which means the answer is not in the paragraph.

Worked example (created for practice) with the toy bars above: best start = token 4 ($s_4=0.60$), best end = token 6 ($e_6=0.60$), $j\ge i$, so answer = tokens 4 to 6 with score $0.36$. If instead the end distribution peaked at token 2 and start at 4 (j=2 < i=4), the answer would be empty.

Training: for the true span $(i^*,j^*)$ minimize $\mathcal L=-\log s_{i^*}-\log e_{j^*}$ (cross-entropy on both softmaxes).

16.4 Practice questions

  1. Why is exhaustive search impossible? $|V|^T$ sequences (e.g. $40000^5=1.024\times10^{23}$).
  2. Show that greedy search can miss the best sequence. Use the 3-word table: greedy gives AB = 0.25, best is BA = 0.32 (or coffee example 0.011 vs 0.036).
  3. What does beam size 1 give? What does a larger beam cost? Greedy; $k|V|$ scores per step.
  4. Why length-normalize beam scores? Products of probabilities shrink with length, so long sentences would always lose.
  5. Why is beam search good for translation but bad for open-ended text? One right answer vs need for variety/surprise.
  6. Describe top-k with $k=2$ on probabilities 0.4, 0.25, ... Keep 0.4 and 0.25, renormalize to 0.61 / 0.39, sample with $r\in[0,1)$.
  7. Why does top-p beat fixed top-k? The nucleus size adapts to peaky or flat distributions.
  8. Effect of $\tau\to0$ and $\tau\to\infty$? Greedy; uniform.
  9. Why can't BERT use the decoder, and why not an attention mask for MLM? The decoder is unidirectional; the blank must stay visible so the model learns to fill it (denoising).
  10. Explain 15% and 80/10/10. Balance between context and learning signal; avoid dependence on [MASK], which is absent at fine-tuning time.
  11. What does NSP add? What are segment embeddings for? Sentence-pair relationship for QA-like tasks; mark tokens of A vs B.
  12. Count BERT-base parameters. ≈23.4M embeddings + 12×7.08M layers ≈ 108M (110M in the paper).
  13. How does BERT answer extractive questions? Softmax over $S\cdot h_i$ and $E\cdot h_j$; choose $(i,j)$ with $j\ge i$ maximizing $s_ie_j$; empty if $j
