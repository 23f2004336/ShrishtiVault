1. From one decision-maker to many

Single-person decision. You have a set of choices. Each choice leads to an outcome. You pick the outcome you like most. Nothing anyone else does changes your result.

Valuation, price and net gain

Valuation (willingness to pay)Raise a price step by step (800, 900, 950, 999, 1000...). The price where you stop caring whether you buy is your valuation. Above it you refuse, below it you gain.Net gain = Valuation − Price
Eating out aloneCheap dish (C): value 1000, price 500. Expensive dish (E): value 2500, price 3000. Net(C) = 1000 − 500 = +500    Net(E) = 2500 − 3000 = −500You pick C. Simple, because the answer depends only on you.

What does "preference" mean? Two rules

Let A ≻ B mean "A is strictly preferred to B" and A ∼ B mean "indifferent".

1. CompletenessFor any two outcomes you can compare them. Exactly one of these holds: A ≻ B, B ≻ A, or A ∼ B. No "I can't decide". 2. TransitivityA ≻ B and B ≻ C ⟹ A ≻ C
Why transitivity matters: the fruit cycleSuppose you prefer Mango ≻ Banana, Banana ≻ Guava, but Guava ≻ Mango. Offer Guava vs Mango and you pick Guava. Offer Banana vs Guava... you swap again. Round and round, because there is no best fruit. Your choice would depend on the order shown, not on what you actually like. No model can predict this person, so we rule it out.

Game = decision with other minds involved

GameA situation where the outcome for each person depends on their own action and the choices of others. Now you must guess what others will do, and they are guessing about you.

2. The restaurant dinner game

Two friends dine together and split the bill 50-50. Same dishes and prices as before. Each picks C or E.

Deriving every payoff

(C, C)Bill = 500 + 500 = 1000. Each pays 500. Each net = 1000 − 500 = 500.
(C, E)Bill = 500 + 3000 = 3500. Each pays 3500 ÷ 2 = 1750.
Player 1 (cheap): 1000 − 1750 = −750. Player 2 (expensive): 2500 − 1750 = +750.
(E, C)Same bill, mirror image: Player 1 gets +750, Player 2 gets −750.
(E, E)Bill = 3000 + 3000 = 6000. Each pays 3000. Net = 2500 − 3000 = −500.
P1 \ P2Cheap (C)Expensive (E)
Cheap (C)500, 500−750, 750
Expensive (E)750, −750−500, −500

First number is the row player (Player 1), second is the column player.

What should Player 1 do?

  • If P2 picks C: compare 500 (play C) with 750 (play E). E is better.
  • If P2 picks E: compare −750 (play C) with −500 (play E). E is better.

E wins in both cases, so E is the sensible choice whatever the friend does. P2 reasons the same way. Both end at (E, E) with −500 each, although (C, C) gave both +500.

The big lessonEveryone acting in their own interest can make everyone worse off. The split bill means you pay only half the cost of your fancy dish, so you over-order.

3. Four motivating examples

3.1 Traffic problem

P1 \ P2Follow (F)Disobey (D)
Follow (F)3, 30, 4
Disobey (D)4, 01, 1

Against F, D gives 4 > 3. Against D, D gives 1 > 0. So D always wins and both end at (1, 1) gridlock, worse than (3, 3).

3.2 Braess's paradox: a new road makes everyone slower

4000 drivers go from A to B. Roads A→C and D→B always take 45 min. Roads C→B and A→D take x/100 min, where x is the number of cars on that road.

Before the bridgeBy symmetry 2000 take ACB and 2000 take ADB.T(ACB) = 45 + 2000/100 = 65    T(ADB) = 2000/100 + 45 = 65Nobody gains by switching, so 65 min is the equilibrium.
After the 1-minute bridge D→C: new route ADCBT(ADCB) = xAD/100 + 1 + xCB/100 Step 1: one driver tries it. Leaving ACB, he now adds a car to AD (2001) and CB stays 2000 (he left it and rejoined). T = 20.01 + 1 + 20 = 41.01 min. That is far below 65, so everyone wants to switch.
Step 2: ADCB beats ACB for any traffic. Compare 45 + xCB/100 with xAD/100 + 1 + xCB/100. The xCB terms cancel. ADCB is better when xAD/100 + 1 < 45, i.e. xAD/100 < 44. Since xAD ≤ 4000, xAD/100 ≤ 40 < 44. Always true.
Step 3: ADCB beats ADB. Compare xAD/100 + 45 with xAD/100 + 1 + xCB/100. Now xAD cancels. Need xCB/100 + 1 < 45, true since xCB/100 ≤ 40.
Step 4: result. ADCB is the best route for every driver, so all 4000 use it:T = 4000/100 + 1 + 4000/100 = 40 + 1 + 40 = 81 min
Paradox65 → 81 minutes. Adding a road made every driver slower, because self-interested choices overload the two shared roads.

3.3 Adverse selection: the acquirer game

Firm T is worth x under its own owners. Only T knows x. Firm A thinks x is spread evenly between 0 and 100. Under A, T is worth 1.5x. A bids y. T accepts if y ≥ x. If accepted, A earns 1.5x − y.

Derivation (continuous case, x uniform on [0, 100]) 1. T accepts only when x ≤ y. So given acceptance, x is uniform on [0, y].
2. The average of a uniform number on [0, y] is the midpoint: E[x | x ≤ y] = y/2.
3. A's expected profit given acceptance:1.5·(y/2) − y = 0.75y − y = −0.25y 4. This is negative for every y > 0. (Including the chance of acceptance, y/100, the overall expected profit is −0.25y²/100, still negative.)
5. So A bids 0 and no deal happens, even though the deal creates 50% extra value.
Why? A only wins when T is willing to sell, and T is willing only when the firm is worth less than the bid. Winning is bad news. This is the "winner's curse" or adverse selection.
Discrete version: x is 50 or 100, each with probability 0.5T accepts if y ≥ x.
Bid y = 50: if x = 50, accepted, A gets 1.5(50) − 50 = 25. If x = 100, rejected, A gets 0. E = 0.5(25) + 0.5(0) = 12.5 > 0 Bid y = 75: if x = 50, accepted, A gets 75 − 75 = 0. If x = 100, rejected, 0. E = 0. So y = 50 is best here. With only two possible values, a bid can catch the low type without overpaying for the high type.

3.4 War game (sequential)

Country 1 chooses Attack or Not Attack. If it attacks, Country 2 sees this and chooses Fight or Retreat. Both prefer holding the island to not holding it, and a fight is worst for both.

Payoffs are (Country 1, Country 2). Country 2's choice depends on what it saw. This is why trees are used for sequential games. (Solved by backward induction in a later week.)

4. Classifying games

Static vs dynamic

  • Static (simultaneous): players choose without seeing others' choices. "Simultaneous" is about information, not the clock. Written as a table (normal / strategic / tabular form).
  • Dynamic (sequential): the game unfolds over time and players see earlier moves. Written as a game tree (extensive form), which also shows how much each player knows when moving.

Complete vs incomplete information

Complete: everyone knows the players, actions and all payoffs. Incomplete: someone is unsure about another's payoff or type (like the acquirer not knowing x).

Non-cooperative vs cooperative

Non-cooperativeCooperative
FocusDetailed model of moves and procedureWhich coalitions form, how to split the gain
AgreementsNot enforceable unless the game enforces themBinding, with free communication
QuestionWhat outcome will happen?Who teams up, who gets what?

Cooperative games split into TU (transferable utility: side payments are possible) and NTU (not transferable).

Formal description of a static game

Normal form: three ingredients Γ = ( N, {Ai}, {ui} ) N = {1, ..., n}, the players. Ai = actions of player i; an action profile is a = (a1, ..., an) in A = A1 × ... × An. ui : A → ℝ gives player i a number for each profile.

Course map. Weeks 1-6 (Prof. Vimal Kumar): normal and extensive form, incomplete information, cooperative games. Weeks 7-12: matching, voting, Shapley value, fair division, auctions, networks.

5. The Prisoner's Dilemma

Ranga and Billa are held in separate cells. Police can prove only a minor crime (1 year) unless someone confesses.

  • Both confess: 5 years each. Both deny: 1 year each.
  • One confesses, one denies: confessor goes free (0), the other gets 20.

Payoff = −(years in jail), so less jail means a higher number.

Ranga \ BillaConfess (C)Deny (D)
Confess (C)−5, −50, −20
Deny (D)−20, 0−1, −1

Rules of this game: one shot (no repeat), moves hidden from each other, and any pre-arrest promise is cheap talk because nobody can enforce it.

Formal versionPlayers {Ranga, Billa}. A₁ = A₂ = {C, D}. Outcomes {(C,C), (C,D), (D,C), (D,D)}. Payoffs as in the table.

More players? With 3 players, Player 3 picks the table: draw one 2×2 table for each of Player 3's actions and put three payoffs in each cell. With continuous actions (like a bid y), a table is impossible, so use a payoff formula ui(a1, ..., an).

6. Dominance

Notationa−i ("deleted profile") = what everyone except i plays. So a profile is written (ai, a−i). Pure action: one definite choice. Mixed action σi: a probability over pure actions, with every probability ≥ 0 and the sum = 1. A pure action is a mix that puts probability 1 on one action.
Strict dominanceai strictly dominates a′i if it is better against every a−i:ui(ai, a−i) > ui(a′i, a−i) for all a−i Weak dominanceui(ai, a−i) ≥ ui(a′i, a−i) for all a−i, and > for at least one A dominant action dominates all the player's other actions.
Prisoner's Dilemma check (Ranga)Billa plays C: u(C,C) = −5 > u(D,C) = −20. Billa plays D: u(C,D) = 0 > u(D,D) = −1. C wins both ways, so Confess is strictly dominant. Same for Billa. Result: (C, C), even though (D, D) gives both −1.

A mix can dominate a pure action

P1 \ P2LR
U3, 20, 5
M0, 53, 2
D1, 11, 1

U vs D: vs L, 3 > 1, but vs R, 0 < 1. M vs D: vs L, 0 < 1, but vs R, 3 > 1. No single action beats D everywhere. Now mix: play U with probability ½ and M with probability ½.

Expected payoff of ½U + ½MExpected payoff = (probability) × (payoff), added up.
Against L: ½(3) + ½(0) = 1.5. Against R: ½(0) + ½(3) = 1.5.
Both beat D's sure 1. So the mix strictly dominates D and D is never sensible.
Which mixes work? Play U with probability p, M with 1−p. Against L: 3p > 1 gives p > 1/3. Against R: 3(1−p) > 1 gives p < 2/3. Any p between 1/3 and 2/3 dominates D.

7. Solving games: Nash equilibrium

EquilibriumA state where nobody wants to change. In a game: nobody has regret. Pure-strategy Nash equilibrium: a profile a* where for every player iui(a*i, a*−i) ≥ ui(ai, a*−i) for every ai in Ai In words: hold everyone else fixed, and your action is a best response. Test one player at a time (a unilateral deviation).

Checking all four cells of the Prisoner's Dilemma

CellWho can gain by deviating?Nash?
(D, D) = −1, −1Ranga: D→C gives 0 > −1No
(D, C) = −20, 0Ranga: D→C gives −5 > −20No
(C, D) = 0, −20Billa: D→C gives −5 > −20No
(C, C) = −5, −5Ranga: C→D gives −20 < −5. Billa: same. Nobody gains.Yes
CarefulNash does not mean "best for the group". (C, C) is the equilibrium but both would prefer (D, D). A dominant-strategy outcome is always a Nash equilibrium, but not every Nash equilibrium comes from dominance.

Matching pennies: no pure equilibrium

P1 wins if the coins match, P2 wins if they differ. Zero-sum: u₁ + u₂ = 0 everywhere.

P1 \ P2HT
H1, −1−1, 1
T−1, 11, −1

In every cell someone is losing and can flip to win. E.g. at (H, H), P2 loses and switches to T. At (H, T), P1 loses and switches to T. And so on around the table, so no cell is stable. (Mixed strategies fix this, in a later week.)

8. Assumptions behind the theory

Rationality

  • Preferences are complete and transitive (Section 1).
  • Players maximise their payoff ui.
  • Perfect recall: they never forget what happened.
  • Perfect calculator: they can work out even huge trees instantly.
What if a player is kind?"Altruism" does not break the theory; it changes the payoffs. Let Ranga count Billa's sentence too: uR = SentenceR + SentenceB, and Billa stays selfish.
Ranga \ BillaCD
C−10, −5−20, −20
D−20, 0−2, −1
Ranga's cell (C,C): −5 + −5 = −10. (C,D): 0 + −20 = −20. (D,C): −20 + 0 = −20. (D,D): −1 + −1 = −2. Now Ranga's best reply depends on Billa (vs C he plays C, vs D he plays D), so C is no longer dominant for him. But Billa still has dominant C (−5 > −20, 0 > −1), so Ranga best-replies with C. Equilibrium is still (C, C), payoffs (−10, −5).

Common knowledge

Levels of knowing a fact XKnowledge: I know X. Mutual knowledge E¹: everyone knows X. E²: everyone knows that everyone knows X. E³: everyone knows E². And so on.Common knowledge CK(X) = E¹(X) and E²(X) and E³(X) and ... forever
The text-message story: where it breaksRanga and Billa planned to meet at India Gate. It closes. Ranga texts "Meet at Rajiv Chowk Gate 1". Billa replies "Yes". Ranga never confirms he got the reply. Billa goes to Rajiv Chowk; Ranga does not.
E¹: both know the new plan. ✔
E²: Ranga knows Billa knows (he saw the reply). Billa knows Ranga knows (Ranga wrote it). ✔
E³: Ranga knows Billa knows that Ranga knows. ✔
E⁴: Does Billa know that Ranga knows that Billa knows that Ranga knows? No. Billa cannot be sure his "Yes" arrived, so Ranga might think Billa never got the news.
One missing level means CK fails, and with it the coordination.

9. Cooperative game: the shoe game

P1 owns a right shoe. P2 and P3 each own a left shoe. A matching pair is worth 1. Nothing else is worth anything.

Characteristic function v(S)A coalition S is any non-empty group of players. With n players there are 2ⁿ − 1 of them, so here 2³ − 1 = 7. v(S) is the total value group S can create alone.
Coalition{1}{2}{3}{1,2}{1,3}{2,3}{1,2,3}
v0001101

{2,3} has two left shoes and no pair, so 0. {1,2,3} still makes only one pair, so 1.

Marginal contribution: Δᵢv(S) = v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S) (how much i adds to group S)
Player 3 joining {1,2}: v({1,2,3}) − v({1,2}) = 1 − 1 = 0.
Player 1 joining {2,3}: v({1,2,3}) − v({2,3}) = 1 − 0 = 1.
Meaning: Players 2 and 3 are identical and compete to partner with P1. P3 adds nothing to {1,2}, so P3 has no bargaining power, and the same logic pushes P2 down too. Competition leaves P1 with the whole unit: split (1, 0, 0).

Practice (tap to reveal)

  1. Why is (E,E) in the dinner game not what the friends would choose if they could agree?
    AnswerBoth get −500, but (C,C) gives both +500. Without a way to enforce the promise, each is tempted to switch to E.
  2. In the 3×2 table, show that D is not dominated by U alone.
    AnswerU gives 0 against R, while D gives 1. So U fails to beat D when P2 plays R.
  3. In the acquirer game, why is "bid higher" worse, not better?
    AnswerHigher y only adds more of the high-value firms to the pool that accept, so the average accepted firm is y/2 and the profit per deal is −0.25y, which gets worse as y rises.
  4. Find the Nash equilibria of the traffic game.
    AnswerOnly (D, D). In every other cell one driver can gain by switching to D.
