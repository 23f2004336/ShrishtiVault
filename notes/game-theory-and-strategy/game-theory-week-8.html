Part A: Information cascades

1. Herding: why copying can be rational

You arrive in an unfamiliar town. Your own research says restaurant A is good. But when you reach it, A is empty and B next door is nearly full. What do you do?

The reasoning. If you think other diners have tastes like yours and some information of their own about where to eat, then a full B is evidence that B is better. Many independent people, each with a hint, chose B. That crowd's information can be stronger than your single private hint. So it can be perfectly rational to go to B even though your own information says A.
Information cascade (herding): people abandon their own information in favour of inferences drawn from what earlier people did.
It can happen when four things are true (slide labels [A] to [D]):
[A]A decision to make (adopt a technology, wear a style, eat in a restaurant, support a politician)
[B]People decide sequentially, and each can see what earlier people chose
[C]Each person has some private information that guides the decision
[D]You cannot see others' private information, only what they do; so you infer it from their actions

Examples the slides give: fashions and fads, voting for popular candidates, books that sell because they are high on the bestseller list, and the spread of a technology among consumers and firms.

2. The classroom marble experiment

Setup (slides). An urn hides three marbles. With probability ½ it holds two red and one blue ("majority-red"). With probability ½ it holds two blue and one red ("majority-blue"). Students come one by one to the front. Each student:
1. draws a marble, looks at its colour, and puts it back without showing it;
2. publicly announces a guess: "majority-red" or "majority-blue".
At the end every student who guessed correctly gets a reward; wrong guessers get nothing. Everyone reasons correctly using everything they have heard.

The key numbers. If the urn is majority-blue, a draw is blue with probability 2/3 and red with probability 1/3. If it is majority-red, the reverse. So a marble is a "signal" that is right 2/3 of the time. I write q = 2/3 for this accuracy.

2.1 Going student by student

Student 1. Sees one marble. If it is blue, majority-blue is more likely (we prove it below: 2/3), so she guesses blue. If red, she guesses red. Her guess equals her draw, so her guess reveals her marble perfectly.
Student 2. Knows student 1's marble (from the guess) and sees her own.
• Same colour as student 1: two draws point the same way, so she guesses that colour.
• Different colour (say student 1 said blue and she draws red): it is as if she had drawn twice, once blue and once red. That is a tie (probability exactly ½ each). The slides assume she breaks the tie by guessing the colour she saw. Either way, her guess equals her draw, so it also reveals her marble perfectly.
Student 3.
• If the first two guesses were different (one blue, one red), they cancel out. Student 3 is in the same position as student 1: she guesses her own colour.
• If the first two guesses were the same (say both blue), she knows two blue marbles were drawn. Suppose she draws red. She now has three draws: blue, blue, red. We compute below that majority-blue has probability 2/3. So she should guess blue and ignore her own red marble.
That is the cascade starting. Her guess is blue whatever she draws, so it tells later students nothing about her marble.
Student 4 and everyone after. Student 4 hears blue, blue, blue. She knows that the first two guesses showed real marbles, and that student 3's blue was automatic (it would be blue whatever he saw), so it carries no information. So she faces exactly the situation of student 3: two blue draws against whatever she holds. Even a red marble is outweighed, so she guesses blue. The same applies to students 5, 6, 7, … by repeating the argument. All later guesses are blue and no new information ever enters the public record.

The tree for the case "first two guesses blue". "Same" has probability 5/9 when we average over both urns (computed in section 3); the other side starts the same story again with a clean slate.

3. Bayes' rule, derived, and used to check each student's reasoning

Derivation of Bayes' rule (slides). For events A and B, Pr[A|B] means "probability of A given that B happened".
1. By definition, Pr[A|B] = Pr[A∩B] / Pr[B] (the part of B's probability where A also happens).
2. Likewise Pr[B|A] = Pr[A∩B] / Pr[A].
3. From step 2, Pr[A∩B] = Pr[B|A]·Pr[A]. Substitute into step 1: Pr[A|B] = Pr[B|A] · Pr[A] / Pr[B] 4. The denominator can be split by the two possible urns ("law of total probability"): Pr[B] = Pr[A]·Pr[B|A] + Pr[not A]·Pr[B|not A]

Why the students use it. A student wants to maximise her chance of being right. She guesses majority-blue if Pr[majority-blue | what she saw and heard] > 0.5 and majority-red if it is below 0.5.

3.1 Student 1 (and 2)

Prior: Pr[majority-blue] = Pr[majority-red] = ½. Likelihoods: Pr[blue | majority-blue] = 2/3 and Pr[blue | majority-red] = 1/3. She draws blue.
Denominator: Pr[blue] = ½·(2/3) + ½·(1/3) = 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2.
Posterior: Pr[majority-blue | blue] = (½·2/3) / (1/2) = (1/3)/(1/2) = 2/3 > 1/2. So she guesses blue. ✔

3.2 Student 3, who has heard blue, blue and draws red

She knows the three draws were blue, blue, red (the first two are revealed by the guesses; the third is her own). The draws are independent given the urn.
If majority-blue: Pr[b, b, r | majority-blue] = (2/3)·(2/3)·(1/3) = 4/27.
If majority-red: Pr[b, b, r | majority-red] = (1/3)·(1/3)·(2/3) = 2/27.
Denominator: Pr[b, b, r] = ½·(4/27) + ½·(2/27) = 2/27 + 1/27 = 3/27 = 1/9.
Posterior: Pr[majority-blue | b, b, r] = (½·4/27) / (1/9) = (2/27)/(1/9) = 2/3 > 1/2.
So she should guess blue, ignoring her red marble. ✔ (Shortcut: the red cancels one blue, leaving one net blue draw, which gives 2/3, the same as student 1's calculation.)

3.3 A general rule: when does a cascade start? (the net count d)

Let each signal be right with probability q (here 2/3). Each informative signal multiplies the odds of its colour by q/(1−q) (for q = 2/3 that factor is 2). A signal of the opposite colour multiplies by (1−q)/(q) (that is ½), cancelling one of the other kind.

Derivation. Let d be the net number of revealed signals favouring blue (blue signals minus red signals) before a student moves. Prior odds are 1 : 1. Suppose the student holds a red signal (against the crowd).
Odds(blue : red) = (q/(1−q))d × ((1−q)/q) = (q/(1−q))d−1.
• d = 1: odds = (q/(1−q))⁰ = 1, a tie. She follows her own signal (the slide's tie-break rule), so her guess reveals her signal.
• d = 2: odds = q/(1−q) = 2 > 1 (for q = 2/3). She follows the crowd against her own signal: cascade.
So a cascade starts as soon as the net revealed count reaches 2. Inside a cascade, guesses carry no information, so d stops changing.

The count d is a walk that stops at ±2. We use this picture to compute the chance of a wrong cascade in section 4.

4. Three lessons from cascades

L1: Cascades can be wrong

Slide example. Suppose the urn is truly majority-red. A blue draw has probability 1/3. The chance that student 1 and student 2 both draw blue is 1/3 × 1/3 = 1/9. Then both guess blue, a cascade of "blue" starts, and every later guess is wrong. The chance of such an error is not reduced by having more students: it stays at 1/9 however many take part.
Something the slides do not say (my own calculation, checked by simulation). The slide's 1/9 counts only the case where the first two draws are both wrong. But a wrong cascade can also start later. If student 1 and 2 disagree, the count resets to d = 0 and the walk starts again. So the full chance of a wrong cascade is bigger than 1/9. Let w = chance that the cascade ends up correct, starting from d = 0 (correct signals have probability q = 2/3, wrong ones 1 − q = 1/3).
From d = 0: step to +1 with probability q, to −1 with probability 1−q. Write a = chance of a correct cascade from d = +1 and b = from d = −1.
w = q·a + (1−q)·b. From +1: with probability q we reach +2 (correct, done), otherwise we go back to 0: a = q + (1−q)·w. From −1: with probability q we go back to 0, otherwise we hit −2 (wrong): b = q·w.
Substitute: w = q(q + (1−q)w) + (1−q)(q·w) = q² + 2q(1−q)·w. So w(1 − 2q(1−q)) = q², and since 1 − 2q(1−q) = q² + (1−q)², we get w = q² / (q² + (1−q)²) = (4/9) / (4/9 + 1/9) = 4/5 So a cascade ends wrong with probability 1/5 = 20%, not 1/9. (Check: 1/9 for "first two wrong" + (4/9 chance of a reset) × (1/5) = 1/9 + 4/45 = 1/5. ✔) The lesson is the same: the error does not shrink with more people. Notice that even a cascade is right 80% of the time, more than one person's 2/3.

L2: Cascades can be based on very little information

Once a cascade starts, private signals are ignored. So only the signals before the cascade matter. In a large population where a cascade starts quickly, most of the information that the group holds (all those private marbles) is never used. Example: with 1,000 students and a cascade starting at student 3, the group decision rests on just 2 marbles; the other 998 are wasted.

L3: Cascades are fragile

Because a cascade rests on so little information (a net count of just 2), it is easy to start and easy to stop.

Slide example. A class of 80 students. The first two guesses are blue, and as predicted all the following guesses are blue. Now students 40 and 41 both draw red, and they "cheat" by showing their marbles to the class. The cascade breaks. Student 42 now has four real pieces of information: the colours seen by students 1, 2 (blue, blue) and 40, 41 (red, red). Two blue vs two red is a tie, so she goes by her own draw. A run of 39 blue guesses was overturned by two pieces of fresh information.
Slide vs summary. The summary uses a 100-agent version (agents 101 and 102 reveal red). The idea is identical; I use the slide's numbers.

Main lesson of the lecture: be careful about concluding what is best from what a crowd does. A crowd can be wrong even when everybody is rational and everybody takes the same action. If guesses are made one after another and seen by later people, there is no reason to expect the average guess to be good.

Where this shows up

  • Committees. A hiring committee goes around the table asking who supports candidate A or B. If everyone assumes the others are about as well informed as they are, a cascade develops quickly. It is not just social pressure; the slides stress that it can be a rational reaction to what earlier speakers know.
  • Remedies. (1) Force the experts to reach partial decisions independently before they discuss and agree. (2) If some people have unusually good information, it matters whether they speak early or late (early is better, since they set the first signals).
  • Marketing. Firms try to start a buying cascade by getting an initial group to adopt the product. Later buyers may follow even if it is no better, or worse, than competitors. It works best if buyers can see adoption decisions but not how satisfied early buyers were. If payoffs or satisfaction statistics of earlier consumers are available, that can prevent a cascade of bad choices.

Part B: Voting

5. Voting and individual preferences

Voting is used when a group must reach one decision that speaks for the group: an election (India, the US, France), a legislature passing a bill, a jury, a prize committee, critics ranking the best films. It can pick a single winner or produce a ranked list (the greatest players of all time).

Online systems use the same ideas (search engines ranking results, recommendation systems). The slide questions: how should we make one ranking from conflicting opinions? Is majority voting good, is something better, and what does "good" even mean for a voting system?

5.1 Modelling one voter

A group evaluates a finite set of alternatives (candidates, verdicts, budget amounts, award nominees). Voter i writes X ≻i Y if she prefers X to Y. We require two properties:
[IP1] Completeness: for any two different alternatives X and Y, voter i prefers X to Y or Y to X, but not both. This rules out ties and "no opinion".
[IP2] Transitivity: if X ≻ Y and Y ≻ Z, then X ≻ Z.
Why transitivity is reasonable: the money pump. Suppose someone ranks Chocolate over Vanilla, Vanilla over Kesar Pista, and Kesar Pista over Chocolate (C ≻ V ≻ K ≻ C, a cycle). She holds a Chocolate cone.
1. You offer a Kesar Pista cone for her Chocolate plus a small fee ε. She prefers K to C, so she pays.
2. You offer Vanilla for her Kesar Pista plus ε. She prefers V to K, so she pays.
3. You offer Chocolate for her Vanilla plus ε. She prefers C to V, so she pays.
She is back to the Chocolate cone she started with, but 3ε poorer. You can repeat this forever. (The slide asks: faced with just these three flavours in a shop, what would she buy? There is no sensible answer.)
Equivalence. A complete and transitive preference is exactly the same thing as a ranked list from best to worst. So we describe each voter by her ranking.
Voting system: any method that takes everyone's complete and transitive rankings and produces a group ranking. (For a fixed number k of voters, it is a function from k rankings to one ranking.)

6. Majority rule and its paradox

6.1 Two alternatives: all fine

With two alternatives and an odd number of voters (so no ties), majority rule ranks first the alternative that more voters prefer. It gives a complete, transitive group ranking.

6.2 More than two: apply majority to every pair

For each pair X, Y, count voters who prefer X and voters who prefer Y. The larger number wins the pair: X ≻ Y or Y ≻ X. Because the number of voters is odd, there is exactly one winner in each pair, so the group preference is complete. But is it transitive?

6.3 The Condorcet paradox

Count each pair.
A vs B: voters 1 and 3 prefer A (voter 3: C ≻ A ≻ B), voter 2 prefers B. A wins 2 to 1: A ≻ B.
B vs C: voters 1 and 2 prefer B, voter 3 prefers C. B wins 2 to 1: B ≻ C.
C vs A: voters 2 and 3 prefer C, voter 1 prefers A. C wins 2 to 1: C ≻ A.
Together: A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A, a cycle. Transitivity would require A ≻ C, but the group says C ≻ A.

Every alternative loses to another: there is no Condorcet winner (an alternative that beats all others head to head).

Condorcet paradox: every voter has a complete, transitive ranking, but the pairwise-majority group preference can be non-transitive. Each individual is "rational" and the group is not.

6.4 Elimination tournaments and agenda setting

To get a full ranking, one can pick a group favourite, remove it, and repeat. A natural way to pick a favourite is a knockout tournament: compare the first two alternatives by majority, then compare the winner with the third, then the winner with the fourth, and so on. The last winner is the "group favourite". (Slide picture: A vs B, winner vs C, winner vs D.)

Use it on the paradox profile (three alternatives).
• Agenda 1: A vs B first, then winner vs C. A beats B; then C beats A (2:1). C wins.
• Agenda 2: B vs C first, then winner vs A. B beats C; then A beats B. A wins.
• Agenda 3: C vs A first, then winner vs B. C beats A; then B beats C. B wins.
The winner is decided entirely by the order of the votes.
Rule of thumb (slide): to make C win, vote A vs B first. To make B win, vote A vs C first. To make A win, vote B vs C first. In short, knock out early the alternative that would have beaten your favourite. If voter 3 (C ≻ A ≻ B) sets the agenda, C wins; if voter 1 (A ≻ B ≻ C) sets it, A wins. The group favourite is chosen by whoever controls the agenda.
Allowing eliminated alternatives back? It does not fix things. With these preferences there is always an alternative that beats the current favourite (C beats A, B beats C, A beats B), so the process never ends.

7. Positional voting: Borda count, plurality

Positional voting: build the group ranking directly from the individual rankings (not from pairwise duels). Each position in a ranking is worth some points; add the points an alternative gets across all voters; rank by total.
Borda count (k alternatives): 1st place = k − 1 points, 2nd = k − 2, …, last = 0.
Plurality: 1 point for 1st place, 0 for everything else: the most first-place votes wins. (It is also a positional rule.)
(The Ballon d'Or gives each journalist's top five 6, 4, 3, 2, 1 points; 100 journalists for the men's award, 50 for the women's award, from a 30-player shortlist. The point values 6-4-3-2-1 come from the lecture summary, not the slide.)
Small example (slide). Two voters, four alternatives. Voter 1: A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ D. Voter 2: B ≻ C ≻ A ≻ D. Points (3, 2, 1, 0): A = 3 + 1 = 4, B = 2 + 3 = 5, C = 1 + 2 = 3, D = 0 + 0 = 0. Group ranking: B ≻ A ≻ C ≻ D.
Good news: Borda (ignoring ties) always produces a complete and transitive ranking, because the total points are numbers and numbers can always be sorted. So it can never give a Condorcet cycle. Bad news: the fight for top places can depend on how alternatives lower down are ranked.

7.1 Problem 1: Borda breaks "independence of irrelevant alternatives"

Pele, Maradona, Messi (slide). Five experts. First vote only on Pele (P) vs Maradona (M): experts 1, 2, 3 pick Pele; experts 4, 5 pick Maradona. Pele wins 3 to 2.
At the last moment Messi (L) is added. Rankings: experts 1 to 3: P ≻ M ≻ L; experts 4 and 5: M ≻ L ≻ P. Borda (2, 1, 0):
P = 3×2 + 2×0 = 6. M = 3×1 + 2×2 = 7. L = 3×0 + 2×1 = 2.
Maradona wins, although in the head-to-head P beat M 3 to 2, and the person who joined the race (Messi) came last.
Check by computer: Pele also beats Messi head to head (3 to 2) and Maradona beats Messi 5 to 0. So Pele beats everyone in pairwise duels (he is the Condorcet winner), yet Borda ranks him second. The "irrelevant" alternative flipped the Pele/Maradona order.
Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): the group's ordering of X and Y should depend only on how each voter ranks X and Y against each other, not on where a third alternative Z is placed. Borda violates it. (Majority rule does satisfy it, but it fails transitivity.)

7.2 Problem 2: strategic misreporting

Same example. Suppose experts 4 and 5 truly believe M ≻ P ≻ L.
If they are honest: P = 3×2 + 2×1 = 8, M = 3×1 + 2×2 = 7. Pele wins.
If they lie and report M ≻ L ≻ P (dropping Pele below Messi): P = 3×2 + 2×0 = 6, M = 7. Maradona wins, which is what they wanted.
So voters can gain by lying, especially by pushing down the main rival of their favourite.
Plurality has similar "third-party" effects: an alternative that is the favourite of very few people can shift the outcome from one of the two leading contenders to the other (vote-splitting).

9. Arrow's impossibility theorem

We have seen pathologies in majority rule (cycles, agenda control), Borda (IIA failure, lying) and plurality (vote splitting). Is there any voting system for three or more alternatives that avoids all of them? Kenneth Arrow (1951) said no.

[VP1] Pareto principle (unanimity). If every voter prefers X to Y, the group ranking must put X above Y.
[VP2] Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA). The group's ordering of X and Y must depend only on how each voter ranks X versus Y. If we take a third alternative Z and move it around in some voters' rankings, without changing any voter's X-versus-Y order, the group must still rank X and Y the same way.
Non-dictatorship. No single voter i whose ranking always becomes the group ranking.
Where IIA matters. Most pathologies above are failures of IIA. In the Borda misreporting example, moving Messi was enough to change Pele vs Maradona. In the agenda example, the key was to eliminate alternative X early, before it could meet an alternative Y that it would beat.

9.1 Dictatorship passes both tests

Dictatorship: pick one voter j and declare the group ranking equal to j's ranking. With k voters there are k such systems.
• Unanimity: if everybody prefers X to Y, j does too, so the group ranks X above Y. ✔
• IIA: the group's order of X and Y is j's order of X and Y, which does not depend on how anyone ranks Z. ✔
(With only two alternatives, majority rule also satisfies both.)
Arrow's Impossibility Theorem. If there are at least three alternatives, every voting system that satisfies both Unanimity and IIA is a dictatorship: dictatorship is the only one.
Equivalent form: with at least three alternatives, no voting system satisfies Unanimity, IIA and non-dictatorship at the same time.
Take-away. Voting rules are subject to unavoidable trade-offs. Whatever we choose will show some undesirable behaviour, so the job is to choose which flaws we can live with. (The summary's practical advice: avoid positional rules where voters have a strong incentive to demote rivals; use pairwise or randomised agendas to reduce agenda control.) The slides state the theorem but do not prove it; the proof is a longer argument beyond these notes.

10. Voting to find the truth: the Condorcet jury theorem

Now the voters do not disagree about tastes. They share one goal: pick the truly best alternative (a jury wants the correct verdict; a board wants the profitable plan). They differ only because each has different, imperfect information. If everybody had all the information, they would all agree.

Model (slides). Two alternatives, X and Y; exactly one is truly best, each with prior probability ½. Each voter gets an independent private signal: an "X-signal" (says X is better) or a "Y-signal". Signals favour the truly best alternative with probability q > 1/2: Pr[X-signal | X best] = q, Pr[Y-signal | Y best] = q All voters vote simultaneously (nobody sees another's vote) and sincerely (each votes for the alternative she believes best given her own signal). Majority wins.

10.1 Why voting sincerely is what you would do (Bayes)

A voter with an X-signal computes the probability that X is best: Pr[X best | X-signal] = Pr[X best]·Pr[X-signal | X best] / Pr[X-signal] Denominator: Pr[X-signal] = Pr[X best]·Pr[X-signal | X best] + Pr[Y best]·Pr[X-signal | Y best] = ½·q + ½·(1 − q) = ½.
So Pr[X best | X-signal] = (½·q)/(½) = q > ½. She favours X. Symmetrically, a Y-signal makes her favour Y. Each voter backs the alternative her own signal points to.

10.2 The theorem

Condorcet jury theorem. Suppose X is the best alternative (Y is symmetric). As the number of voters N grows, the fraction voting X converges to q, which is more than ½. So the probability that the majority is right goes to 1. A big group makes a better decision than any single expert.

10.3 Derivation (exact numbers first)

Three voters. The majority is right if at least 2 of 3 voters get the right signal. Each is right with probability q:
P(exactly 3 right) = q³. P(exactly 2 right) = 3q²(1−q) (three ways to choose the wrong one).
P(majority right) = q³ + 3q²(1−q) = 3q² − 2q³.
For q = 2/3: 3·(4/9) − 2·(8/27) = 12/9 − 16/27 = 36/27 − 16/27 = 20/27 ≈ 0.741 (better than a single voter's 0.667).
For q = 0.6: 3(0.36) − 2(0.216) = 1.08 − 0.432 = 0.648 (better than 0.6).
N voters (N odd): P(majority right) = Σ over k > N/2 of C(N,k)·qk·(1−q)N−k.

Computed exactly. More voters always help, and the weaker the signal, the more voters it takes.

Why it tends to 1 (the law of large numbers).
1. Suppose X is best. Voter i votes X with probability q. Write Vi = 1 for an X-vote, 0 for a Y-vote: independent, with mean q and variance q(1−q).
2. Let SN = V₁ + … + VN. Then SN/N has mean q and variance q(1−q)/N, which shrinks to 0 as N grows.
3. The majority is right if SN/N > ½. Let ε = q − ½ > 0. The majority is wrong only if SN/N ≤ ½, i.e. it is at least ε below its mean.
4. By Chebyshev's inequality: P(wrong) ≤ P(|SN/N − q| ≥ ε) ≤ q(1−q) / (N·ε²).
5. This bound goes to 0 as N → ∞. For q = 2/3: ε = 1/6, so the bound is (2/9)/(N/36) = 8/N. (For N = 101 it gives 0.079, much looser than the true 0.0003, but it proves the point.)
So P(majority right) → 1. ∎ (The slides state "converges almost surely"; the law of large numbers is the engine.)
Two assumptions hide here: (1) voters vote sincerely; (2) they vote simultaneously and independently. The next two sections drop each in turn, and the theorem fails.

11. Dropping sincerity: the ten-marble urn

Voting sincerely sounds mild: if you cannot share signals, why vote against your own best guess? But if your goal is that the group decides correctly, your vote only matters in certain situations, and that changes what is best.

The experiment (slides). An urn has 10 marbles. With probability ½ it is "pure" (10 white). With probability ½ it is "mixed" (9 green, 1 white). Three people each draw one marble in secret (and replace it), then cast simultaneous votes: pure or mixed. If the majority is right, all three get a prize; otherwise nobody gets anything (even those who personally voted correctly).
What each marble tells you.
• Green: a pure urn has no green, so the urn is mixed for certain.
• White: Pr[pure | white] = (½·1) / (½·1 + ½·(1/10)) = 0.5/0.55 = 10/11 ≈ 91%. So white points strongly to pure.
Sincere voting: white → vote pure; green → vote mixed.

11.1 Why sincere voting is not an equilibrium: the pivotal voter

Suppose voters 1 and 2 vote sincerely, and you are voter 3 with a white marble. When does your vote change the outcome? Only when the other two are split: one votes pure and the other votes mixed. (If they agree, the majority is already decided.)
Under sincere voting, a voter votes mixed only if she drew green. So "the others are split" means one of them drew a green marble. A green marble means the urn is mixed with certainty.
Conclusion: whenever your vote matters, the urn is mixed. So you should vote mixed, even though your own marble is white. Sincere voting is not an equilibrium: you can do better by voting insincerely.
Check the numbers (computed exactly).
All three sincere. Pure urn (prob ½): all three see white, all vote pure: always right. Mixed urn (prob ½): the majority is wrong only if at least two of three drew white: 3·(0.1)²·(0.9) + (0.1)³ = 0.027 + 0.001 = 0.028, so right with 0.972. Total: ½·1 + ½·0.972 = 0.986.
Voter 3 always votes mixed (others sincere). Pure urn: voters 1 and 2 both vote pure, voter 3 votes mixed, pure wins 2 to 1: still always right. Mixed urn: wrong only if voters 1 and 2 both drew white (0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01), so right with 0.99. Total: ½ + ½·0.99 = 0.995.
0.995 > 0.986: voting "mixed" regardless raises everyone's chance of the prize.
The lesson. When the aim is a correct group decision, you should think about the case in which your vote is decisive, and use all the information that case reveals. Sincerity can fail even among people who all want the same thing.

12. Dropping simultaneity: sequential voting is a cascade

Keep sincerity, but let voters vote one after another, each seeing earlier votes. This is exactly the marble experiment of section 2: votes are public actions and signals are private.

What happens. Early votes reveal signals. When the first two voters choose X, the net count is d = 2 and a cascade starts: every later voter votes X whatever her own signal says. In general a cascade eventually forms. It can be wrong: even if Y is best, a cascade for X can develop, and adding more voters does not stop it. So there is no reason to expect a large crowd of sequential voters to get the answer right. The Condorcet jury theorem fails.
Correction on the summary's limit formula. The summary writes the long-run probability of a correct group decision as 1 − (1 − q)² (that is 8/9 for q = 2/3). That counts only the case where the first two voters are wrong. The exact value (section 4, L1) is q²/(q² + (1−q)²) = 4/5 for q = 2/3. Either way it is below 1 and does not improve with N, which is the lesson.

13. Practice questions (try first, then open the answer)

Q1. In the marble game, the first guess was blue. Student 2 draws red. What does she guess, and what does her guess tell student 3?
AnswerOne blue and one red: a 50-50 tie, so she follows her own draw and guesses red. Her guess equals her draw, so it reveals her marble. The net count goes back to 0.
Q2. Compute Pr[majority-blue | blue, blue, red].
AnswerPr[b,b,r | blue-urn] = (2/3)(2/3)(1/3) = 4/27; Pr[b,b,r | red-urn] = (1/3)(1/3)(2/3) = 2/27. Posterior = (½·4/27) / (½·4/27 + ½·2/27) = 4/6 = 2/3.
Q3. The urn is majority-red. What is the probability that students 1 and 2 both guess blue? Why does it not shrink with more students?
AnswerEach draws blue with probability 1/3, so (1/3)² = 1/9. After that a cascade of blue starts and no new information enters, so adding students changes nothing. (The exact chance of ending in a wrong cascade, counting later starts, is 1/5.)
Q4. In a cascade of 80 students, students 40 and 41 show red marbles. What does student 42 do?
AnswerShe has four real signals: blue, blue (students 1, 2) and red, red (40, 41). That is a tie, so she follows her own draw. The cascade is broken.
Q5. Three voters have rankings A≻B≻C, B≻C≻A, C≻A≻B. Who wins if the agenda is "A vs C first, then the winner vs B"?
AnswerA vs C: voters 2 and 3 prefer C, so C wins 2:1. Then C vs B: voters 1 and 2 prefer B, so B wins 2:1. B wins.
Q6. Borda with three voters: X≻Y≻Z, Y≻Z≻X, Z≻X≻Y. What are the scores?
AnswerEach alternative gets 2 + 1 + 0 = 3 points. A three-way tie (the same profile gives a pairwise-majority cycle, so neither method names a winner).
Q7. In the Pele, Maradona and Messi example, why does Borda violate IIA?
AnswerPele beats Maradona 3:2 when only they are compared. After adding Messi (who finishes last), Borda scores are M 7, P 6, L 2, so Maradona now beats Pele. Messi's placement changed the Pele/Maradona order.
Q8. Budget example: who wins under plurality and under instant runoff?
AnswerPlurality: Security (5 first places vs Education 4, Health 3). Instant runoff: Health (fewest first places) is eliminated, its 3 voters move to Security, so Security wins 8 to 4.
Q9. State Arrow's theorem. Which of the three properties does dictatorship violate?
AnswerWith at least three alternatives, any voting system satisfying unanimity and IIA is a dictatorship (equivalently, no system satisfies unanimity, IIA and non-dictatorship). Dictatorship satisfies unanimity and IIA but violates non-dictatorship.
Q10. Jury theorem with 3 voters and q = 0.6: probability the majority is right?
Answer3q² − 2q³ = 3(0.36) − 2(0.216) = 0.648.
Q11. In the ten-marble urn game, you draw white and the other two vote sincerely. Which way should you vote and why?
AnswerMixed. Your vote matters only if the others split, which under sincere voting means one drew green, which means the urn is mixed for certain. (Exact check: 0.995 correct this way vs 0.986 if everyone is sincere.)
Q12. Why does the jury theorem fail with sequential voting?
AnswerVotes are observed, so a cascade starts once the net count reaches 2. Later voters ignore their own signals, the effective sample stays at the first few signals, and a wrong cascade has a fixed positive probability however large N is.

One-page recap

  • Cascade: sequential choices + private signals + only actions visible. Starts when the net revealed count reaches 2 (for 2/3-accurate signals). Can be wrong, uses little information, is fragile.
  • Voting system: function from complete, transitive rankings to a group ranking.
  • Majority rule: complete but can cycle (Condorcet paradox), so the agenda setter decides.
  • Borda: transitive but violates IIA and invites lying. Plurality: ignores lower ranks, vote-splitting.
  • Different rules, different winners (budget example).
  • Arrow: with 3 or more alternatives, unanimity + IIA forces a dictator.
  • Jury theorem: independent, simultaneous, sincere votes with q > ½ make the majority right with probability → 1. It fails with insincere (pivotal) voting and with sequential voting.
