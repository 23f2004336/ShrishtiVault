Maths you need this week
Probability of both: for independent events multiply. If P1 plays H with probability p and P2 plays H with probability q, the cell (H, H) has probability pq.
Expected payoff: add up (probability × payoff) over the possible outcomes. 50% of 10 and 50% of 0 gives 5.
Slope of a straight line: in U = (1 − q) + p(4q − 1), the number multiplying p, namely (4q − 1), is the slope. Positive slope: bigger p is better, so p = 1. Negative: p = 0. Zero: any p is equally good.
Solving "set two things equal": this is just a one-variable equation, e.g. 2q − 1 = 1 − 2q gives 4q = 2, so q = 1/2.

1. How the solution concepts relate

Reading the picture: every dominant strategy equilibrium is a NE, and every NE survives IESDS. The reverse does not hold. Each statement below is proved.

Theorem 1. A game has at most one strictly dominant strategy equilibrium

Proof (by contradiction). Suppose there are two different ones, a* and b*. Then some player i has a*i ≠ b*i.
1. a*i is strictly dominant, so it beats every other action against every opponent profile. Use the profile b*−i and the other action b*i:ui(a*i, b*−i) > ui(b*i, b*−i) 2. b*i is also strictly dominant. Use the profile b*−i and the other action a*i:ui(b*i, b*−i) > ui(a*i, b*−i) 3. Put them together: u(a*i, ·) > u(b*i, ·) > u(a*i, ·). A number cannot be bigger than itself. Contradiction. ∎

Theorem 2. Dominant strategy equilibrium ⟹ Nash equilibrium (not the other way)

Proof. Dominance says: for every player i, ui(a*i, a−i) ≥ ui(a′i, a−i) for all a′i and all a−i. "All" includes the one profile a−i = a*−i, soui(a*i, a*−i) ≥ ui(a′i, a*−i) for all a′i That is exactly the Nash condition. ∎
Why the reverse fails: Battle of the Sexes
P1 \ P2Action (A)Romantic (R)
A3, 10, 0

Best responses: BR1(A) = A, BR1(R) = R, and the same for P2. So (A, A) and (R, R) are NE. But for P1, A beats R when P2 plays A (3 > 0), and loses when P2 plays R (0 < 1). No dominant action, so neither NE is a dominant strategy equilibrium.

Theorem 3. IESDS never deletes a Nash equilibrium

Proof (by contradiction). Suppose a NE a* loses a component. Deletions happen in finite rounds, so look at the first round k where some a*i is deleted.
1. In rounds before k nothing from a* was deleted, so a*−i is still available to the opponents.
2. a*i is deleted in round k, so some a′i (pure or mixed) strictly beats it against every remaining opponent profile. Remaining profiles include a*−i:ui(a′i, a*−i) > ui(a*i, a*−i) 3. That says player i gains by deviating from a*, so a* is not a NE. Contradiction. ∎

Theorem 4. If only one profile survives IESDS, it is the unique NE

Proof. Existence: by Nash's theorem (Section 5) a NE exists, and by Theorem 3 it survives. Only one profile survives, so that profile is a NE. Uniqueness: any other NE would also survive (Theorem 3), but only one profile survived. ∎

Both (U, L) and (D, R) are NE. (Check (D, R): P1 switching to U gets 0, no gain. P2 switching to L gets 0, no gain.) Now delete weakly dominated actions: U weakly dominates D (1 > 0 vs L, 0 = 0 vs R). Delete D. Then L beats R for P2. Delete R. Only (U, L) survives, so the NE (D, R) was lost.

Three ways to find a pure NE (from the slides)

  1. Every player has a dominant strategy: play them.
  2. Delete dominated strategies; whatever survives is a candidate.
  3. Best responses: mark each player's best replies and look for cells where both are marked.

2. Why we need mixed strategies

Some games have no pure NE. These are games of pure conflict, where being predictable gets you beaten.

Zero-sum: u1 + u2 = 0 in every cell. Constant-sum: u1 + u2 = c. One player's gain is exactly the other's loss.
Rock-paper-scissorsRockPaperScissors
Rock0, 0−1, 11, −1
Paper1, −10, 0−1, 1
Scissors−1, 11, −10, 0

Best replies chase each other in a circle: against Rock play Paper, against Paper play Scissors, against Scissors play Rock. No cell is stable. Matching pennies (Week 1) behaves the same way: if P2 plays H, P1 wants H; but then P2 wants T; then P1 wants T; then P2 wants H.

The fix: let players randomise. That adds new candidate equilibria. The cost is that we must now explain what randomising means (Section 9).

Mixed strategy. A probability αi(a) on each pure action a in Ai, with all probabilities ≥ 0 and Σa ∈ Ai αi(a) = 1 The set of all such αi is the simplex Δ(Ai). It "convexifies" the choice space: any blend of two strategies is also a strategy. A pure action is the special case with probability 1 on one action.
Support: supp(αi) = the pure actions with positive probability.
Expected payoff (players randomise independently). For a profile α = (α1, ..., αn): Ui(α) = Σa ∈ A [ α1(a1) × ... × αn(an) ] · ui(a) Each cell's payoff is weighted by the chance of that cell. Mixed Nash equilibrium: Ui(α*) ≥ Ui(αi, α*−i) for every player i and every mixed strategy αi.
Solving rock-paper-scissors. Let P2 play Rock, Paper, Scissors with probabilities qR, qP, qS. P1's expected payoffs:
Rock: 0·qR − qP + qS   Paper: qR − qS   Scissors: −qR + qP.
For P1 to be happy mixing, all three must be equal. Set Rock = Scissors: −qP + qS = −qR + qP, so qR + qS = 2qP. With qR + qP + qS = 1 this gives 1 − qP = 2qP, so qP = 1/3. The same steps for the other pairs give qR = qS = 1/3. By symmetry, both play (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) and the value is 0.

3. The indifference principle

Theorem 5. In a mixed NE α*, player i's expected payoff against α*−i:
(1) is the same for every pure action in her support, and equals her equilibrium payoff c;
(2) is at most c for every pure action outside the support.
Proof. Expected payoff is a weighted average of the pure-action payoffs in the support: Ui(α*) = Σk ∈ supp α*i(ak) · Ui(ak, α*−i) Let M be the best pure-action payoff available against α*−i. Suppose some action ak̂ in the support pays strictly less than M. Replace every payoff in the sum by M (bigger or equal, and strictly bigger for ak̂ which has positive weight): Ui(α*) < M · Σ α*i(ak) = M · 1 = M So she would do strictly better by just playing the best pure action (payoff M). Then α*i was not a best response. Contradiction. Hence every support action pays exactly M = c, and outside-support actions cannot pay more than M. ∎
Plain-English version: a weighted average cannot beat its best ingredient. If she is willing to put weight on an action, that action must be as good as the best one. Practical rule for solving: to find the probabilities a player uses, make the other player indifferent between her pure actions.

5. Nash's existence theorem

Theorem 6 (Nash, 1950). Every finite game (finitely many players, each with finitely many actions) has at least one Nash equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies.

This is reassuring but does not tell you how to find it. Finiteness is enough but not necessary: some infinite games (like Cournot) also have equilibria.

Words used in the proof, in plain terms. Convex set: if two points are inside, every blend of them is inside too (a filled circle is convex, a ring is not). Mixed strategies are blends, so they form a convex set. Compact: closed (includes its edges) and bounded (does not run off to infinity). Fixed point: a point that a map sends to itself; stir a cup of coffee and, by a similar theorem, some point ends up where it started. Correspondence: a rule that can return a set of answers (ties), not just one.
Idea of the proof (sketch). Define the combined best-response map BR(α) = BR1(α−1) × ... × BRn(α−n). A NE is a point with α* ∈ BR(α*), a fixed point. Kakutani's fixed-point theorem gives one if:
1. The strategy space (a product of simplexes) is non-empty, compact and convex. ✔
2. BR(α) is never empty: expected payoff is continuous and the choice set is compact. ✔
3. BR(α) is convex: if two strategies are both best, any blend of them is too, because expected payoff is linear in the probabilities. ✔
4. BR has a closed graph (limits of best responses are best responses), from the maximum theorem. ✔

6. Mixed NE of Battle of the Sexes

Using the table from Section 1. P1 plays A with probability p. P2 plays A with probability q.

Step 1: expected payoffs. Weight each cell by its probability: U1 = 3(pq) + 1((1−p)(1−q)) = 3pq + 1 − p − q + pq = (1 − q) + p(4q − 1) U2 = 1(pq) + 3((1−p)(1−q)) = pq + 3 − 3p − 3q + 3pq = (3 − 3p) + q(4p − 3) Factoring out the player's own variable shows how it moves her payoff.
Step 2: best responses. U1 changes by (4q − 1) per unit of p. If q > ¼, p = 1. If q < ¼, p = 0. If q = ¼, any p. For P2, the slope is (4p − 3): if p > ¾, q = 1; if p < ¾, q = 0; if p = ¾, any q. BR1(q): 0 if q < ¼, [0,1] if q = ¼, 1 if q > ¼    BR2(p): 0 if p < ¾, [0,1] if p = ¾, 1 if p > ¾ Step 3: intersections. Three points: (p, q) = (1, 1), (0, 0), and (¾, ¼).

7. Application: reporting a crime (the bystander effect)

Story. In 1964, Kitty Genovese was murdered in New York. Reports said 38 witnesses did not call the police. Social psychologists explain it with diffusion of responsibility (more people, less guilt for not helping), audience inhibition and social influence (others' inaction signals "no need"). They all say the benefit or cost per person changes as the crowd grows.

The game-theory question: can the chance that nobody helps rise with the crowd even when each person's cost and benefit stay exactly the same?

Model. n identical witnesses, each chooses Report (R) or Don't (N). If at least one reports, each witness gets V. Calling costs C. Assume V > C > 0 (each prefers the crime reported, but prefers someone else to pay). Payoffs for n = 2:
1 \ 2Don't (N)Report (R)
N0, 0V, V − C
RV − C, VV − C, V − C

Pure NE: exactly one person calls. (The caller gets V − C > 0 and would get 0 by quitting. A non-caller gets V and would get only V − C by calling.) There are n of these, and the witnesses cannot agree on who calls. So look for the symmetric mixed NE.

Derivation. Let p = probability that a witness does not call. Fix witness i.
1. If i calls: payoff = V − C (the crime is surely reported).
2. If i does not call: the other n − 1 all stay silent with probability pn−1. So at least one other calls with probability 1 − pn−1. Payoff = V(1 − pn−1) + 0·pn−1.
3. Indifference (so she is willing to mix): V − C = V(1 − pn−1) = V − V pn−1 4. Cancel V: C = V pn−1, so pn−1 = C/V and p* = (C/V)1/(n−1)
What happens as n grows?
Individual: each calls with probability 1 − p* = 1 − (C/V)1/(n−1). Since 0 < C/V < 1 and 1/(n−1) → 0, (C/V)1/(n−1) → (C/V)0 = 1. So 1 − p* → 0. Each person becomes less likely to call.
Group: nobody calls with probability (p*)n = (C/V)n/(n−1). Write the exponent as n/(n−1) = 1 + 1/(n−1), which falls toward 1 as n grows. Raising a fraction between 0 and 1 to a smaller power makes it bigger. So P(unreported) rises with n and P(reported) = 1 − (C/V)n/(n−1) falls, approaching 1 − C/V but never below it.
Numbers: V = 10, C = 2.5, so C/V = 0.25.

Takeaway: the bystander effect can come purely from rational mixing. No change in cost or benefit is needed: more people means each relies on someone else, and together they are more likely to all rely on each other.

8. Dominance by mixed strategies and "never a best response"

Example from the slides. Left matrix: P1's second row (4, 4) against rows (10, 0) and (0, 10). A 50-50 mix of rows 1 and 3 pays ½(10) + ½(0) = 5 against column 1 and ½(0) + ½(10) = 5 against column 2. 5 > 4 both times, so row 2 is strictly dominated by the mix, though not by any single row. In the right-hand version row 2 is (6, 6): the mix pays 5 < 6, so it does not dominate. Indeed if P2 plays 50-50, rows 1 and 3 pay 5 but row 2 pays 6, so row 2 is a best response there.

Belief: a player's guess (a probability) about what the others do. Never a best response (NBR): an action that is not the best choice under any belief.
Facts: (1) In a mixed NE, only best-response actions get positive probability, so an NBR action is never used. (2) A strictly dominated action is NBR. (3) An action is NBR if and only if it is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy. The idea behind (3): if no belief makes the action best, there is always a blend that beats it everywhere.

The spy example (Watson, Ch. 11)

Step 3: best responses. For q < 2/3 the best is d. At q = 2/3, a, c and d tie. For q > 2/3 the best is a. So c is a best response only at exactly q = 2/3; b is never best (at q = 2/3 it pays 1 + 6.67 = 7.67 < 8).

Step 5: all Nash equilibria. Pure NE? If 002 plays x, 001's best is a, but against a 002 prefers y (6 > 0). If 002 plays y, 001's best is d, but against d 002 prefers x (3 > 0). The best replies chase each other, so no pure NE.
Mixed NE. 001 mixes over a, c, d only (b is dropped), so 001 needs q = 2/3 (Step 2). For 002 to mix, 002 must be indifferent. Let 001 play a, c, d with probabilities pa, pc, pd: U2(x) = 0·pa + 2pc + 3pd    U2(y) = 6pa + 2pc + 0·pd Set equal: 3pd = 6pa, so pd = 2pa. Then pa + pc + 2pa = 1 gives pc = 1 − 3pa, which needs pa ≤ 1/3. NE: 001 plays (pa, 0, 1 − 3pa, 2pa) for any pa in [0, 1/3]; 002 plays x with probability 2/3 Payoffs: 001 gets 8, 002 gets 2pc + 3pd = 2(1 − 3pa) + 6pa = 2. A whole continuum of equilibria.

War game (3×3): solved without trusting dominance

Command 1 attacks Left, Center or Right (L′, C′, R′). Command 2 defends Left, Center or Right (L, C, R). Payoffs (Cmd 1, Cmd 2):

1 \ 2LCR
L′0, 33, 14, 0
C′2, 11, 22, 1

Step 1: shrink the game. Compare C′ with ½L′ + ½R′. Against L: 2 vs 2. Against C: 2 vs 1. Against R: 2 vs 2. The mix is never worse and once better, so C′ is weakly dominated (not strictly). Likewise, once C′ is gone, C is weakly dominated by ½L + ½R (1.5 vs 1 against L′, and 2 vs 2 against R′). Drop both to get a 2×2:
1 \ 2L (q)R (1−q)
L′ (p)0, 34, 0
R′ (1−p)4, 00, 4
Step 2: indifference. Make Cmd 1 indifferent: 0·q + 4(1 − q) = 4q + 0 ⟹ 4 − 4q = 4q ⟹ q = ½. Make Cmd 2 indifferent: 3p + 0 = 0 + 4(1 − p) ⟹ 7p = 4 ⟹ p = 4/7.
Step 3: check in the full 3×3 (necessary, because deleting weakly dominated actions can lose equilibria). Cmd 2 plays (½, 0, ½). Cmd 1 gets L′: 0(½) + 4(½) = 2, C′: 2(½) + 2(½) = 2, R′: 4(½) + 0 = 2. All equal 2, so Cmd 1 is happy to play (4/7, 0, 3/7). Cmd 1 plays (4/7, 0, 3/7). Cmd 2 gets L: 3(4/7) = 12/7, C: 1(4/7) + 2(3/7) = 10/7, R: 4(3/7) = 12/7. C is worse (10/7 < 12/7), so giving it zero weight is right. It is a NE: payoffs 2 for Cmd 1 and 12/7 ≈ 1.71 for Cmd 2.
Step 4: more equilibria. Because C′ ties for Cmd 1, Cmd 1 may also use it. Let Cmd 1 play (4t, 1 − 7t, 3t). Cmd 2's payoffs against it: L = 3(4t) + 1(1 − 7t) = 1 + 5t and R = 1(1 − 7t) + 4(3t) = 1 + 5t, so L and R are always equal (Cmd 2 can keep mixing ½-½). C = 4t + 2(1 − 7t) + 2(3t) = 2 − 4t. Cmd 2 does not want C when 2 − 4t ≤ 1 + 5t, i.e. t ≥ 1/9. Also 1 − 7t ≥ 0 needs t ≤ 1/7. So every t in [1/9, 1/7] is a NE, and t = 1/7 is the one found above.
Why this matters: reducing a game by weak dominance is only a shortcut. Always plug the answer back into the full game.

9. What does a mixed equilibrium mean?

Do people really roll dice? Two common readings:

  • Steady state, individual randomisation: the game is played over and over, and the probabilities are the long-run frequencies of each action.
  • Steady state, population: in Battle of the Sexes, think of a large group of P1-types and P2-types. "p = ¾" means 75% of P1-types always play A and 25% always play R. Random pairing gives the same average payoffs as individual randomisation.

Is the steady state stable?

Battle of the Sexes: unstable. Start at the mixed point and nudge P1 to p = ¾ + ε. P2's slope is 4p − 3 = 4ε > 0, so P2's best reply is q = 1. Then P1's best reply to q = 1 is p = 1. The system slides to the pure NE (A, A). A nudge the other way slides to (R, R). So the mixed point is like a ball balanced on a hilltop.
Matching pennies: self-correcting (neutral). Nudge P1 to p = ½ + ε. P2's slope is 1 − 2p = −2ε < 0, so P2 moves toward q = 0. Once q is below ½, P1 wants to lower p. The play circles around (½, ½) instead of running away.

The puzzle, and Harsanyi's answer

Puzzle: at a mixed NE a player is indifferent among the actions in her support. Why would she pick exactly the probabilities that keep the other indifferent?

Harsanyi's purification (1973). Suppose each player's payoffs have a tiny private random shock (a mood, a small preference). Each player knows her own shock but not the other's. Then she is almost never exactly indifferent, so she plays a plain pure action. From the opponent's view, her action looks random. As the shocks shrink to zero, the share of shock values on which she picks each action converges to the mixed NE probabilities. So the randomness is just hidden information, not dice.
