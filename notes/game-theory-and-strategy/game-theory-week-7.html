1. Why study matching?

In most markets, price decides who gets what: if more people want a thing, its price goes up until supply equals demand. But there are many cases where price cannot do this job:

  • Doctors and hospitals: each new doctor wants a particular hospital and each hospital wants particular doctors.
  • Students and schools: a public school cannot auction its seats.
  • Kidneys for transplant: selling organs is ruled out on ethical grounds.

An allocation must still be made. These are two-sided matching markets: both sides have preferences, and each person is matched to someone on the other side. The question is: how do we match them well, and what does "well" even mean?

The Nobel Prize (2012) went to Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth. Shapley developed the abstract theory in the 1960s. Roth did the empirical work in the 1980s and then helped redesign real markets (doctors, schools, kidney exchange).

1.1 Where it started: Yale admissions (1962)

David Gale and Lloyd Shapley wrote the paper "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" (RAND Corporation, 1962). It began with a New Yorker article about the trouble with undergraduate admissions at Yale. The trouble:

  • Students apply to many universities. An admissions officer cannot tell who is serious about coming.
  • Students have every reason to pretend that each university is their first choice.
  • Universities, knowing many offers will be turned down, admit more students than they have seats.
  • The whole thing became a guessing game, and the final enrolments were far from the best possible.
What Gale and Shapley did. (1) They defined the idea of a stable matching. (2) They proved that a stable matching always exists. (3) To keep it simple they started with a toy case: exactly n universities, n applicants, and each university has one seat. That is the same as matching n men with n women, which is why the paper's title mentions marriage.

The path of this week.

2. The matching problem

Setting (the marriage problem). A community has the same number of men and women. Each person ranks all members of the opposite sex from best to worst. No ties: nobody is indifferent between two people (we relax this in section 8). The objective: propose a good matching, i.e. pair every man with exactly one woman.

Why it is a game-theory problem: the players (men and women) have preferences and can walk away from a proposed pairing. A matching is only useful if people will actually stay in it.

3. Three attempts at a good matching

A matching (or "matching system") pairs each man with exactly one woman. With 4 men there are 4! = 24 possible matchings. Which one should a matchmaker announce? The slides try three natural ideas.

3.1 Attempt 1: an arbitrary matchmaker's pairing

Al–Dot, Bob–Ann, Cal–Beth, Dan–Cher. Ranks (man × woman): 2×2, 2×2, 2×3, 2×4.

Solid green = the matchmaker's pairs. Dashed red = the pair that will break it.

Why it fails. Cher is stuck with Dan, her last choice. Could Cher be happier elsewhere? She asks:
• Bob (her 1st choice)? Bob ranks Cher last, so he says no.
• Cal (her 2nd)? Cal has Beth, his 2nd choice, and ranks Cher only 3rd, so he says no.
• Al (her 3rd, still better than Dan)? Al ranks Cher 1st, and he is currently with Dot, his 2nd choice. He says yes.
So Al and Cher prefer each other to the partners they were given. They will ignore the matchmaker and pair up. The proposal is feasible but not a good one: it will not survive.

3.2 Attempt 2: give every man his first choice

First choices: Al wants Cher, Bob wants Dot, Cal wants Ann, Dan wants Dot. Conflict: Bob and Dan both want Dot. Dot prefers Bob (Bob is her 3rd, Dan her 4th), so Bob gets Dot, and Dan is pushed to the only woman left, Beth.

Check for trouble. Al, Bob and Cal have their first choice, so they will never walk away. Dan has Beth, his last choice, and would love to leave. Whom could he ask? Dot, Cher, Ann: all four women rank Dan last, so none will leave her partner for him. No pair wants to break away. This matching is stable. (Slide notation: 1×3, 1×3, 1×3, 4×4.)

3.3 Attempt 3: give every woman her first choice

First choices: Ann wants Al, Beth wants Al, Cher wants Bob, Dot wants Cal. Conflict: Ann and Beth both want Al. Al prefers Ann (his 3rd) to Beth (his 4th), so Ann gets Al, and Beth is pushed to Dan, her last choice. Result: Ann–Al, Beth–Dan, Cher–Bob, Dot–Cal.

Why it fails. Beth (with Dan, her 4th choice) approaches Bob. Bob is with Cher, whom he ranks 4th; he ranks Beth 3rd, so Bob is happier with Beth. Beth ranks Bob 2nd, much better than Dan. Beth and Bob both gain, so they reject the matching and pair up. Unstable.
Correction to the lecture summary. The summary says Bob's partner Cher is his "3rd choice". From the slide table, Bob ranks Cher 4th and Beth 3rd. The conclusion (Beth and Bob block) is the same.
The lesson. "Make one side happy" is not enough. What matters is whether some man and some woman, not paired with each other, would rather be together. That is the idea of stability.

4. Stable matching

Blocking pair. In a matching, a man m and a woman w who are not paired with each other form a blocking pair if m prefers w to his own partner, and w prefers m to her own partner. Stable matching: a matching with no blocking pair. Written with symbols: for no (m, w) do we have w ≻m μ(m) and m ≻w μ(w), where μ(x) means "the partner of x under matching μ".

It is the same idea as a Nash equilibrium: a matching is stable when no pair gains by deviating together.

To test a matching, check every unmatched pair. Only a pair that both want counts.

4.1 A worked check (slide example with letters a to d and A to D)

Slide data (ranks): woman A ranks the men a, b, c, d as 1, 2, 3, 4; B ranks them 2, 4, 1, 3; C ranks them 4, 2, 1, 3; D ranks them 2, 1, 3, 4. Man a ranks A, B, C, D as 4, 2, 1, 3; b: 2, 1, 3, 4; c: 3, 1, 4, 2; d: 2, 4, 1, 3. So the lists are:

Aa ≻ b ≻ c ≻ daC ≻ B ≻ D ≻ A
Bc ≻ a ≻ d ≻ bbB ≻ A ≻ C ≻ D
Cc ≻ b ≻ d ≻ acB ≻ D ≻ A ≻ C
Db ≻ a ≻ c ≻ ddC ≻ A ≻ D ≻ B

Candidate matching: A–b, B–c, C–d, D–a.

Check each man for a better woman who would take him.
• c has B, his 1st choice, and d has C, his 1st choice. They need look no further.
• b has A (his 2nd). He prefers only B. But B has c, her 1st choice, and ranks b last. B says no.
• a has D (his 3rd). He prefers C and B. C has d and ranks a last, so no. B has c, her 1st choice, and ranks a only 2nd, so no.
No man finds a woman who prefers him to her partner, so there is no blocking pair. The matching is stable. (A computer check of all 24 matchings confirms this is the only stable one here.)

Why matching 3 is stable. In A–c, B–a, C–b: a (with B, his 2nd) prefers A, but A (with c, her 2nd) prefers only b, and a is her last. b (with C, 2nd) prefers B, but B ranks b last. c (with A, 2nd) prefers C, but C ranks c last. Nobody has a willing partner above their match, so it is stable. Take-away: there can be many stable matchings.
Stable does not mean everyone is happy. A stable matching just means no pair can improve together. Some people may get low-ranked partners (in matching 1 every woman gets her 3rd choice, which is her last). Stability serves the matchmaker: the recommendation will be honoured, because nobody has a reason to walk away. It does not promise to serve everyone's interests.

5. The Roommate problem: a stable matching may not exist

Roommate problem. Divide an even-sized group into pairs of roommates. Any person can be paired with any other (there are no two "sides"). A set of pairs is stable if no two people, not roommates, prefer each other to their actual roommates.

Slide example with four boys a, b, c, d (each ranks the other three):

1st2nd3rd
abcd
bcad
cabd
dabc

a, b and c each chase the next one in a circle; d is last for all three.

There are only three ways to split four people into two pairs. Each has a blocking pair:
PairsWho wants to break away, and why
(a,b), (c,d)b and c: b has a (his 2nd) but prefers c; c has d (her last) and prefers b to d. Both gain.
(a,c), (b,d)a and b: a has c (2nd) but prefers b; b has d (last) and prefers a to d.
(a,d), (b,c)a and c: a has d (last) and prefers c; c has b (2nd) but prefers a.
Every possible matching is unstable, so no stable matching exists.
Why is the two-sided marriage problem different? In the roommate problem the preferences can go round in a circle (a → b → c → a), so whoever you fix, someone else can steal. With two separate sides, men only rank women and women only rank men, and the algorithm in the next section shows that a stable matching always exists. So the two-sided structure is what guarantees a stable matching. Drop it, and a stable matching may fail to exist.

6. The Gale-Shapley algorithm (deferred acceptance)

Three questions from the slides: (1) Does a stable matching always exist (for equal numbers of men and women)? (2) How do we find one? (3) Is there only one? Gale and Shapley answered the first two with one simple procedure. The third answer is "no" (we saw three in Example 4).

Rules of the algorithm (men propose).
Stage 1. Every man proposes to the woman at the top of his list. Every woman who gets more than one proposal keeps her favourite and tells the others "no, never". Each man who is not rejected is on a waiting list (she is holding him, not yet marrying him).
Stage 2. Every man who was rejected proposes to the next woman on his list. Each woman now looks at everyone who proposed to her, including the man she is already holding, keeps her favourite among them, and rejects the rest.
Stage 3, 4, … repeat the same.
Stop when nobody is rejected. Everyone on a waiting list becomes a match.

The whole algorithm is a loop of "propose, then keep the best and reject the rest".

Why "deferred" acceptance? A woman never says "yes, forever" in the middle of the process. She only says "maybe, I'm holding you" and may drop him if a better man shows up. The final "yes" is deferred to the end. This is the key to why the result is stable.

6.3 The two basic facts (GS-P1 and GS-P2)

GS-P1: the algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps.
GS-P2: it terminates in a stable matching. So for equal numbers of men and women, a stable matching always exists.

7. Proofs that the algorithm works

These are the proofs behind GS-P1 and GS-P2. They use only careful counting and the "suppose not, then contradiction" idea.

Fact A (a woman's holdings only get better). Once a woman receives a proposal, she is never again without a partner (she holds someone from then on), and every time she changes the man she holds, the new man is better in her ranking than the old one (she only swaps for her favourite). Reason: at each stage she keeps the best among {the man she holds} ∪ {new proposers}, which is at least as good as the man she held.
Proof of GS-P1 (it stops).
1. A man never proposes to the same woman twice, because after a rejection he moves to the next name on his list. So each man proposes at most n times (once per woman).
2. So the total number of proposals is at most n × n = n².
3. In every stage that does not stop the algorithm, at least one man is rejected and proposes again, i.e. at least one new proposal is made.
4. Since there are at most n² proposals, there are at most n² stages. So the algorithm stops. (A sharper count shows at most n² − 2n + 2 proposals are ever needed; the simple bound n² is enough for the proof. The 4×4 examples used 7 and 9 proposals; 4² = 16.)
Everyone gets matched (equal numbers). Suppose, when the algorithm stops, some man is unmatched. A man stops proposing only when he has been rejected by every woman. But a woman who has received a proposal always holds someone (Fact A), so every woman is holding some man. That is n women holding n different men, so all n men are held, contradicting "some man is unmatched". So everyone is matched.
Proof of GS-P2 (the result is stable). Let μ be the final matching.
1. Suppose, for contradiction, that μ has a blocking pair (m, w). So m and w are not matched together, w ≻m μ(m) (m prefers w to his final partner) and m ≻w μ(w) (w prefers m to her final partner).
2. Men propose in order of preference. Since m prefers w to his final partner, he must have proposed to w earlier (before he ever got to μ(m)).
3. Because m did not end up with w, w must have rejected m at some stage. When she rejected him, she was holding or receiving a man m′ she liked better: m′ ≻w m.
4. By Fact A, w's partners only improve over time, so her final partner is at least as good as m′: μ(w) ≽w m′ ≻w m.
5. That says w prefers her final partner to m. This contradicts step 1 (w prefers m to μ(w)).
6. So no blocking pair exists, and μ is stable. ∎
In words. If a man likes a woman more than his final match, he asked her first. She turned him down only for someone better, and she never goes backwards. So she is better off than she would be with him.

Key result: the algorithm works for any numbers. The number of matched pairs is the smaller of the two sides (here 3). A man or woman left over is single, and they cannot form a blocking pair with a matched person, because the algorithm already tried them.

8.2 Incomplete lists (some people are unacceptable)

In real life you may prefer to stay single rather than be paired with someone awful. Each person submits a list of only those they would accept. A matching must now also be individually rational: nobody is matched with someone they find unacceptable.

Algorithm change: men propose only to women on their list; a woman immediately rejects anyone who is not on her list.

Slide example (0 in a rank table means "unacceptable"): women A, B, C; men a, b, c, d.

8.3 Ties (indifference)

A man may be equally happy with two women (for example B and C). Then "prefer" needs a rule. The slide uses:

Rule: nobody leaves a partner for someone they like equally. A pair blocks only if both strictly prefer each other to their partners. (The summary calls this "weak stability" and also describes a stronger version where one side is only indifferent; the slides use just the one above.)

Slide example: women A, B, C; men a, b, c. Women's ranks (rows = men): a: 3 1 3; b: 2 2 1; c: 1 3 2. Men's ranks: a: 1 2 2; b: 1 1 2; c: 1 2 3. So man a is indifferent between B and C; man b is indifferent between A and B.

What happens when the algorithm gets stuck on a tie? At the start b must choose between A and B, which he likes equally. The slide's fix: break the tie any way you like (it just needs to make the lists strict) and then run the usual algorithm. The slide breaks the tie as: a: A ≻ C ≻ B (men's ranks 1 3 2), b: B ≻ A ≻ C (2 1 3), c unchanged. Run: a→A, b→B, c→A. A holds c (her favourite), rejects a. a→C (his next). Result A–c, B–b, C–a.
A check by computer: with the tie broken this way, there are two stable matchings (A–c, B–b, C–a and A–c, B–a, C–b); the one found is stable for the original tied lists too.
Take-away. Ties are no real problem: break them arbitrarily and run the algorithm. The result is stable in the sense above.

• Men: a gets rank 2 in System 1 (3 in System 2, 2 in System 3). b gets rank 1 in System 1. c rank 1. d rank 2 (4 in System 2, 3 in System 3). So System 1 is best for every man.
• The conflict: every woman is worst off (or tied) in System 1, which is exactly where every man is best off.
Summary vs slides. The lecture summary lists these rank vectors but attaches the wrong vectors to some systems and claims, for instance, that woman D is rank 3 in System 3. The slide (and the matchings above) give D rank 2 in System 3. Use the table above.

9.3 Why the proposing side wins (proof)

The slides state GS-P3A without a proof. Here is the standard one. It is the hardest proof of the week, so go slowly.

Possible partner. Call a woman possible for a man if there is some stable matching in which they are partners. The best any man could hope for is his favourite possible woman.
Claim: in men-proposing Gale-Shapley, no man is ever rejected by a woman who is possible for him. If the claim is true, every man ends up with his favourite possible woman, which is exactly "optimal for the men".
Proof (by contradiction, looking at the first mistake).
1. Suppose some man m is, at some stage, rejected by a woman w who is possible for him. Take the first time in the whole run that this happens.
2. Since w is possible for m, there is a stable matching μ with μ(m) = w.
3. She rejected m because she had a better man m′: m′ ≻w m. (m′ either just proposed or was already held by her.)
4. Since m′ proposed to w and men go down their lists, m′ has been rejected by every woman he prefers to w. By step 1 (first mistake), none of those rejections was by a woman possible for him. In particular, μ(m′), his partner in the stable matching μ, is not someone who rejected him. So m′ likes w at least as much as μ(m′). They are different women (μ(m) = w), so w ≻m′ μ(m′).
5. Now look at μ: m′ prefers w to μ(m′), and w prefers m′ to m = μ(w). So (m′, w) is a blocking pair for μ, contradicting that μ is stable.
6. So the first mistake never happens. ∎
Women get their worst possible (woman-pessimality). Let μ_M be the men-proposing result. Suppose some stable matching μ gave a woman w a partner m′ she likes less than her man m = μ_M(w). In μ, m has some other partner μ(m) ≠ w. By men-optimality, w = μ_M(m) is at least as good for m as μ(m), and since it is a different woman, strictly better: w ≻m μ(m). Also m ≻w m′. Then (m, w) is a blocking pair for μ, a contradiction. So every woman gets her worst stable partner when men propose.
Meaning for market design. "Who proposes" is a policy choice. The proposing side gets its best stable matching, the other side its worst. Check with the numbers: in System 1 (men propose) every woman has rank 3, her worst stable rank in this market.

9.4 Can the receiving side cheat? (an example)

The slides say that in some algorithms the receiving side can gain by lying (for example, turning down offers it actually prefers), and that the NRMP was redesigned to be immune to this for students. Here is a concrete case using Example 4 with men proposing.

Setup. Truthful lists (Example 4): A: b ≻ c ≻ a; B: c ≻ a ≻ b; C: a ≻ b ≻ c; a: A ≻ B ≻ C; b: B ≻ C ≻ A; c: C ≻ A ≻ B. Men propose truthfully: result A–a, B–b, C–c. So woman A gets her last choice a.
The lie. Woman A reports a list with only b on it (she says "I will accept nobody else"). Run: a→A (rejected, unacceptable), b→B, c→C. Then a→B: B has b and a, she prefers a, so rejects b. b→C: C has c and b, prefers b, rejects c. c→A: A rejects (unacceptable). c→B: B prefers c, rejects a. a→C: C prefers a to b, rejects b. b→A: A accepts b. Final: A–b, B–c, C–a. A now has her first choice b. She gained by lying.
And the men? I tested every possible list (every order of every subset of the women) for each man in this example and checked that no man gets a better woman by lying. This is the "strategyproofness for the proposing side" the summary describes: with men proposing, telling the truth is a dominant strategy for the men, but not for the women. (The slides state this result for the NRMP and school-choice redesigns; the full proof is beyond this course, so I only show the example.)

10. College admissions: many students, few seats

Assignment problem: matching real people to real things. Real courtship does not follow the algorithm, so the marriage story is of limited practical value, but with a slight generalisation Gale-Shapley can be applied to real problems. The first one is college admissions, where a college has a quota (many seats), not just one.

10.1 Why the decentralised way is a mess

  • A college must decide whom to accept. If it only admits up to its quota, applicants who got several offers turn some down, and seats stay empty (the quota is not filled).
  • If it admits more than the quota to cover for rejections, and too few reject, it ends up over-full (more than it can absorb).
  • The college does not know whether an applicant applied elsewhere, how she ranks colleges, or who else will admit her. So it must plan for a class that may not fill and one that may overflow.
  • Applicants have problems too. If asked to list all colleges in order, she may think "if I tell College X it is only my 3rd choice, I will hurt my chances". With waiting lists it is worse: should she accept College 1 now (safe) or hold out for College 2, whose waiting list may admit her later? Is it ethical to accept one college and then withdraw?
Root of the trouble: the colleges lack data about the applicants, and applicants are suspicious of the colleges.

10.2 The placement center (a clearinghouse)

What each side submits (from the slides).
Applicant: a ranked list of the colleges she is willing to accept, leaving out only those she would not accept even if they had vacancies.
College: its quota, and a ranked list of applicants, saying which ones it will not admit even if seats are empty.
The center then prunes each applicant's list, deleting colleges that would never admit her.
The procedure (applicants apply).
Stage 1. Every applicant is sent to her first-choice college. A college whose number of applicants is at most its quota keeps them all on a waiting list. If more applied than the quota, it keeps its top quota-many (by its own ranking) and rejects the rest.
Stage 2, 3, … Rejected applicants go to their next choice. Each college adds the new applicants to its waiting list, keeps its top quota-many of all of them, and rejects the rest.
Stop when there are no more rejections (every applicant is on a waiting list or has used up her list). Each college admits everyone on its final list. The assignment is stable.

Check stability. s4 would like X, but both X's students rank above s4 for X. s2 would like Y, but Y prefers s1. s1 and s3 have their first choice. So no applicant and college would rather be together: stable. No seat is left empty, no college is over-full, and nobody had to guess.
Why the stability proof carries over. Treat a college with quota q as q separate seats with the same ranking. The "she only ever improves, and a man (applicant) who prefers her asked her first" argument works unchanged. So the many-to-one algorithm also ends in a stable assignment and removes the guessing games.

11. The market for doctors (NRMP)

The slow recognition that the Gale-Shapley paper mattered in real life.

11.1 The problem before 1950

In the US, medical-school graduates work as residents (interns) at hospitals. In the early 1900s the market was decentralised. In the 1940s, competition for scarce students pushed hospitals to offer positions earlier and earlier, sometimes years before graduation.

  • Matches were made before students could show evidence of their qualifications, and even before they knew which branch of medicine they wanted.
  • When an offer was rejected, it was often too late to make offers to other candidates, so positions were left unfilled.
  • Hospitals imposed strict deadlines to reply. Students had to decide in a hurry, without knowing what other options would appear.
Link to Week 6 and earlier: this is an unstable market. Not enough offers can be made in time to find mutually beneficial matches, so the early-offer race (a prisoner's-dilemma-like situation) wrecks it.

11.2 The fix and Roth's discovery

In the early 1950s a centralised clearinghouse, the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), was introduced. In a 1984 paper, Alvin Roth studied its algorithm and found that it was closely related to Gale-Shapley. (The lecture summary adds that the clearinghouse algorithm predated the 1962 paper.) He hypothesised that the main reason for its success was that it produced stable matches.

Test of the hypothesis: the UK. In the early 1990s Roth studied similar medical markets in the UK. Different regions had adopted different algorithms: those that produced stable matches were successful, and those that did not broke down in various ways. (The summary names Edinburgh and Cardiff as stable and successful, Newcastle and Birmingham as unstable and abandoned; the slides do not name regions.) This is evidence that stability is what keeps a clearinghouse alive: if it is not stable, people (hospitals and doctors) have a reason to leave it.

11.3 New problems and the 1995 to 1997 redesign

  • Couples. More women were in medical school, so more dual-doctor couples wanted internships in the same region. The old NRMP could not take such joint requests, so many applicants stopped using it: a sign it was no longer stable.
  • Who proposed. In the NRMP the hospitals offered positions to students. By section 9, the proposing side is systematically favoured, so hospitals were favoured over students. Critics complained.
  • Manipulation. Applicants could gain by turning down offers they actually prefer and keeping worse ones. Roth showed in several theoretical papers that in some algorithms misrepresenting your preferences can pay for the receiving side.
The redesign. In 1995, Roth was asked to help; with Elliott Peranson he built an algorithm in which applicants propose, and which is designed to handle couples. It was adopted by the NRMP in 1997 and worked well. It was designed to be immune to student misrepresentation; computer simulations showed that in practice it is not sensitive to strategic manipulation by hospitals either.
Exam link: "Why did the NRMP switch to applicant-proposing?" Because (1) the proposing side gets its best stable matching, so students should propose; (2) the proposing side cannot gain by lying, so student reports can be trusted; (3) couples had to be allowed, so the algorithm was extended.

12. Schools, organs, and prices

12.1 Markets without prices

Traditional economics studies markets where prices adjust until supply equals demand. But many schools and universities are not allowed to charge fees, and for human organs payments are ruled out on ethical grounds. Yet an allocation must still be made. How do such processes work, and when is the outcome efficient? Matching theory is the tool.

12.2 New York high schools (2003)

Before 2003. Applicants to government high schools were asked to rank their five favourite choices. The lists were sent to schools, which admitted, rejected or waitlisted. This repeated for two more rounds, and students still unassigned after round three were placed by an administrative process.
The failures. Students did not get enough chances to list preferences, and schools did not have enough chances to make offers. As a result about 30,000 students a year ended up at schools they had not listed. It also encouraged lying: schools were more likely to admit students who ranked them first, so a student unlikely to get his dream school did better listing a more realistic school first. Students who simply told the truth suffered unnecessarily poor outcomes.
The 2003 redesign. Roth and colleagues replaced it with an applicant-proposing version of Gale-Shapley. Result: a 90% reduction in the number of students assigned to a school for which they had expressed no preference, and school-choice mechanisms of this kind spread widely. (The summary adds that this removes the incentive to hide your true favourite, because the proposing side can list its true preferences safely, which matches section 9.4.)

12.3 Can matching include prices? Auctions

  • One feature of all the examples above is that prices are not part of the process. That does not limit the theory.
  • Shapley and others extended the model so that prices (for example salaries in the doctors' market) are part of the offers. Algorithms with prices work in much the same way and give stable matches with broadly similar features.
  • Matching with prices is closely related to auctions, where objects are matched with buyers and prices are decisive. Research linking the two has produced results useful in practice, for instance for internet search engines that auction ad space to advertisers. Such firms have used economists as experts in designing new auctions.
Link to Week 6. Auctions (Week 6A) and cooperative games (Week 6B) are the "with prices" cousins of this week's "without prices" matching.

13. Practice questions (try first, then open the answer)

Q1. In the first market (Al, Bob, Cal, Dan), is Ann–Al, Beth–Bob, Cher–Cal, Dot–Dan stable?
AnswerNo. Al is with Ann, his 3rd choice, and prefers Dot (his 2nd). Dot is with Dan, her last choice, and prefers Al (her 2nd). So (Al, Dot) is a blocking pair and the matching is unstable.
Q2. Run men-proposing Gale-Shapley on Example 4 (A: b ≻ c ≻ a; B: c ≻ a ≻ b; C: a ≻ b ≻ c; a: A ≻ B ≻ C; b: B ≻ C ≻ A; c: C ≻ A ≻ B).
AnswerStage 1: a→A, b→B, c→C. Each woman has one proposer, so nobody is rejected. STOP: A–a, B–b, C–c (men get their first choices, 1 stage, 3 proposals). Women-proposing instead gives A–b, B–c, C–a.
Q3. What is an upper bound on the number of proposals with n men and n women? Why?
Answern². Each man proposes to each woman at most once (after a rejection he moves to the next name), and there are n men and n women. Every non-final stage has at least one new proposal, so the algorithm stops.
Q4. Define a blocking pair and say what it means for a matching to be stable.
AnswerA man and woman who are not matched to each other but each prefer the other to their own partner. A matching is stable if it has no blocking pair.
Q5. Why does the roommate problem with 4 people (a: b,c,d; b: c,a,d; c: a,b,d; d: a,b,c) have no stable matching?
AnswerThere are only 3 ways to pair 4 people. (a,b)(c,d) is broken by b and c; (a,c)(b,d) by a and b; (a,d)(b,c) by a and c. All three are unstable. The cycle a→b→c→a in the preferences is the cause.
Q6. In the 4×4 market of section 9, which stable matching is best for the women, and how do the men rank their partners in it?
AnswerSystem 2 (women propose): A–d, B–b, C–c, D–a. The women's ranks are 1, 2, 2, 1; the men (a, b, c, d) get ranks 3, 2, 3, 4, their worst of the three stable matchings.
Q7. Why is "men propose" always the worst stable matching for women?
AnswerMen-proposing gives every man his best stable partner (no man is rejected by anyone possible for him). If some stable matching gave a woman w a worse man than her man m, then m would prefer w (his best stable partner) to his partner there, and w prefers m, so (m, w) would block that matching. Contradiction.
Q8. A woman is unhappy with her partner in the men-proposing result. Can she always do better by lying?
AnswerNot always, but sometimes. In Example 4 woman A reports only "b" and gets her first choice b instead of her last choice a. The men, proposing, cannot gain by lying.
Q9. Run the college algorithm: X has quota 1 ranking s1 ≻ s2; Y has quota 1 ranking s2 ≻ s1; s1: X ≻ Y; s2: X ≻ Y.
AnswerStage 1: both apply to X. X keeps s1 (its favourite), rejects s2. Stage 2: s2 applies to Y; Y holds s2. STOP. X–s1, Y–s2. Stable: s2 prefers X, but X prefers s1.
Q10. Give three reasons the NRMP moved to applicant-proposing.
AnswerThe proposing side gets its best stable matching (so students rather than hospitals); the proposing side cannot gain from lying; the redesign could be extended to handle couples.

One-page recap

  • Stable matching: no man-woman pair, not matched together, prefer each other to their partners. Many stable matchings can exist.
  • Roommate problem: a stable matching may not exist (the 4-person cycle).
  • Gale-Shapley: propose, keep the best, reject the rest, repeat. Stops in at most n² proposals and always ends in a stable matching (so one always exists for two-sided markets).
  • Generalisations: unequal sizes, unacceptable partners (individual rationality), and ties (break them arbitrarily).
  • Optimality: the proposing side gets its best stable matching and the other side its worst. No stable matching is best for everyone.
  • Real markets: college admissions (quotas, many-to-one), NRMP (stability keeps a clearinghouse alive; applicant-proposing since 1997), NYC schools (2003, 90% fewer unlisted placements), and prices and auctions.
