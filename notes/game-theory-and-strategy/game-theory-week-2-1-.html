Maths you need this week
Expected value: multiply each possible payoff by its probability and add. Example: 50% chance of 10 and 50% of 0 gives 0.5(10) + 0.5(0) = 5.
Derivative to find a maximum: for a profit that looks like (something)·q − b·q², the top is where the slope is zero. The slope of q² is 2q, the slope of a plain k·q is k. Set slope = 0, solve for q. The second derivative being negative confirms it is a peak and not a valley.
Averages: a number picked uniformly between 0 and y has average y/2.

1. Where we are

Week 1 gave us the language: players, actions, payoffs, and the assumptions of rationality and common knowledge. Week 2 asks: given a game, what will actually be played? A rule that picks the outcome is called a solution concept. We build them from weakest-assumption to strongest-insight:

Action vs strategy. In a table game (one simultaneous move) an action and a strategy mean the same thing. In a tree game a strategy is a full plan: what you would do at every point where you might have to move. This week we only use table games, so we use the words interchangeably.

2. Dominant strategy equilibrium

Strict dominance. Action ai strictly dominates a′i if it pays more whatever the others do: ui(ai, a−i) > ui(a′i, a−i) for every a−i Weak dominance: ≥ for every a−i, and strictly > for at least one a−i.
A dominant action dominates every other action of that player.
Dominant strategy equilibrium (DSE). A profile a* = (a*1, ..., a*n) where every player's action is dominant: ui(a*i, a−i) ≥ ui(a′i, a−i) for all i, all a′i, all a−i (strict inequality for a strictly dominant equilibrium, and the weak version gives a weakly dominant one.)

Why it is "good news": a player does not need to guess what others will do. Her best action is the same in every case. The Prisoner's Dilemma is the standard example: Confess is dominant for both, so (C, C) is the DSE.

General Prisoner's Dilemma. Row player's payoffs: (C,C) = a, (C,D) = c, (D,C) = b, (D,D) = d (the column player's are the mirror image). C strictly dominates D when a > b (against C) and c > d (against D). It is a social dilemma when d > a: both prefer mutual D to mutual C. Check with a = −5, b = −20, c = 0, d = −1: all three hold.

Trap. Most games have no dominant strategies at all. That is why we need weaker tools below.

3. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS)

Idea. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated action. If I know you are rational, I can delete your dominated actions from the game too. That makes a smaller game with new dominated actions. Repeat. This uses common knowledge of rationality: "you are rational, you know I am rational, ..." as deep as needed.

Example 1 (3×3)

P1 \ P2LCR
U0, 01, −11, −1
M−1, 10, 0−1, 1
D−1, 12, −10, 0
Round 1. P1: compare M with U. Against L: −1 < 0. Against C: 0 < 1. Against R: −1 < 1. So U strictly dominates M. Delete M.
Round 2. P2 with rows U and D left. Use the second number in each cell, since those are P2's payoffs. Row U: L = 0, C = −1. Row D: L = 1, C = −1. So L ≥ C everywhere and L > C against U (0 > −1) and against D (1 > −1). C is strictly dominated by L. Delete C.
Round 3. Remaining: U row (L: 0,0 and R: 1,−1), D row (L: −1,1 and R: 0,0). P1: U beats D against L (0 > −1) and against R (1 > 0). Delete D.
Round 4. Only row U: P2 compares L (0) with R (−1). Delete R.
Result: (U, L) with payoffs (0, 0). One cell is left, so the game is dominance solvable.

Example 2 (2×3)

P1 \ P2LMR
U1, 01, 20, 1
D0, 30, 12, 0
P1 has no dominated action at first (U wins vs L and M, D wins vs R). P2: compare M with R. Against U: 2 > 1. Against D: 1 > 0. So M strictly dominates R: delete R. Now P1: U gives 1 and 1, D gives 0 and 0, so U strictly dominates D: delete D. P2 facing U: L = 0, M = 2, so M. Result: (U, M) = (1, 2).

The "guess 2/3 of the average" game

Everyone picks a whole number from 1 to 100. The winner is whoever is closest to 2/3 of the average of all guesses.

Derivation of the unravelling
Step 1. The average can never exceed 100, so the target 2/3 × average is at most 2/3 × 100 = 66.67. Any guess above 67 is further from every possible target than 67 is. Delete 68-100.
Step 2. Now everyone is at most 67, so the average is at most 67 and the target at most 2/3 × 67 = 44.67. Delete 46-67 (keep up to 45).
Step 3. Max 45, target at most 30. Then 20, 14, 9, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1. In general after k rounds the upper limit is 100·(2/3)k, which shrinks toward 1.
Result: the only survivor is everyone guesses 1.
Real life. Try it with friends. People rarely go the full way down, because it needs them to trust that others will do all the rounds (this is exactly common knowledge of rationality). Your winning guess is usually well above 1.

Path independence. For strict dominance, the order in which you delete does not matter. You always end with the same reduced game.

4. Iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies (IEWDS)

Same idea, but deleting weakly dominated actions (never worse, sometimes better). It removes more, but it is less safe.

Example: two stores choosing High, Low or Match price

A \ BHighLowMatch
High10, 102, 1510, 10
Low15, 25, 55, 5
Match10, 105, 510, 10
Round 1. For A compare Match with High: vs High 10 = 10, vs Low 5 > 2, vs Match 10 = 10. Never worse, once better, so Match weakly dominates High. Same for B. Delete High for both.
Round 2. Left with Low and Match. Compare Match with Low for A: vs Low 5 = 5, vs Match 10 > 5. Match weakly dominates Low. Delete Low for both.
Result: (Match, Match) = (10, 10).
Path dependence. Take this table (my reading of the slide):
P1 \ P2LR
T1, 10, 0
M1, 12, 1
B0, 02, 1
Order 1: delete B (M is never worse: 1 > 0 and 2 = 2). Then L weakly beats R for P2 (1 > 0, 1 = 1), delete R. Left: T and M against L, both pay (1, 1).
Order 2: delete T (M is never worse: 1 = 1, 2 > 0). Then R weakly beats L for P2 (1 = 1, 1 > 0), delete L. Left: M and B against R, both pay (2, 1).
Different order, different answer. So IEWDS is path-dependent and IESDS is not.
Dominance solvable. A game where repeated deletion (strict or weak) leaves exactly one action per player.

5. Nash equilibrium

Most games are not dominance solvable. Nash equilibrium needs far less: only that each player is doing the best she can given what the others do.

Best response. Player i's best response to a−i is the set of actions that maximise her payoff against it: BRi(a−i) = argmaxai ∈ Ai ui(ai, a−i) It can contain more than one action (ties), which is why it is called a correspondence.
Nash equilibrium (NE). A profile a* = (a*1, ..., a*n) such that for every player i and every action ai: ui(a*i, a*−i) ≥ ui(ai, a*−i) Same thing in best-response language: a*i ∈ BRi(a*−i) for all i. In words: nobody gains by changing only her own action.

Two ways to find a NE

  1. Test a candidate. Pick a cell, and for each player check every unilateral switch. If nobody gains, it is a NE.
  2. Best-response intersection. For each column of the table, mark (underline) P1's best payoff in that column. For each row, mark P2's best payoff in that row. Cells where both numbers are marked are the NE. (For continuous games, draw both reaction curves and find where they cross, as in Cournot below.)
Easy walkthrough of method 2 (Prisoner's Dilemma). Payoffs: (C,C) = −5,−5; (C,D) = 0,−20; (D,C) = −20,0; (D,D) = −1,−1. Column C: P1 gets −5 (play C) or −20 (play D), so mark −5. Column D: P1 gets 0 or −1, mark 0. Row C: P2 gets −5 or −20, mark −5. Row D: P2 gets 0 or −1, mark 0. The only cell where both numbers are marked is (C, C). That is the NE.
How the two methods fit with Week 1: every dominant strategy equilibrium is a NE (a dominant action is a best response to everything). Every NE survives IESDS (a strictly dominated action is never a best response). But a NE need not be dominant, and a NE can be bad for everyone (Prisoner's Dilemma).

6. Classic 2×2 games

These use the payoffs written on the lecture slides.

Battle of the Sexes: coordinate, but disagree on where

A girl (row) and a boy (column) each choose Movie (M) or Cricket (C). Both want to go to the same place, but they prefer different ones.

Girl \ BoyMC
M1, 20, 0
C0, 02, 1

At (M, M): the girl switching to C gets 0 < 1, the boy switching to C gets 0 < 2. Nobody gains, so it is a NE. At (C, C): same check (0 < 2 and 0 < 1), also a NE. At (M, C) both get 0 and either one gains by switching, so it is not. Two pure NE, each better for a different person. Theory cannot say which one will occur.

Stag Hunt: trust vs safety (Rousseau's social contract)

Two hunters each choose Stag (S) or Rabbit (R). Stag needs both of them; a rabbit can be caught alone.

1 \ 2SR
S8, 80, 4
R4, 02, 2

At (S, S): switching to R gives 4 < 8, so no gain. At (R, R): switching to S gives 0 < 2, no gain. Both are NE. (S, S) is better for both (payoff-dominant). (R, R) is the safe outcome: R guarantees at least 2, while S risks 0.
How much trust is needed? Let q = chance the other plays S. S pays 8q. R pays 4q + 2(1 − q) = 2 + 2q. They are equal when 8q = 2 + 2q, so q = 1/3. If you think the other plays S with more than 1/3 chance, go for Stag. The game is a model of cooperation that needs mutual trust.

Hawk-Dove (Chicken): two cars meet on a narrow road

The slide shows the story (two cars facing each other on a narrow road) and an empty table, so these payoff numbers are my own. Any numbers with the same ordering give the same result.

P1 \ P2HawkDove
Hawk0, 03, 1

At (H, D): P1 switching to D gets 2 < 3, P2 switching to H gets 0 < 1. NE. (D, H) is also NE. (D, D) is not (P1 deviates to H and gets 3 > 2). (H, H) is not (deviating to D gives 1 > 0). Two asymmetric pure NE, where one player is aggressive and the other yields.

Common thread: Battle of the Sexes, Stag Hunt and Hawk-Dove each have more than one pure NE. Nash tells you which outcomes are stable, not which one will happen. That motivates the next section.

7. How should we read a Nash equilibrium?

Two big readings (Osborne and Rubinstein):

  • Evolutive: people play the game again and again, learn from others, and drift to an equilibrium. (Like a norm at a college event about whether people stand or sit.)
  • Educative (deductive): perfectly rational players reason their way there from common knowledge, in one shot.

Dutta's "Nash parables" are five stories for why a NE might actually be played:

ParableMeaning
Play prescriptionA convention or rule tells everyone what to do (drive on the left). If it is a NE, nobody wants to break it.
Pre-play communicationPlayers talk first. Talk is not binding ("cheap talk"), but it sticks only if the agreed profile is a NE.
Rational introspectionEach player reasons it out alone from the game and common knowledge.
Focal point (Schelling)Among many NE, one stands out as obvious (a 50-50 split), and everyone expects everyone to pick it.
Trial and errorRepeated play; players adjust until nobody wants to change.

8. Application: Braess's paradox as a game

4000 drivers go from A to B. Roads A→C and D→B take x/100 minutes, where x is the number of cars on that road. Roads C→B and A→D always take 45 min. Players: the 4000 drivers. Strategies: ACB or ADB. Payoff: minus the travel time.

Before the bridge: finding the NE
Let n drivers take ACB, so 4000 − n take ADB.T(ACB) = n/100 + 45    T(ADB) = 45 + (4000 − n)/100 In a NE nobody gains by switching, so the two times must be equal:n/100 = (4000 − n)/100  ⟹  n = 4000 − n  ⟹  n = 2000 Travel time = 2000/100 + 45 = 65 min.
Check: one driver switches from ADB to ACB. Now ACB has 2001 cars: 2001/100 + 45 = 65.01 > 65. He loses. So no one wants to move. It is a NE.
After adding the 0-minute bridge C→D: new route ACDBT(ACDB) = xAC/100 + 0 + xDB/100 Test one driver deviating from the old equilibrium. A driver from ACB moves to ACDB. Cars on A→C stay 2000 (he leaves and rejoins it). Cars on D→B go from 2000 to 2001. T = 20 + 0 + 20.01 = 40.01 min, much less than 65. Everyone wants to switch.
Why ACDB wins whatever others do. Compare with ACB = xAC/100 + 45. The xAC terms are the same, so ACDB is better when xDB/100 < 45. Since xDB ≤ 4000, xDB/100 ≤ 40 < 45, always true. Compare with ADB = 45 + xDB/100: better when xAC/100 < 45, also always true. So ACDB strictly dominates both old routes.
Result. All 4000 take ACDB:T = 4000/100 + 0 + 4000/100 = 40 + 40 = 80 min
The paradox. A free shortcut moved everyone from 65 to 80 minutes. Each driver is rational, but together they overload both congested roads. Real cases: Stuttgart (1969), where traffic got worse until a newly built road was closed; New York (1990), where closing 42nd Street on Earth Day improved flow; Seoul (2002), where a 6-lane road was torn down to speed traffic. The same logic applies to electricity and internet networks.

9. Application: Hotelling's location model

Setup. A 1 km beach, consumers spread evenly along it, each buys exactly one ice cream (price INR 10, every 100 m of walking costs INR 1, so each customer simply goes to the nearer seller). Two sellers A and B pick positions xA, xB from {0, 0.1, 0.2, ..., 1}. Each wants the biggest market share.

Deriving market share. Say xA < xB. A customer at position t is equally far from both when t − xA = xB − t, i.e. at the midpoint t* = (xA + xB) / 2 Everyone left of t* goes to A, everyone right goes to B. So share of A = t* and share of B = 1 − t* (if they are at the same spot, 50-50).

Best responses, case by case

B at 0: A at 0.1 gives t* = 0.05, so A's share = 1 − 0.05 = 95%. A at 0.2 gives t* = 0.1, A's share = 90%. Closer is better, so BRA(0) = 0.1.
B at 0.6: A at 0.5 gives t* = 0.55, so A gets 55%. A at 0.7 gives t* = 0.65, and A gets only 35%. A at 0.6 gives 50%. So BRA(0.6) = 0.5: sit just on the longer side (the left) and next to B.
B at 0.4: by mirror image, BRA(0.4) = 0.5, with 55%.
B at 0.5: A at 0.4 gives t* = 0.45, 45%. A at 0.6 gives 1 − 0.55 = 45%. A at 0.5 gives 50%. So BRA(0.5) = 0.5.
If B is atleft of centre (< 0.5)0.5right of centre (> 0.5)
A's best responseone step to B's right (xB + 0.1)0.5one step to B's left (xB − 0.1)

The seller always squeezes in next to the rival, on the side with more customers. By symmetry the same holds for B. The two best-response rules cross at only one cell:

Unique NE: xA = xB = 0.5. Check: if A is at 0.5, B's best reply is 0.5 and vice versa. Both sell in the middle and split the market 50-50.

Why "ε" in some notes? On a continuous beach there is no "next point", so one writes xB − ε for "just beside B". The grid version avoids that. Other uses: the same logic explains why two political parties move toward the centre, and why rival shops cluster on the same street.

10. Application: Cournot competition

Two firms sell the same product. Each chooses a quantity qi at the same time. The market price depends on the total.

  • Inverse demand: P = a − b(q1 + q2) (more total output, lower price; a, b > 0)
  • Cost: ci qi (constant cost ci per unit)
Step 1: profit. Revenue minus cost:πi = P·qi − ciqi = [a − ci − b(qi + qj)] qi Step 2: best response. Treat qj as fixed and maximise over qi. Differentiate:∂πi/∂qi = a − ci − 2b qi − b qj = 0 (The 2b comes from differentiating the qi² term: −b qi² gives −2b qi.) The second derivative is −2b < 0, so this is a maximum. Solve for qi:qi = (a − ci)/(2b) − qj/2    (reaction function) If the rival makes more, you make less.
Step 3: equilibrium. A NE is where both reaction functions hold at once. With equal costs c1 = c2 = c, by symmetry q1 = q2 = q*:q* = (a − c)/(2b) − q*/2  ⟹  (3/2)q* = (a − c)/(2b)  ⟹  q* = (a − c)/(3b) Step 4: market outcome.Q* = 2q* = 2(a − c)/(3b)    P* = a − bQ* = a − 2(a − c)/3 = (a + 2c)/3 Profit of each firm = (P* − c)q* = [(a − c)/3]·[(a − c)/(3b)] = (a − c)²/(9b).
Unequal costs (bonus). Put q2 = (a − c2)/(2b) − q1/2 into firm 1's reaction function:
q1 = (a − c1)/(2b) − (a − c2)/(4b) + q1/4. Move q1/4 left: (3/4)q1 = [2(a − c1) − (a − c2)]/(4b). Soq1* = (a − 2c1 + c2)/(3b),   q2* = (a − 2c2 + c1)/(3b) The lower-cost firm produces more. Setting c1 = c2 = c gives back (a − c)/(3b).
Numbers: a = 100, b = 1, c = 10. Reaction: q1 = 45 − q2/2. Equilibrium q* = 90/3 = 30 each, Q = 60, P = 100 − 60 = 40, profit = (40 − 10)(30) = 900 each. A single monopolist would produce (a − c)/(2b) = 45 at price 55 and earn 45 × 45 = 2025, more than the duopoly's combined 1800. Competition lowers price and joint profit.

Where the two reaction lines cross is the Nash equilibrium (drawn for a = 100, b = 1, c = 10).
