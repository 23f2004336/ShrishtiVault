Part A: Bayesian games (games with incomplete information)

1. Complete vs incomplete information

All games before this week were complete information: everyone knows the players, the actions, and everybody's payoffs. Real life is not like that. A seller does not know how much a buyer is really willing to pay. A firm does not know a rival's costs. A boy does not know whether the girl he likes wants to meet him or avoid him.

Complete information: all payoffs are common knowledge.
Incomplete information: at least one player does not know something about another player's payoffs (or about the game itself).
Asymmetric information: one side knows more. The better-informed (BI) player knows the true situation. The less-informed (LI) player only has a guess (a probability).

What can the players do about it?

"Why not simply ask?" Because talk is cheap. If a high-value buyer could get a discount by saying "I value this at only 500", he would say it. A claim is believable only if lying is costly or unprofitable. That is why economists look for actions (signals) rather than words.

Week 6 Part 1 builds the tool for solving such games: the Bayesian game and its equilibrium.

2. Harsanyi's trick: turn incomplete into imperfect information

John Harsanyi's idea: pretend a "player" called Nature moves first and secretly draws each player's type (the private information, like "Meet type" or "Avoid type") from a known probability distribution. Then each player sees only her own type.

Type: a complete description of a player's private information (her payoffs). Common prior: everybody knows the probabilities with which Nature draws the types. Bayesian game: players, types, a prior over types, actions, and payoffs that depend on actions and types.

After Harsanyi's trick, "incomplete information" becomes a game where Nature moves first and the Boy cannot see Nature's move.

Why this works. Before, the Boy was "unsure which game he is in". Now the Boy is in one game with a first move by Nature that he cannot observe. That is an ordinary game with imperfect information, which we already know how to handle (information sets, from the extensive-form weeks). The common prior is what lets him compute expected payoffs.

3. Bayes' theorem (needed for updating beliefs)

When a player sees something, he should update his belief about the other player's type. The tool for updating is Bayes' theorem. Here is where the formula comes from.

Derivation.
1. The probability of "A and B" can be written two ways: P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B) and also P(A∩B) = P(B|A)·P(A). ("P(A|B)" means "probability of A given that B happened".)
2. Set them equal: P(B|A)·P(A) = P(A|B)·P(B).
3. Divide by P(A): P(B|A) = P(A|B)·P(B) / P(A) 4. The event A can happen in two ways: with B or with "not B" (written Bᶜ). So P(A) = P(A|B)P(B) + P(A|Bᶜ)P(Bᶜ) (law of total probability).
5. Put step 4 in step 3: P(B|A) = P(A|B)·P(B) / [ P(A|B)·P(B) + P(A|Bᶜ)·P(Bᶜ) ]
Tiny example. A girl is Meet type with prior ½ and Avoid type with prior ½. Suppose a Meet type plays B with probability 1 and an Avoid type plays B with probability 0. You see her play B. Then P(Meet | B) = (1·½) / (1·½ + 0·½) = 1 Her action revealed her type completely. If instead both types played B with probability ⅔, then P(Meet|B) = (⅔·½)/(⅔·½+⅔·½) = ½: nothing is learned. That is exactly signal jamming (mixing the same way for every type so the action carries no information).
Where this matters this week. In the Bayesian Battle of the Sexes below, the Boy moves simultaneously, so he cannot observe the Girl's action before choosing. He just uses the prior ½-½. Bayes' theorem becomes essential in sequential incomplete-information games (signalling games), which is why the lecture reminds you of it.

4.4 Solving it: best responses

Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE): a strategy for every player and every type such that nobody wants to change. The Boy maximises his expected payoff (over the Girl's types). Each type of the Girl maximises her payoff given that she knows her type. (Maximising the ex-ante payoff and maximising type by type give the same answer, as long as every type has positive probability.)
Boy's best responses (look down each column, pick the larger first number):
vs B1B2: B (2) beats S (0). vs B1S2: B (1) beats S (½). vs S1B2: B (1) beats S (½). vs S1S2: S (1) beats B (0).
Girl's best responses (look along each row, pick the larger second number):
vs B: B1B2 gives ½, B1S2 gives 3/2, S1B2 gives 0, S1S2 gives 1. Best = B1S2.
vs S: B1B2 ½, B1S2 0, S1B2 3/2, S1S2 1. Best = S1B2.
Finding the equilibrium. Look for a cell where both are best responses. If the Boy plays B, the Girl's best reply is B1S2; and the Boy's best reply to B1S2 is B. So (B, B1S2) is a BNE. If the Boy plays S, the Girl replies S1B2, but the Boy's best reply to S1B2 is B, not S, so it is not an equilibrium.
Intuition for the answer. The Boy plays B (his favourite). The Meet girl goes to B (she wants to meet him). The Avoid girl goes to S (she wants to avoid him). Both types do the sensible thing, the Boy bets on his favourite, and he gets 1 on average, the Girl 3/2 on average. This is the unique pure-strategy BNE.

The recipe for every Bayesian game.

5. Mixed-strategy BNE (full derivation)

Now allow randomising. Let

  • x = probability the Boy plays B (so S with 1 − x),
  • y = probability the Meet type plays B,
  • z = probability the Avoid type plays B.

Rule: a player mixes strictly between two actions only if the two actions give the same expected payoff (indifference).

Step 1: the Meet type. Game 1 payoffs for the Girl: if she plays B she gets 1 when the Boy plays B and 0 when he plays S; if she plays S she gets 0 when the Boy plays B and 2 when he plays S. The Boy plays B with probability x.
Girl plays B: x·1 + (1−x)·0 = x. Girl plays S: x·0 + (1−x)·2 = 2 − 2x.
Indifferent when x = 2 − 2x, so 3x = 2, x = 2/3. If x > 2/3, B is better (she plays B for sure). If x < 2/3, S is better.
Step 2: the Avoid type. Game 2 Girl payoffs: (Boy B, Girl B) 0, (Boy S, Girl B) 1, (Boy B, Girl S) 2, (Boy S, Girl S) 0.
Girl plays B: x·0 + (1−x)·1 = 1 − x. Girl plays S: x·2 + (1−x)·0 = 2x.
Indifferent when 1 − x = 2x, so x = 1/3. If x > 1/3, S is better. If x < 1/3, B is better.
Step 3: the Boy. Boy payoffs are the same in both games (2 for BB, 0 for BS, 0 for SB, 1 for SS):
Boy plays B: ½[y·2 + (1−y)·0] + ½[z·2 + (1−z)·0] = y + z.
Boy plays S: ½[y·0 + (1−y)·1] + ½[z·0 + (1−z)·1] = 1 − (y+z)/2.
Indifferent when y + z = 1 − (y+z)/2, so (3/2)(y+z) = 1, y + z = 2/3.

The two types switch their behaviour at different x, which is what pins down the equilibria.

Putting the three conditions together

Because 1/3 ≠ 2/3, the Boy's x can make only one type indifferent. So there are two cases.

Flag. The slides and summary present only Case A and call it the unique mixed BNE. My check (done by script, all fractions exact) found Case B too. If an exam asks for "the" mixed BNE, give Case A (the lecture's answer), but you can mention that Case B also works. Why no other: if x is anything other than 1/3 or 2/3, both types play pure actions, so y and z are 0 or 1 and y + z can never equal 2/3. And x = 0 or x = 1 are the pure cases covered by the matrix (only (B, B1S2) survives).
Summary of all BNE of this game: (1) pure: Boy B, Girl (B if Meet, S if Avoid). (2) mixed Case A. (3) mixed Case B.

6. Auctions: a Bayesian game with continuous types

6.1 Setup: first-price sealed-bid auction

One item is sold. Each bidder privately writes a bid in a sealed envelope. The highest bid wins and the winner pays her own bid (that is "first-price"). Each bidder has a private value v for the item: this is her type. Nobody knows the others' values, only their distribution.

Assumptions: two bidders; each value is drawn independently and uniformly from [0, 1]. So the probability that a value is below t is F(t) = t (the CDF), the density is 1, and the average value is E[v] = ½.
Payoff of bidder i with value vi and bid bi:
  • wins: vi − bi (value minus what she pays)
  • tie: ½(vi − bi) (a coin toss decides)
  • loses: 0
The trade-off. Bid high: you win more often but your profit per win (v − b) is small. Bid low: bigger profit per win, but you may lose. Bidding your full value v gives profit 0 even if you win, so you would never do it. The equilibrium finds the best balance.

A sketch (not to scale): the expected profit is the product of the two lines, so it peaks in the middle. We find the peak with calculus.

6.2 Derivation of the equilibrium bid

Step 1: guess the rival's behaviour. Suppose bidder 2 bids a fixed fraction a of her value: b2 = a·v2, where a is a number between 0 and 1 (to be found).
Step 2: probability that bidder 1 wins with bid b1. She wins when b1 > a·v2, i.e. v2 < b1/a. Since v₂ is uniform on [0,1]: P(win) = P(v2 < b1/a) = b1/a (valid when b₁ ≤ a; nobody bids above a, the rival's highest bid).
Step 3: expected payoff. (The tie has probability 0 for continuous values, so ignore it.) U1(b1) = (v1 − b1) · (b1/a) Step 4: maximise. Write U₁ = (v₁b₁ − b₁²)/a. Differentiate with respect to b₁ and set to 0: dU1/db1 = (v1 − 2b1)/a = 0 ⟹ b1 = v1/2 The second derivative is −2/a < 0, so this is a maximum.
Step 5: make the guess consistent. Bidder 1's best reply is v₁/2, whatever a is. In a symmetric equilibrium bidder 1 uses the same rule as bidder 2, so a = ½. Check that b₁ = v₁/2 ≤ a = ½ always, since v₁ ≤ 1. ✔
Result (2 bidders): b(v) = v/2. Each bidder shades her bid to half her value.
Example: value 0.8 gives bid 0.4. If the rival has value 0.6 (bid 0.3), I win and my profit is 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4.

6.3 Many bidders (n of them)

Derivation. Now n bidders; each of the other n − 1 bids a·v. Bidder 1 wins only if all the others bid less than b₁, each with probability b₁/a, independently: P(win) = (b1/a)n−1 U1 = (v1 − b1)(b1/a)n−1 Differentiate with the product rule (derivative of (v−b) is −1): dU/db = −(b/a)n−1 + (v − b)(n−1) bn−2/an−1 = 0 Divide everything by bn−2/an−1 (both are positive): −b + (v − b)(n − 1) = 0 ⟹ (n−1)v = n·b ⟹ b = ((n−1)/n)·v

Meaning. More competition forces you to bid closer to your true value, which is good for the seller. (In the lecture's handwriting the same result appears as "n/(n+1)·v", where n counts the rivals. Same formula: with n rivals there are n+1 bidders.)

6.4 The seller's expected revenue (2 bidders)

Revenue = the winning bid = the larger of the two bids = ½ · (larger value). For two uniform values, the expected maximum is E[max(v1, v2)] = ∫01 x · 2x dx = 2/3 (the density of the maximum is 2x, because P(max ≤ x) = x²). So expected revenue = ½ · 2/3 = 1/3.

Part B: Cooperative games

7. From competition to cooperation

In non-cooperative games (Weeks 1 to 6A) the players act separately and we ask "what will each one choose?". In cooperative games, players can sign binding agreements and form groups (coalitions). The questions become: how much value can a group create, and how should it be shared?

Cooperative game = (N, v).
N = the set of players. A coalition S is any subset of N. N itself is the grand coalition.
v = the characteristic function: v(S) is the total value (money) that coalition S can create on its own. We take v(∅) = 0.

7.1 Running example: a seller and two buyers

Player 1 is a seller with one item that costs her 400. Player 2 is a buyer willing to pay 900. Player 3 is another buyer willing to pay 1100. Value created by a sale = (buyer's willingness to pay) − (seller's cost).
v({1}) = 0 (a seller alone has nothing to gain), v({2}) = v({3}) = 0, v({2,3}) = 0 (two buyers cannot trade without the seller),
v({1,2}) = 900 − 400 = 500, v({1,3}) = 1100 − 400 = 700,
v({1,2,3}) = 700 (the item can be sold only once, so the best use is to sell to buyer 3).

7.2 Allocation and its rules

Allocation: a vector x = (x₁, x₂, x₃) saying how much of the value each player gets.
Efficiency: the whole value is handed out: x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = v(N) = 700.
Individual rationality: nobody takes less than she could get alone: xi ≥ v({i}) = 0.
BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): what a player gets if the talks fail. A player will not accept less than her BATNA.

7.3 Marginal contribution and the "can't take more than you add" principle

Marginal contribution of player i: how much the pie shrinks if i leaves: MCi = v(N) − v(N \ {i}) Marginal Contribution Principle: nobody can sensibly claim more than she adds: xi ≤ MCi. (If she demanded more, the others would just do the deal without her.)
Compute for the example.
MC₁ (seller) = v(N) − v({2,3}) = 700 − 0 = 700 (without the seller there is no deal).
MC₂ (buyer 900) = v(N) − v({1,3}) = 700 − 700 = 0 (buyer 3 and the seller make the same 700 without buyer 2).
MC₃ (buyer 1100) = v(N) − v({1,2}) = 700 − 500 = 200.
So x₂ ≤ 0, and with x₂ ≥ 0 we get x₂ = 0. And x₃ ≤ 200, x₁ ≤ 700.
Efficiency: x₁ = 700 − x₃ (as x₂ = 0). So x₃ ∈ [0, 200] and x₁ ∈ [500, 700].
Plain-English reading. The lower-valuing buyer is just "competition": he gets nothing. The high-valuing buyer can keep at most 200, which is his advantage over buyer 2 (1100 − 900). The seller gets at least 500, because she could always sell to buyer 2 for 900 − 400 = 500. Where in these ranges they end up depends on bargaining power.

8. The core

The core is the set of allocations that no coalition wants to break away from. For every coalition S, the members' total must be at least what S could create alone: Σi∈S xi ≥ v(S) for every S, and Σi∈N xi = v(N)

8.1 Two equivalent ways to write the core

Derivation (the link to marginal contributions). Take any coalition S and let N\S be everybody else. Efficiency says the total is v(N), so Σi∈S xi = v(N) − Σj∈N\S xj The condition "outsiders N\S get at least v(N\S)" reads Σj∈N\S xj ≥ v(N\S). Putting it in: Σi∈S xi ≤ v(N) − v(N\S) =: MCS So "no coalition gets less than v(S)" is the same as "no coalition gets more than its marginal contribution MCS". They are two views of one statement (we use S and its complement N\S in turn). For a single player S = {i} this gives exactly x_i ≤ MC_i.

8.2 The core of the seller and two buyers

Coalition Sv(S)Condition Σx ≥ v(S)

{2,3}0x₂ + x₃ ≥ 0

Core = { (x₁, 0, x₃) : x₃ ∈ [0, 200], x₁ = 700 − x₃ }. It is exactly the set found from marginal contributions. (I checked this by script on a grid.)
Why the core is useful. It tells you which splits are stable. A split outside it, e.g. seller 400, buyer 3 gets 300, buyer 2 gets 0, fails: the seller and buyer 2 could break away, trade for 500, and share it so that both beat what they get now (the seller needs only to get more than 400). So that split is not stable.
Core can be empty, and can be large. This example gave a whole range. Other games have no allocation satisfying all the conditions at once. The core says what is stable, not what is fair (Shapley value, not in this week, is a rule for a single "fair" point).

Part C: Coopetition, "playing the right game"

9. Change the game, not just your move

Brandenburger and Nalebuff (the authors of Co-opetition) say: most people only analyse the game they are given. The best players ask a bigger question: "Is this the right game to be playing? Can I change it?"

Coopetition = cooperation + competition at the same time. Business is war and peace: you cooperate with others to create the pie (value) and compete to divide it.
Analyse the gameDesign a new game
Take players, actions, payoffs as fixedChange who plays, what they can do, how payoffs are decided
Find the equilibrium and play itReshape the game so the equilibrium is better for you

9.1 Burning the bridge (Sun Tzu)

The ancient general Sun Tzu advised putting your army in a position with no way back. Your own troops then fight harder, and the enemy knows it. Game theory reading: removing your own option can make you better off.

The game (slide payoffs, written (Country 1, Country 2)). Country 1 chooses Attack (A) or Don't Attack (DA). If it does not attack, payoffs are (5, 3). If it attacks, Country 2 chooses Fight (F) or Retreat (R): (A, F) gives (0, 1); (A, R) gives (10, 2).

Green = backward-induction path before burning the bridge.

Before burning (backward induction). At Country 2's node: Fight gives 1, Retreat gives 2, so Country 2 retreats. Country 1 expects this: Attack gives 10, Don't attack gives 5. It attacks. Outcome (A, R) = (10, 2).
After Country 2 burns its bridge (it removes the Retreat option): at its node only Fight remains, giving (0, 1). Country 1 now compares Attack (0) with Don't attack (5), and chooses DA. Outcome (5, 3).
Who gained? Country 2 improves from 2 to 3. Country 1 drops from 10 to 5. Country 2 gained by making its threat credible: an empty threat to fight is not believed, but a bridge that is gone cannot be crossed back.
Lesson. Having fewer options can be good if it is visible and irreversible. Where the bridge can be quietly rebuilt, burning it proves nothing.

9.2 The card-burning game (cooperative game with a twist)

Setup. A teacher holds 50 black cards, and 50 students each hold 1 red card. A black-red pair together is worth 1000 (slide: INR 1000; the written summary says $, the number is the same). Unpaired cards are worth 0. The group may form pairs and then decide how to split the money. How does the split come out?
Value of coalitions. v(all 51 players) = 50 pairs × 1000 = 50,000.
Marginal contribution of a student: without one student there are still 50 black cards but only 49 red cards, so 49 pairs: v = 49,000. MC_student = 50,000 − 49,000 = 1,000.
Marginal contribution of the teacher: without the teacher there are no black cards, so v = 0. MC_teacher = 50,000 − 0 = 50,000.
The "equal split" intuition: it looks like the teacher is crucial, so she could take much. With a symmetric 50-50 division of each pair's 1000, the teacher gets 25,000 and each student 500. (This is the slide's "25,000, 500 each".)

Now the teacher burns 5 of her own black cards

The teacher destroys 5 of her black cards, keeping 45. Now:
v(N) = 45 pairs = 45,000 (there are 45 black cards and 50 red, only 45 pairs possible).
Remove one student: 45 black, 49 red, still 45 pairs: v = 45,000.
MCstudent = 45,000 − 45,000 = 0 By the Marginal Contribution Principle, x_student ≤ 0, so each student gets 0. The teacher takes the full 45,000, which is more than the 25,000 she got before, even though the total value fell.
Why it happens. Scarcity gives power. After burning, black cards are scarce, and there are spare red cards. Any single student is replaceable (marginal contribution 0), so no student can demand anything. This is the same logic as buyer 2 getting 0 in the seller example.

The stated result "students get 0, teacher takes everything" follows from MC = 0 on its own. (In the exam, rely on the MC argument.)

B, L: 2. B, M: ½·0 + ½·3 = 3/2. B, R: ½·3 + ½·0 = 3/2.
LMR
T1, 2ε1, 3ε/21, 3ε/2

L dominates M and R for Player 2: against T, 2ε > 3ε/2 (true for ε > 0); against B, 2 > 3/2. So Player 2 plays L. Player 1's best reply to L is B (2 > 1). Unique NE: (B, L), payoffs (2, 2).
Case 2: Player 2 LEARNS which game it is (Player 1 still does not). Now P2's plan can depend on the game, so we check what is best for her in each game.
In Game 1, P2's payoffs are: against T (L, M, R) = (2ε, 0, 3ε); against B = (2, 0, 3). R is best against both (3ε > 2ε > 0 and 3 > 2 > 0). So in Game 1 she plays R.
In Game 2: against T = (2ε, 3ε, 0); against B = (2, 3, 0). M is best against both. So in Game 2 she plays M.
Her plan "R in Game 1, M in Game 2" is best whatever P1 does. Now P1 (who does not know the game) predicts it: B gives 0 (against R in Game 1 and against M in Game 2), while T gives 1. So P1 plays T.
Unique BNE: (T, (R in Game 1, M in Game 2)). Payoffs: P1 gets 1, P2 gets 3ε in either game.
Why does knowing more hurt? Once P2 is known to react to the true game, Player 1 can predict her moves and exploit them: P1 switches to the safe T. When P2 is "blind", L is her only sensible move, and P1 best-replies with B, which happens to give P2 the high 2. Being able to commit to not reacting is valuable. This links to burning the bridge: less flexibility can be a strength.
Note. The slide circles ε < ½. My working shows the conclusion needs only ε < 2/3 (so 3ε < 2), and ε < ½ certainly satisfies it. I could not tell what extra role ½ plays. The answer is the same for any ε in (0, ½).

10. The Value Net: who are the players?

To change a game, first map everyone who affects it. The Value Net places a firm among four groups.

Vertical axis = the value chain (supply to customers). Horizontal axis = substitutes versus complements.

Value chain: supplier → firm/business → customer. Money flows one way, products the other.
WTP (willingness to pay): the most a customer would pay. Opportunity cost (OC): the least a supplier would accept.
Competitor (substitutor) of A: a player whose product makes customers value yours less: customers' WTP for A falls when they have B.
Complementor of A: a player whose product makes customers value yours more: WTP(A and B) > WTP(A) + WTP(B) (peanut butter and jelly; a console and games).
On the supplier side, B is a complementor if OC(A and B) < OC(A) + OC(B): serving both together costs a supplier less than serving them separately (e.g. two firms that share the same supplier's delivery run).
"Friend or foe: BOTH." The same player can be a competitor and a complementor. Think of two airlines on a route: they compete for passengers but together they make the airport and route more valuable. Lesson: do not label players simply as enemies.

11. PARTS: the five levers to change a game

The levers spell PARTS. The slides use PAARTS: Players, Actions, Added value, Rules, Tactics, Scope. (Brandenburger and Nalebuff's original PARTS has "Added value" as the A. The lecture summary uses "Actions" as the A, and the burning bridge is its example. Learn both: they are two sides of the same idea.)

LetterLeverQuestion to ask
PPlayersWho else could be in the game? Who could be removed or added?
AActionsCan I expand or restrict the actions available? (burning bridges, long-term supply contracts)
AAdded valueWhat would the pie lose without me? (my marginal contribution)
RRulesWhat rules fix how the game is played? Can I change or add them?
TTacticsHow do players perceive the game? Can I shape their perception?
SScopeIs the game too narrow or broad? Can I link it to other games?

11.1 Players: NutraSweet

The story (as in the lecture). NutraSweet, the sweetener in diet drinks, was supplied by Monsanto, which held a monopoly (patent protected until about 1992). Diet Coke and Diet Pepsi both depended on it, so Monsanto kept the price high. Coke brought in a new supplier, HSC, as an alternative source. The handwritten slide shows Coke, Monsanto and HSC and a "n/2, n/2" split sketch, plus numbers 500 and 3500 and the years 1992 to 1998. I could not decode what 500 and 3500 measure, so I do not use them.
Lesson (change the players): adding a supplier changes the game from "one supplier sets the price" to "suppliers compete for Coke's business". Monsanto's bargaining power falls because it is now replaceable.
Think with marginal contribution. With no rival supplier, Monsanto's MC is the whole value of diet drinks, so it can charge almost anything. A second supplier cuts its MC to the cost saving over the rival. That shrinks its bargaining power. Players with bigger MC get larger shares.

11.2 Added value: Nintendo

The slide recalls market values (1990 to 91): Sony 2.2, Nissan 2.0 and Nintendo 2.4 trillion yen, so Nintendo, a game company, was worth more than Nissan. The lecture's point: you can't take away more than you add. Nintendo kept high margins on its 8-bit NES console by strictly limiting cartridge supply to retailers. Holding back supply is destroying a little value on purpose, which keeps retailers needing Nintendo and avoids price wars. This is the same logic as the teacher burning cards: scarcity raises your share.

11.3 Rules: simultaneous vs sequential (Cournot vs Stackelberg)

The lecture example: moving from Cournot to Stackelberg. Same two firms and same costs, but different rules of moving:
CournotStackelberg
TimingBoth choose quantity at the same timeLeader chooses first, follower sees it and then chooses
Who gainsNeither has an edgeThe leader gets a first-mover edge
Rule of thumb from the slides: "look forward, reason back". Whoever can set the rules of timing can change the outcome.

11.4 Tactics: NY Post vs Daily News

The NY Post made a targeted price cut in Staten Island only, not across the whole city. The slide shows prices of 25¢, 40¢ and 50¢ around the Daily News and Post; I could not read exactly which price belongs to which paper, so I do not give them. The idea: a small, local move sends the rival a signal (and a test) that a price war is possible, without starting one everywhere. Tactics include fog (hiding your intentions), signalling, and local tests.

11.5 Scope: Nintendo vs Sega

Nintendo stayed with its profitable 8-bit console (the slide circles "8 Bit" and crosses out "16 Bit") and left the 16-bit market to Sega, to protect its 8-bit business. Lesson: the boundaries of the game matter. Deciding which market you will and will not play in is a strategic choice. Widen or shrink the frame on purpose.

12. Practice questions (try first, then open the answer)

Q1. In the Bayesian BoS, the Boy's payoff table is as in section 4. Find the Boy's expected payoff from S if the Girl plays B1S2.
AnswerGame 1 (S vs B): Boy 0. Game 2 (S vs S): Boy 1. Expected = ½·0 + ½·1 = ½.
Q2. In the pure BNE, what does each type of Girl do, and what do the Boy and the Girl get?
AnswerBoy plays B. Meet type plays B, Avoid type plays S. Boy's expected payoff 1; Girl's ex-ante expected payoff 3/2 (Meet type gets 1, Avoid type gets 2).
Q3. Why does the Meet type mix only when x = 2/3?
AnswerHer payoffs are x from B and 2 − 2x from S. She is indifferent only if x = 2 − 2x, i.e. x = 2/3.
Q4. First-price auction, 3 bidders with values uniform on [0,1]. Your value is 0.9. What do you bid?
Answerb = ((n−1)/n)v = (2/3)(0.9) = 0.6.
Q5. Two bidders, uniform values. What is the seller's expected revenue?
AnswerExpected max value = 2/3, bids are half of values, so revenue = 1/3.
Q6. Seller cost 400, buyers 900 and 1100. Give the core.
Answerx₂ = 0, x₃ ∈ [0, 200], x₁ = 700 − x₃ ∈ [500, 700]. Prices: the item is sold to buyer 3 at a price between 900 and 1100, since the seller's gain is price − 400 ∈ [500, 700].
Q7. The teacher has 50 black cards and the students 50 red cards, a pair worth 1000. After the teacher burns 5 black cards, what is a student's marginal contribution and share?
Answerv(N) = 45,000. Without one student still 45 pairs: 45,000. MC = 0, so a student's share is 0.
Q8. In the burning-bridge game, why does Country 2 gain by burning the bridge?
AnswerWithout the bridge Country 2 can only Fight, so Attack gives Country 1 just 0 and it chooses DA. Country 2 gets 3 instead of 2.
Q9. Why can extra information hurt Player 2 in Example 3?
AnswerWhen informed, she plays the best reply to the real game (R or M), and Player 1 responds with T, leaving her 3ε < 2. When uninformed she plays L, Player 1 replies B and she gets 2.
Q10. Name the five PARTS levers and give one example.
AnswerPlayers (Coke adds HSC), Actions (burning a bridge), Added value (Nintendo limits cartridge supply), Rules (Cournot to Stackelberg), Tactics (NY Post in Staten Island), Scope (Nintendo leaves 16-bit to Sega).

One-page recap

  • Bayesian game: Nature picks types; a strategy is a plan per type; payoffs are averaged using the prior.
  • BNE: mutual best responses, type by type. For the BoS: pure (B, B1S2); mixed Case A (⅔, ⅔, 0) and Case B (⅓, 0, ⅔).
  • Auction: b = ((n−1)/n)v; 2 bidders b = v/2, revenue 1/3.
  • Cooperative game: (N, v); MCi = v(N) − v(N\{i}); the core = allocations where no group gets less than it can make alone ⟺ no group gets more than its MC.
  • Coopetition: change the game: Value Net (customers, suppliers, substitutors, complementors) and PARTS.
