1. Game trees: games that unfold over time

Until now every game was a table: everyone chose at once. Many real situations happen in order and later players see what happened. We draw those as a game tree (the extensive form).

Normal (table) formExtensive (tree) form
TimeIgnored; simultaneousMoves happen in sequence
DescribesPlayers, actions, payoff for each profilePlayers, who moves when, what they know, actions, payoffs

Parts of a tree

  • Initial node (root): where the game starts. Exactly one.
  • Decision node: a point where one named player picks an action.
  • Branch: one available action, drawn as a line out of a node.
  • Terminal node: the end. It carries a payoff vector (u₁, u₂, ...).
Three rules for a valid tree: (1) one initial node, (2) no cycles (you can never come back to an earlier node), (3) from the root there is exactly one path to every node.

Example: the war game

Country 1 decides whether to attack Country 2, which holds an island. If attacked, Country 2 sees the attack and either fights or retreats across a bridge. Each army prefers holding the island to not holding it. A fight is the worst outcome for both. The slides give words only, so the numbers below are mine; only their ordering matters.

Payoffs are (Country 1, Country 2). Country 1 likes: take the island (10) > status quo (0) > war (−10). Country 2 likes: keep the island (2) > retreat (−2) > war (−10).

Two kinds of uncertainty (from the slides)

  • Nature's move: chance decides something (a coin, a random type). Drawn as a node owned by "Nature".
  • Strategic uncertainty: you do not know what another player chose. Drawn with an information set (Section 7).

The Prisoner's Dilemma as a tree

Ranga (R) moves first, but Billa (B) cannot see it. The dashed red line joins Billa's two nodes: he cannot tell which one he is at. So "simultaneous" and "sequential" are really about what you know, not the clock.

2. Rules of the game, actions and strategies

What a tree must specify: who the players are, who moves when (the player function), what actions are feasible, how much each player knows when moving, and the payoffs.
Formal version: N (players); H (set of histories, i.e. sequences of actions so far, starting from the empty history, where every start of a history is also a history); Z (terminal histories, where play stops); P(h) = the player who moves after history h; ui(z) = payoff at each z in Z.
Action: one choice at one decision point. Strategy: a complete plan that names an action at every decision point of that player, including points that will never be reached.
Trap: "Play U, then a" is a strategy. "Play U" alone is not, if you also move later. In a table game, an action and a strategy are the same thing.
Counting strategies. If player i has decision points 1, ..., k and mj actions at point j, she has a choice at each point, independently, so |Si| = m1 × m2 × ... × mk Example: 3 decision points with 2, 3 and 2 actions give 2 × 3 × 2 = 12 strategies. The count grows fast, which is why we prefer trees to tables for sequential games.

3. Backward induction (rollback)

Forward reasoning ("what looks best right now?") goes wrong because it ignores how others will react. Backward reasoning starts at the end, where choices are easy, and works up.

Works for: finite games of perfect information (every information set has exactly one node). The result is the rollback equilibrium: a complete strategy profile.

War game by rollback

Step 1: Country 2's node: Fight −10 vs Retreat −2. Retreat is better, so Country 2 retreats. Roll up (10, −2).
Step 2: Country 1: Attack leads to (10, −2), so payoff 10. Not attack gives 0. 10 > 0, so Country 1 attacks.
Result: Attack, Retreat. Payoffs (10, −2). A "forward" thinker who feared war would wrongly stay home.

Burning the bridge: changing the game helps Country 2

Country 2 destroys the bridge, deleting Retreat. Rollback: at Country 2's node only Fight remains, giving (−10, −10). Country 1 compares Attack (−10) with Not attack (0) and stays home. Country 2 ends with 2 instead of −2.
Lesson: having fewer options can help if it makes your threat credible. Cutting off your own escape route is a commitment.

A three-stage example with the full derivation

Stage 3 (Player 1 at node after U, R): a gives 5, b gives 3. Choose a. Roll up (5, 3).
Stage 2 (Player 2): after U: L gives 4, R (now worth 3) gives 3. Choose L, roll up (2, 4). After D: L gives 1, R gives 5. Choose R, roll up (0, 5).
Stage 1 (Player 1): U leads to payoff 2, D leads to 0. Choose U.
Full equilibrium: Player 1 = (U, a) [U at the root, a if the other node is ever reached]. Player 2 = (L, R) [L after U, R after D]. s* = ( (U, a), (L, R) )    path: U → L    outcome (2, 4)
Common mistake: writing only the path (U, L). The actions "a" and "R after D" are never played, but they must be stated: they are exactly what makes Player 2 choose L (if he chose R after U, Player 1 would get 5 and he would get only 3).

Ties. If a mover is indifferent between two actions, each choice gives a different rolled-up tree, so there can be several rollback equilibria. Check each.

Slide example: Lucy and Charlie Brown (the football)

Lucy (L) moves first: Ask (A) Charlie Brown to kick the football, or Don't ask (DA). If she asks, Charlie Brown (CB) can Accept or Reject. If he accepts, Lucy decides at the last moment to Take Away the ball (TA) or Let Go (LG). (TA and LG are my reading of the slide's abbreviations.) Payoffs are (Lucy, CB).

Rollback. Last node (Lucy): TA gives her 4, LG gives 1. She takes the ball away. Roll up (4, 1).
Charlie Brown: Accept now means (4, 1), so he gets 1. Reject gives him 3. He rejects. Roll up (2, 3).
Lucy at the root: Ask leads to (2, 3), so she gets 2. Don't ask gives 3. She doesn't ask.
Result: Lucy plays (DA, TA), Charlie Brown plays Reject, outcome (3, 2). Charlie Brown does not trust her, and he is right.
Table form. Lucy has 2 decision points with 2 actions each, so 2 × 2 = 4 strategies. Charlie Brown has 1, so 2 strategies.
Lucy \ CBAcceptReject
(A, TA)4, 12, 3
(A, LG)1, 42, 3
(DA, TA)3, 23, 2
(DA, LG)3, 23, 2
Check (DA, LG) with Reject: Lucy switching to (A, ·) gets 2 < 3. Charlie Brown's choice does not matter (the game ends at DA), so no gain. ✔ NE. Same for (DA, TA) with Reject. The other cells fail: e.g. (DA, TA) with Accept, Lucy switches to (A, TA) and gets 4 > 3.
Two Nash equilibria, one subgame perfect. Both have the same outcome (3, 2). But (DA, LG) says "if we ever get to the last node, Lucy lets go", and at that node LG pays 1 against TA's 4. That plan is not credible. Subgames: the whole game, the game after A, and Lucy's last node (3 in all). Only (DA, TA) with Reject is a NE in all three, so it is the SPE.

4. From tree to table, and non-credible threats

Converting a tree to a table: (1) list each player's complete strategies; (2) make rows and columns from them; (3) for each pair, follow the tree to the end and write its payoffs in that cell.

The entry game

The Challenger can Enter or Stay Out. If it enters, the Incumbent can Accommodate or Fight. Payoffs from the slide, written (Challenger, Incumbent): Stay out = (1, 2). Enter then Accommodate = (2, 1). Enter then Fight = (0, 0).

Challenger \ IncumbentAccommodateFight
Enter2, 10, 0
Stay out1, 21, 2
Nash equilibria of the table (check each cell for a profitable one-player switch):
(Enter, Accommodate): Challenger switching to Stay out gets 1 < 2; Incumbent switching to Fight gets 0 < 1. ✔ NE.
(Stay out, Fight): Challenger switching to Enter gets 0 < 1. Incumbent switching to Accommodate still gets 2 (the Incumbent's node is never reached, so nothing changes). ✔ NE.
(Enter, Fight): Incumbent switches to Accommodate (1 > 0). ✘. (Stay out, Accommodate): Challenger switches to Enter (2 > 1). ✘.
The problem. In (Stay out, Fight) the Incumbent "threatens" to fight. If the Challenger actually entered, the Incumbent would compare Fight (0) with Accommodate (1) and accommodate. The threat is non-credible. The table cannot see this because it forgets time order; the equilibrium only survives because the Incumbent's node is never reached.
Rollback fixes it. Incumbent's node: Accommodate (1) beats Fight (0), so cut Fight and roll up (2, 1). Challenger: Enter gives 2, Stay out gives 1. Enter. Result: (Enter, Accommodate) only.

5. Three classic tree games

5.1 "I cut, you choose" (fair division)

Player 1 cuts a loaf into pieces of size x and 1 − x. Player 2 looks and picks a piece. Player 1 gets the other.

Stage 2 (chooser): takes x if x > 1 − x, i.e. x > ½; takes 1 − x if x < ½; indifferent at x = ½. So the chooser always gets max(x, 1 − x) and the cutter gets min(x, 1 − x).
Stage 1 (cutter): choose x to maximise min(x, 1 − x). For x < ½ this equals x, which rises as x rises. For x > ½ it equals 1 − x, which falls as x rises. So the top is at the meeting point x = 1 − x, i.e. x* = ½, giving each ½.

Mechanism design. The parent does not need to measure a "fair" cut. She only sets the rules so selfish children produce a fair split on their own.

5.2 Agenda setting and voting

Three voters rank options A, B, C in a cycle: Voter 1: A ≻ B ≻ C. Voter 2: B ≻ C ≻ A. Voter 3: C ≻ A ≻ B.

Head-to-head winners (majority): A vs B: voters 1 and 3 prefer A, so A wins. B vs C: voters 1 and 2 prefer B, so B wins. C vs A: voters 2 and 3 prefer C, so C wins. A beats B, B beats C, C beats A: a cycle (the Condorcet paradox). There is no overall favourite, so the order of votes matters.

Vote on two options first, then the winner against the third. If voters simply vote for their favourite in each pair:

OrderRound 1 winnerRound 2Final
B vs C, then ABA beats BA
A vs C, then BCB beats CB
A vs B, then CAC beats AC

So whoever sets the agenda can make any option win. That is a lot of power.

Careful (my addition). This table assumes voters vote for their favourite in each round without thinking ahead. If voters use backward induction, the outcome can change. Take "A vs B, then C": if round 1 picks A, round 2 gives C (C beats A). If it picks B, round 2 gives B (B beats C). So round 1 is really a vote between final outcomes C and B. Voters 1 and 2 prefer B to C, so B wins 2-1. With far-sighted voters the result is B, not C.

5.3 The ultimatum game

The Proposer offers the Responder x from {0, 25, 50, 75, 100} out of 100. The Responder accepts (they get x and 100 − x) or rejects (both get 0).

Stage 2: for any x > 0, accepting gives x > 0 = rejecting, so accept. At x = 0 the Responder is indifferent.
Stage 1: if the Responder accepts a zero offer, the Proposer offers 0 and keeps 100. If the Responder rejects 0 (but accepts everything positive), the Proposer offers the smallest positive amount on the menu, 25, and keeps 75. Either way the prediction is: offer almost nothing, and it is accepted.
Reality check. In experiments Responders often reject offers below about 30%, to punish unfairness, and Proposers anticipate this by offering 40-50%. The theory assumes people care only about their own money.

6. Information sets

Information set: a group of one player's decision nodes that she cannot tell apart when she moves. Drawn with a dashed line or a cloud.
Singleton: one node, so she knows exactly where she is (perfect information). Non-singleton: two or more nodes (imperfect information, or a simultaneous move).

Two rules for a valid information set: (1) all its nodes belong to the same player; (2) the same actions are available at every node in it. Otherwise she could tell the nodes apart by what she is allowed to do.

Example: bully and student. The Bully either arms himself with knuckles (K) or not (NK). The Student must choose Submit or Fight back without seeing which. The Student's two nodes (after K and after NK) sit in one information set, because he cannot observe the Bully's preparation.

Order words. Node x is a predecessor of y if x lies on the unique path from the root to y; then y is a successor of x. The subtree at x is x plus all its successors.

Slide example: reading a bigger tree

Here Player 1 moves at A, Player 2 at B and C (joined by the dashed line), and Player 3 at D and E. Letters X, Y, Z, W, U, V, T are terminal nodes.

Information sets. {A} for Player 1 (singleton). {B, C} for Player 2 (non-singleton). {D} and {E} for Player 3 (singletons: Player 3 sees which one he is at).
Is {B, C} valid? Both belong to Player 2 ✔. Both offer the same actions {S, T} ✔. Yes.
Meaning: Player 2 knows Player 1 played P or Q, but not which. He must pick S or T once, for both nodes.
Strategy counts. Player 1: one node, 3 actions (R, P, Q), so 3 strategies. Player 2: one information set with 2 actions, so 2 strategies (S or T), not 4, because he cannot act differently at B and C. Player 3: two information sets with 2 actions each, so 2 × 2 = 4 strategies, e.g. (G, J) or (H, K).
Predecessors and successors. A is a predecessor of every other node. D's predecessors are B and A; its immediate predecessor is B. D's successors are W and U. E's predecessors are C and A; its successors are V and T. B's successors are D, Y, W, U.
Subgames in this tree. A: singleton, contains everything, and the dashed set {B, C} is wholly inside ✔. B and C: not singletons ✘. D: singleton, subtree {D, W, U} ✔. E: singleton, subtree {E, V, T} ✔. So there are 3 subgames: the whole game, the game at D, and the game at E.
How to solve it (no payoffs on the slide): solve D and E first (each is a one-player choice), replace them by their payoffs, then solve the remaining game between Players 1 and 2, where Player 2's move is simultaneous with respect to P and Q.

7. Subgames

A subgame starts at node x if:
1. x is alone in its information set (a singleton);
2. it contains x and all successors of x;
3. it does not cut an information set: if one node of an information set is inside, all of them are.

Plain meaning: a subgame is a smaller game that you could play on its own, where everyone knows the history up to x. The whole game is always a subgame.

Worked example (a slide game)

Player 1 picks U or D. D ends the game with (2, 2). After U, Player 1 picks A or B. Then Player 2 picks X or Y without seeing A or B (dashed line). Payoffs: (A,X) = (1,3), (A,Y) = (0,0), (B,X) = (0,0), (B,Y) = (3,1).

Which nodes can start a subgame? The root (always). Player 1's second node (after U): it is a singleton, and its subtree contains both of Player 2's nodes together, so no information set is cut. ✔. Player 2's nodes: each is in a non-singleton information set, so ✘. So there are exactly 2 subgames: the whole game and the game after U.
Invalid example: a "subgame" starting at only one of Player 2's two nodes would cut the dashed information set (condition 3), and also does not start at a singleton.

Counting tip. Count the singleton decision nodes whose subtree keeps every information set whole. Each is a subgame root.

8. Subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE)

Selten's definition. A strategy profile is a subgame perfect equilibrium if it gives a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including the whole game and subgames that are never reached. ui( s*|G′ ) ≥ ui( si, s*−i|G′ ) for every player i, every subgame G′, every strategy si in G′

Immediate consequences. Every SPE is a Nash equilibrium (the whole game is one of the subgames). But a NE need not be an SPE, and the extra requirement is what removes non-credible threats.

Entry game again. NE: (Enter, Accommodate) and (Stay out, Fight). The Incumbent's node is a proper subgame (a one-player game). In it, "Fight" (0) is not best, so (Stay out, Fight) fails there. The only SPE is (Enter, Accommodate).

Why we need SPE when we have backward induction

Backward inductionSPE
Needs every information set to be a single nodeAlso works with simultaneous or hidden moves
Gets stuck at a non-singleton information set (what is the best action when you do not know the node?)Solves the Nash equilibrium of that small simultaneous game and rolls the result up

How to find SPE

Full solution of the slide game

Step 1: the subgame after U. It is a simultaneous game between Player 1 (A or B) and Player 2 (X or Y):
1 \ 2XY
A1, 30, 0
B0, 03, 1
This is a Battle-of-the-Sexes game. Pure NE: (A, X) with (1, 3), and (B, Y) with (3, 1). Mixed NE: let Player 1 play A with probability p. Player 2's payoffs: X gives 3p, Y gives 1(1 − p). Equal when 3p = 1 − p, so p = ¼. Let Player 2 play X with probability q. Player 1's payoffs: A gives q, B gives 3(1 − q). Equal when q = 3 − 3q, so q = ¾. Payoffs at the mixed NE: Player 1 gets q = ¾, Player 2 gets 3p = ¾.
Step 2: roll back to the root. Player 1 compares D (sure 2) with U, which now equals the subgame's value for her:

A NE that is not an SPE. Convert to a table. Player 1 has four strategies (UA, UB, DA, DB), Player 2 has two (X, Y):
1 \ 2XY

Pure NE: (UB, Y), (DA, X), (DB, X). The last one, (DB, X), is a NE because Player 1 never has to play B. But "B after U" is a non-credible plan: if Player 1 got to that node and Player 2 played X, B pays 0 while A would pay 1. In the subgame, (B, X) is not a NE. So (DB, X) is not an SPE. Subgame perfection removes it.
Take-away: SPE does not always give one answer. Here there are three, because the simultaneous subgame has several equilibria.

Practice (tap to reveal)

  1. A player has 2 decision points with 3 and 4 actions. How many strategies?
    Answer3 × 4 = 12.
  2. In the entry game, why is (Stay out, Fight) a Nash equilibrium but not subgame perfect?
    AnswerNo one gains by deviating from it in the table, because the Incumbent's node is never reached. But in the subgame at the Incumbent's node, Fight (0) is worse than Accommodate (1), so Fight is not a Nash equilibrium there.
  3. Does the cutter in "I cut, you choose" gain by cutting 0.4/0.6? What does she get?
    AnswerThe chooser takes 0.6, so the cutter gets 0.4. Cutting ½/½ gives her 0.5, so no.
  4. In the slide game, why can't Player 2's nodes start a subgame?
    AnswerEach is in a non-singleton information set, and a subgame must start at a singleton node.
